How to Prepare for NISM Series XV in 30 Days

If you are figuring out how to prepare for NISM Series XV, start by accepting that it is a substantive exam. The Research Analyst certification covers the securities market, economic and industry analysis, company analysis, financial statements, valuation, risk and return, report writing and the SEBI research analyst regulations. It has a higher pass mark than many NISM papers and plenty of applied questions on ratios and valuation. A 30-day plan suits most working professionals. This guide sets out a week-by-week schedule, approximate chapter weightage, a revision approach and a mock-test routine for the three-hour exam.

Last updated 11 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Exam pattern and what it implies

The indicative pattern is 100 questions for 100 marks in 180 minutes, with a pass mark of 60 percent and negative marking of 0.25 marks per wrong answer. These are indicative — confirm in the latest official notification on nism.ac.in before registering.

The three-hour window reflects the number of applied questions: computing a ratio from given financial data, choosing an appropriate valuation method, or interpreting an economic indicator. You will need roughly 65 or more correct answers with few errors, so the plan gives real time to financial analysis and valuation rather than treating them as reading chapters.

Approximate chapter weightage

NISM does not publish an official chapter-wise split. Based on the syllabus and the depth of workbook chapters, this grouping is approximate.

  • High: Company Analysis – Financial Analysis; Corporate Valuation; Company Analysis – Business & Governance; Industry Analysis.
  • Medium: Economic Analysis; Fundamentals of Research; Fundamentals of Risk and Return; Legal and Regulatory Environment; Terminology in Equity and Debt Markets.
  • Lower but direct: Introduction to Research Analyst Profession; Introduction to Securities Market; Qualities of a Good Research Report.
  • Calculation-heavy areas: profitability, liquidity, leverage and efficiency ratios; DCF and relative valuation; return, standard deviation and beta.

Who should allow more time

Thirty days assumes about two hours on weekdays and three to four hours on weekends. Chartered accountants, CFA candidates and MBA finance graduates often need less time on financial analysis and can finish in three weeks. Engineers, sales professionals or anyone without accounting exposure should allow five to six weeks, because reading a balance sheet and cash flow statement fluently is a prerequisite for half the syllabus.

Whatever your background, use the current workbook from nism.ac.in as your primary source, particularly for the regulatory chapter, which follows the SEBI research analyst regulations as amended.

Week 1: the profession and the market

The first week builds context. These chapters are lighter, so use spare time to refresh accounting basics if needed.

  • Day 1: Introduction to Research Analyst Profession — role of sell-side and buy-side analysts, qualities, ethics.
  • Day 2: Introduction to Securities Market — primary and secondary markets, participants, instruments.
  • Day 3: Terminology in Equity and Debt Markets — corporate actions, market capitalisation, yields and bond terms.
  • Days 4–5: Fundamentals of Research — fundamental vs technical vs quantitative research, top-down and bottom-up approaches, sources of information.
  • Day 6: Economic Analysis — GDP, inflation, interest rates, fiscal and monetary policy, and their link to markets.
  • Day 7: Revision and chapter-wise practice questions for Week 1.

Week 2: industry and company analysis

This week covers how analysts judge a business before the numbers. Questions often describe a situation and ask which framework applies.

  • Days 8–9: Industry Analysis — industry life cycle, Porter's five forces, competitive structure, key industry metrics.
  • Days 10–11: Company Analysis – Business & Governance — business model, competitive advantage, management quality, corporate governance red flags.
  • Days 12–14: Company Analysis – Financial Analysis — reading the three statements, common-size analysis, ratio analysis, DuPont, cash flow quality. Solve at least 30 ratio problems.

Week 3: valuation, risk and reports

Valuation is the hardest technical part of the exam. Work every example in the workbook by hand before moving to practice questions.

  • Days 15–17: Corporate Valuation — DCF (FCFF and FCFE concepts), dividend discount model, relative valuation using P/E, EV/EBITDA, P/B and other multiples covered in the workbook.
  • Day 18: Fundamentals of Risk and Return — return measures, standard deviation, beta, CAPM.
  • Day 19: Qualities of a Good Research Report — structure, disclosures, clarity, avoiding bias.
  • Days 20–21: Legal and Regulatory Environment — SEBI research analyst regulations, conflicts of interest, disclosures, record keeping. Read twice.

Week 4: mocks and revision

Take full-length timed mock tests in a single 180-minute sitting and review every explanation carefully. Reviewing is where you discover whether a wrong valuation answer came from a concept gap or an arithmetic slip.

  • Day 22: First full-length mock and review.
  • Day 23: Rework all ratio and valuation errors.
  • Day 24: Second mock; revise regulations.
  • Day 25: Revise industry and governance frameworks.
  • Day 26: Third mock and error log update.
  • Days 27–28: Weakest chapters from mocks.
  • Day 29: Fourth mock or a mixed set of 50 questions.
  • Day 30: Light revision of formula sheet, regulatory notes and error log.

Revision habits that help

Keep a formula sheet with every ratio grouped by type — profitability, liquidity, leverage, efficiency, valuation — and write a one-line interpretation next to each, such as 'higher interest coverage means lower default risk'. Many questions test interpretation, not just computation.

For the regulatory chapter, write each obligation as a short question and answer card. Revise the cards on at least four separate days; exact provisions are easy to blur between this exam and other advisory certifications.

Practise valuation with real annual reports as well as workbook examples. Pick two listed companies from different industries, extract a few years of revenue, operating profit, debt and cash flow, and calculate the main ratios yourself. Then compare them with each other and ask what explains the differences. This exercise takes an evening, but it turns abstract formulas into something you can reason about, which is exactly what applied questions test.

  • Maintain an error log with the chapter, the concept and a one-line correction.
  • Re-solve every valuation question you got wrong a day later without looking at the solution.
  • Revise the economic indicators and their typical effect on interest-sensitive and export-oriented sectors.

Exam-day approach

Finish a first pass in about 100 minutes, answering direct questions and short calculations, and mark lengthy valuation or ratio sets. Use the next hour for marked questions and keep 20 minutes for review. Guess only after eliminating at least two options, given the 0.25 negative marking.

Keep in mind that the certificate is one part of the path to practising as a research analyst. Registration requirements, qualifications and continuing obligations are set by SEBI and may change, so check sebi.gov.in and nism.ac.in for the current position before you plan your career around the exam date.

FAQs

Is NISM Series XV tough?

It is among the more demanding NISM exams because of its broad syllabus, the 60 percent pass mark in the indicative pattern, negative marking and applied questions on financial analysis and valuation. Candidates with an accounting or finance background usually find it manageable with three to four weeks of steady practice.

Which chapters are most important for NISM XV?

Based on the syllabus, company financial analysis, corporate valuation, business and governance analysis, and industry analysis tend to carry the most weight. Economic analysis, risk and return, and the regulatory chapter follow. NISM does not publish an official split, so cover every chapter.

Do I need an accounting background for NISM XV?

It helps a great deal, but it is not essential. If you are new to accounting, spend a few extra days learning to read a balance sheet, profit and loss statement and cash flow statement before starting the financial analysis chapter. That foundation makes ratios and valuation far easier.
