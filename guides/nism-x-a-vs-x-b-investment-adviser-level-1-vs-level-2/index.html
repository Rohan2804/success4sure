NISM X-A vs X-B: Investment Adviser Level 1 vs Level 2

If you are planning to register as an investment adviser, the NISM X-A vs X-B question is not really about choosing one — under the current framework you generally need both. What candidates actually want to know is how the two levels differ, which is harder, which to take first and how much of the preparation overlaps. This guide compares Investment Adviser Level 1 and Level 2 chapter by chapter, explains the indicative exam patterns and suggests a sensible order and timeline for clearing both.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Do you need both X-A and X-B?

For individuals seeking SEBI registration as an investment adviser, and for principal officers and persons associated with investment advice in a registered entity, the regulations have required certification in both levels. Requirements can change, so verify the current position on sebi.gov.in and the exam details on nism.ac.in before you plan.

Some candidates take only X-A first, for example to build credibility in a wealth management role, and add X-B later. That is perfectly reasonable, but if registration is your goal, plan for both from the start.

Pattern comparison

Both exams follow the same indicative pattern: 100 questions for 100 marks in 180 minutes, with a pass mark of 60 percent and negative marking of 0.25 for each wrong answer. Treat this as indicative — confirm in the latest official notification on nism.ac.in.

The identical structure means your exam-day technique transfers directly from one to the other. What changes is the content and the style of questions: X-A leans towards concepts and products, while X-B leans towards applying planning concepts to client situations.

What X-A (Level 1) covers

X-A builds the foundation an adviser needs. It is about understanding markets, products and portfolios, plus the rules that govern advice.

  • Investment advisory and financial planning basics, and the client's financial position and debt management.
  • Time value of money — compounding, discounting, annuities and loan calculations.
  • Indian financial markets and their segments; equity, debt, derivatives and mutual funds.
  • Real estate, gold and other assets; PMS, AIF and other managed portfolios.
  • Asset allocation, portfolio construction and performance evaluation.
  • Behavioural finance, operational aspects such as KYC and demat, the regulatory framework for investment advisers, and ethics and grievance redressal.

What X-B (Level 2) covers

X-B takes the adviser into comprehensive financial planning. It assumes you already understand the products and asks you to apply them to protection, retirement, tax and estate needs.

  • Risk management and insurance concepts; life, health and general insurance products; insurance needs analysis.
  • Retirement planning concepts, retirement products and pension schemes, and retirement corpus and distribution planning.
  • Income tax fundamentals and taxation of investment products.
  • Wealth management and estate planning concepts; wills, succession and trusts.
  • Comprehensive financial planning and case studies that pull these areas together.

Which is tougher?

Candidates are split, and it depends on background. Those from a markets or equity background usually find X-A comfortable and X-B harder, because insurance, retirement, tax and succession law are new territory. Those from insurance or tax backgrounds often find the reverse.

In general, X-B feels more demanding to many candidates because of two features: the taxation chapters require precise knowledge of rules that are revised through Finance Acts, and the case-study chapter asks you to combine several calculations — insurance need, retirement corpus, tax impact — in a single scenario. X-A has more breadth; X-B has more depth and application.

A useful self-test before deciding how to split your time: try ten mixed questions from each level's chapter-wise practice sets before starting serious preparation. The level where you score lower is the one that needs the longer runway, regardless of what other candidates say about difficulty.

Which should you take first?

Take X-A first. Its time value of money, asset class and portfolio chapters are the building blocks for X-B's retirement corpus calculations and case studies. Candidates who attempt X-B first often find themselves learning X-A concepts in a hurry anyway.

A practical sequence is to prepare for X-A for about four weeks, sit the exam, and begin X-B preparation within a week or two while TVM and product knowledge are still fresh. Allow four to five weeks for X-B, with more time on taxation and case studies.

How much of the preparation overlaps?

The overlap is mainly in skills rather than chapters. TVM, return calculations and product understanding from X-A are used constantly in X-B. Regulatory and ethics knowledge from X-A also helps you interpret adviser-client situations in X-B case studies.

Plan around 30 to 40 percent less effort on the shared foundations for X-B if you have just cleared X-A, and redirect that time to tax rules, insurance needs analysis, retirement distribution and estate planning tools such as wills, nomination and trusts.

  • Reuse your X-A formula sheet and extend it with retirement corpus and insurance need methods.
  • Start a fresh notes file for taxation, organised by product, because questions are often product-specific.
  • Practise case studies end to end rather than as isolated calculations.

A sample combined timeline

For a working professional studying around two to three hours a day, the following ten-week outline covers both certifications without burning out. Adjust it to your background: compress the X-A weeks if you already work in markets, or stretch the X-B weeks if tax and insurance are new to you.

  • Weeks 1–3: X-A chapters, with chapter-wise practice questions after each.
  • Week 4: X-A full-length mocks, revision and the exam.
  • Week 5: Break of a few days, then X-B insurance chapters.
  • Week 6: X-B retirement chapters, reusing your X-A TVM sheet.
  • Week 7: X-B taxation and estate planning chapters.
  • Week 8: X-B case studies.
  • Weeks 9–10: X-B full-length mocks, revision and the exam.

Mock tests for both levels

For both exams, take several full-length timed mock tests in the final week and spend time reviewing explanations, especially for questions that took longer than two minutes. In X-A, the reviews usually reveal gaps in TVM or regulation; in X-B, they tend to reveal gaps in tax rules or in the sequence of steps in a case. Track accuracy on attempted questions, and skip rather than guess when you cannot eliminate at least two options.

Finally, keep the two sets of notes separate but cross-referenced. When an X-B case study uses a concept from X-A — say, real rate of return or the price-yield relationship of bonds — add a short pointer to the relevant X-A page. This turns your notes into a single reference for advisory work after the exams, which is ultimately the purpose of both certifications.

FAQs

Can I take NISM X-B before X-A?

Check the current rules on nism.ac.in, as eligibility conditions can change. Even where it is permitted, X-A first is the more logical order, because X-B's retirement and case-study questions build directly on X-A's time value of money, asset class and portfolio concepts.

Is X-B harder than X-A?

Many candidates find X-B harder because of taxation, insurance needs analysis, retirement corpus calculations and integrated case studies. X-A covers a broader range of topics but at a more conceptual level. Your own background in markets, insurance or tax will influence which feels tougher.

How long does it take to clear both X-A and X-B?

A working professional studying two to three hours a day typically needs about four weeks for X-A and four to five weeks for X-B, so roughly two to two and a half months in total. Keeping the gap between the two exams short helps retain the shared foundations.
