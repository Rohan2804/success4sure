Toughest Topics in SSC CGL Tier 1 and How to Score

Every aspirant has a list of SSC CGL toughest topics — the chapters that cost marks in every mock no matter how many times they are revised. For most candidates, that list includes geometry and trigonometry in Quant, a few non-verbal and syllogism types in Reasoning, idioms and one-word substitutions in English, and the vast spread of static General Awareness. This guide explains why these topics are hard, which parts of them carry the most marks approximately, and specific methods to make them reliable. The aim is not to master everything but to convert your weakest areas into steady, predictable marks.

Last updated 16 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

A quick note on the pattern

Our mock tests follow an indicative Tier 1 pattern of 100 questions, 200 marks and 60 minutes, with 0.25 of a question's marks deducted for each wrong answer, across Reasoning, General Awareness, Quantitative Aptitude and English. Indicative — confirm in the latest official notification on ssc.gov.in.

Because negative marking applies, a tough topic is costly in two ways: you may skip easy marks out of fear, or you may attempt and get it wrong. The methods below aim to fix both.

Quant: geometry and trigonometry

Geometry is tough because questions mix several theorems in one figure, and trigonometry because identities and standard values must be recalled instantly. Together with algebra and mensuration, these advanced topics carry a meaningful share of the Quant section, approximately.

The fix is to learn a compact set of results thoroughly rather than many results loosely. Most geometry questions use a small group of properties of triangles, circles and quadrilaterals. In trigonometry, standard angle values, basic identities and the habit of substituting convenient angles solve a large share of questions.

  • Geometry: centroid, incentre, circumcentre and orthocentre properties; similar triangles; circle chord, tangent and angle theorems
  • Trigonometry: values at 0°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 90°; identities; maximum and minimum values; heights and distances
  • Algebra: identities for squares and cubes, x + 1/x type questions — use value substitution
  • Practice: 15 questions per day from these areas for four weeks

Quant: time-consuming arithmetic

Arithmetic is not conceptually hard, but questions on time and work, pipes and cisterns, speed and distance and compound interest can take two or three minutes if solved the long way. In a 60-minute paper, that is expensive.

Learn efficient methods: the LCM method for work problems, relative speed for trains and boats, and the successive percentage approach for compound interest over two or three years. Then practise these with a timer until each takes under a minute.

Another useful habit is to read the options before calculating. In many arithmetic questions, the options differ enough that a rough estimate identifies the answer, saving the full calculation for questions that really need it.

Reasoning: non-verbal and syllogism

Many candidates who are comfortable with verbal reasoning lose marks in non-verbal questions — mirror and water images, paper folding and cutting, embedded figures, counting figures and cube problems. Others struggle with syllogism when 'some', 'all' and 'no' combine with possibility cases.

Non-verbal questions improve with sheer volume: solve 20 to 30 a day for two weeks, checking each against the explanation. For syllogism, draw Venn diagrams every time, including the minimum-overlap case, until the patterns become automatic.

  • Mirror and water images: track one asymmetric feature
  • Paper folding: unfold step by step, mirroring each cut
  • Counting figures: use a systematic labelling method
  • Syllogism: always test the possibility diagram

English: idioms, one-word substitution and vocabulary

Vocabulary-based questions — synonyms, antonyms, idioms and phrases, one-word substitution and spellings — feel tough because there is no method to fall back on. Either you know the word or you guess. Yet together they carry a large share of the English section, approximately.

Build vocabulary in small, repeated doses. Learn 15 to 20 words or idioms a day with a sentence each, and revise them on a spaced schedule. Grammar questions, by contrast, respond to rules; make a list of the 25 to 30 most tested rules and revise them weekly.

For grammar, error-spotting questions tend to test a predictable set of rules: subject-verb agreement, tenses, prepositions, articles, pronouns and degrees of comparison. Practise 20 such questions a day and write the rule next to every mistake you make.

General Awareness: the widest syllabus

General Awareness is the toughest for many because it has no fixed boundary: history, polity, geography, economics, physics, chemistry, biology, static GK and current affairs. It is also the section where a strong candidate can save the most time, since each question takes only seconds.

Prioritise by approximate frequency: polity, history and general science usually contribute most, with geography and economics close behind. Study in short daily sessions, keep one-page notes per subtopic, and test recall with practice questions.

  • Polity: fundamental rights, DPSP, Parliament, important articles
  • History: modern Indian history and freedom movement first
  • Science: human body, everyday physics and chemistry
  • Current affairs: the last six to eight months before the exam

Turning tough topics into scoring topics

Choose two tough topics at a time, not all of them. Spend two weeks on each pair with daily timed practice, then test them inside full-length timed mock tests, reviewing the explanations for every mistake. Keep a simple chart of accuracy per topic across mocks; when a topic stays above 80 percent accuracy for three mocks, move on.

Also decide what to leave. If, after honest effort, a particular question type still takes too long, mark it as a skip-first item and return to it only if time remains. Skipping wisely is part of scoring well.

How to find your own toughest topics

The topics above are tough for most candidates, but your list may differ. After every full mock, tag each wrong or skipped question by subject and topic. After five or six mocks, the topics with the most tags are your real weak areas, and they are where your preparation time will pay off most.

Separate topics you do not understand from topics you understand but solve slowly. The first need concept study from a textbook or class notes; the second need timed drills. Mixing the two leads to wasted hours, such as re-reading theory when the real problem is speed.

Keep official details in view

Topic emphasis shifts from year to year, and SSC may revise the pattern. Check ssc.gov.in for the latest notification and syllabus, and use it as the final reference for what to study.

FAQs

Which is the toughest section in SSC CGL Tier 1?

It varies by background, but many candidates find Quantitative Aptitude the toughest because of geometry, trigonometry and time-consuming arithmetic, while others struggle most with the breadth of General Awareness. Use your mock-test data to identify your own weakest section rather than relying on general opinion.

How can I improve in geometry for SSC CGL?

Learn a focused set of theorems on triangles, circles and quadrilaterals thoroughly, draw every figure, and solve 15 timed questions a day for about four weeks. Review each mistake against the explanation and note which property you missed; that list becomes your revision sheet.

Should I skip tough questions in SSC CGL Tier 1?

Skip them in the first pass, not permanently. With negative marking, attempt a tough question only if you can solve it or eliminate at least two options. Return to skipped items after finishing the easier ones, and use mocks to learn which question types you should leave.
