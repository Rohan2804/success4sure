NISM Series VII Syllabus Explained Chapter by Chapter

The NISM Series VII syllabus covers what happens after an investor clicks 'buy': how a broker processes the order, how the trade is cleared, how money and securities move, how risk is controlled and how disputes are resolved. Securities Operations and Risk Management is designed for back-office, settlement and risk staff, so it tests processes and responsibilities more than theory. This guide explains each chapter, the kind of questions it produces and what to focus on, so you can read the official workbook from nism.ac.in with a clear map in mind.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

The big picture

The syllabus moves in a logical order: first the market and its participants, then broking operations, then the risk, clearing and settlement chain, and finally grievance redressal and additional services. The indicative pattern is 100 questions for 100 marks in 120 minutes, with a 50 percent pass mark and 0.25 negative marking — indicative, so confirm in the latest official notification on nism.ac.in.

Because operational rules are revised by SEBI and the exchanges, NISM updates the workbook periodically. Always check the edition and effective date before you begin, and study timelines and limits only from that edition.

As you read, notice that chapters 4, 5 and 6 describe a single continuous chain: risk is managed before and during trading, obligations are determined in clearing, and they are discharged in settlement. Studying them together, rather than as three separate topics, makes the whole syllabus much easier to retain.

Chapters 1 and 2: the securities market and its participants

This chapter sets the scene: the role of securities markets in an economy, primary and secondary markets, the products traded — equity, debt, derivatives, mutual fund units — and the regulators and market infrastructure institutions. Questions are mostly definitional and quick to answer, making it a good chapter to secure early marks.

Focus on the distinction between primary and secondary markets, the functions of exchanges, clearing corporations and depositories, and the broad responsibilities of SEBI.

Here you meet everyone in the chain: investors, stock brokers, clearing members, custodians, depositories and depository participants, clearing banks, registrars and merchant bankers. Expect questions on who does what and who is regulated by whom.

A simple table with each participant, its role and its regulator is the most effective way to learn this chapter. Many wrong answers come from confusing clearing members with trading members, or custodians with depository participants.

Chapter 3: Introduction to Securities Broking Operations

This chapter covers the front-to-back workflow inside a broking firm: client registration and KYC, account opening documents, risk disclosure, order entry and management, trade confirmation and contract notes, and the back-office functions that support them. Questions are practical — for example, which document must be issued after a trade, or what a broker must verify before activating a client.

Learn the sequence of steps from client onboarding to trade confirmation, and the documents and records involved at each step.

Contract notes, client registration documents and the rights and obligations of brokers and clients are common question areas, so read those parts of the chapter slowly and note any specific timelines the workbook mentions.

Chapter 4: Risk Management

Risk management is one of the most important chapters. It explains how exchanges and clearing corporations protect the system from default: types of margins, margin collection and reporting, mark-to-market settlement, position limits, capital adequacy requirements and surveillance measures. Some questions involve simple calculations on margins or mark-to-market.

Focus on what each margin covers, who collects it from whom and when, and how risk containment measures respond when limits are breached. Draw the margin flow from client to broker to clearing member to clearing corporation.

Chapter 5: Clearing Process

This chapter explains how trades become obligations. It covers the role of the clearing corporation, novation (the clearing corporation becoming counterparty to every trade), clearing members and custodians, and how net obligations are determined for funds and securities.

Questions often test the concept of novation, the difference between gross and net obligations, and the responsibilities of clearing banks. A worked example of netting several trades for one member is the best way to master it.

It is worth working through at least one simple numerical by hand: a client's position over three days, the daily mark-to-market amount, and the effect on the margin balance. Once you have done this yourself, multiple-choice questions on the topic feel familiar rather than intimidating.

Chapter 6: Settlement Process

Settlement is where obligations are discharged: pay-in and pay-out of funds and securities, settlement cycles, handling of shortages, auctions and close-out, and corporate action adjustments. This chapter overlaps with risk management and clearing, and together the three form the core of the exam.

Learn the settlement cycle as described in the current workbook and the sequence of events within it. Pay attention to what happens when a seller fails to deliver, because shortage handling and auctions are a favourite question area.

Chapter 7: Investor Grievances and Arbitration

This chapter explains how investors raise complaints and how they are resolved: the broker's internal redressal, exchange mechanisms, SEBI's complaint systems and the online dispute resolution and arbitration framework as described in the workbook. Questions are factual and often test the order of escalation and timelines.

Since grievance frameworks have been updated in recent years, rely only on the current workbook and verify any details on the official SEBI and exchange websites.

Make sure you understand which complaints go first to the broker, when the exchange steps in and how the process moves to conciliation or arbitration, since sequence questions are common.

Chapter 8: Other Services Provided by Brokers

The final chapter covers services beyond plain trading: depository participant services, margin trading facility, securities lending and borrowing, distribution of mutual funds and IPOs, and similar offerings. Questions test the basic features and conditions of each service.

Based on the syllabus, chapters 4, 5 and 6 tend to carry the most weight, with chapters 2, 3 and 7 next. Read every chapter at least twice, practise chapter-wise questions after each one, and finish with full-length timed mock tests and a careful review of their explanations.

A sensible study order is to cover chapters 1 to 3 in the first few days, spend the longest stretch on chapters 4 to 6 with flowcharts and worked examples, and then finish chapters 7 and 8, which are shorter and factual. Keep a single page of timelines and limits from the workbook and revise it every day in the final week.

FAQs

How many chapters are in the NISM Series VII syllabus?

The current syllabus is organised into about eight chapters, from an introduction to the securities market through risk management, clearing and settlement to investor grievances and other broker services. Download the latest workbook from nism.ac.in to confirm the chapter list for your exam date.

Which part of the NISM VII syllabus is most important?

Based on the syllabus, risk management, the clearing process and the settlement process form the core of the exam and tend to carry the most questions. Broking operations, market participants and investor grievances follow. Cover every chapter, as the remaining ones provide quick marks.

Is the NISM Series VII syllabus difficult?

It is not conceptually difficult, but it is detailed. Questions test exact processes, responsibilities and timelines, and negative marking punishes guesswork. Candidates who draw flowcharts for margins, clearing and settlement and practise chapter-wise questions usually find the syllabus manageable in two to three weeks.
