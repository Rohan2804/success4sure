NISM V-A vs V-B: Which Mutual Fund Exam Do You Need?

The NISM V-A vs V-B question comes up the moment someone decides to sell mutual funds. Both are mutual fund certifications from NISM, both appear on the same registration pages, and both lead into the distribution business — yet they serve different people. Series V-A (Mutual Fund Distributors) is the full certification for distributors who want to sell the complete range of schemes. Series V-B (Mutual Fund Foundation) is a shorter, foundation-level exam aimed at a new cadre of distributors who sell only a limited set of simpler schemes. This guide compares the two and helps you pick the right one.

Last updated 19 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

The short answer

If you intend to build a mutual fund distribution practice, work as a relationship manager in a bank or wealth firm, or advise clients across equity, hybrid and debt schemes, you need NISM Series V-A. It is the standard certification behind an AMFI Registration Number (ARN) for regular distributors.

Series V-B suits people who will distribute only the restricted basket of simple products permitted for the new-cadre category, or who want a gentle, entry-level introduction to mutual funds before attempting V-A. The exact list of eligible persons and permitted schemes is set by SEBI and AMFI, so check the current rules on amfiindia.com and the exam details on nism.ac.in before deciding.

Pattern comparison at a glance

The indicative patterns below come from the current exam structure as we understand it. They are indicative — confirm in the latest official notification on nism.ac.in, since NISM updates patterns and workbooks.

  • V-A: 100 questions, 100 marks, 120 minutes, pass mark 50 percent, negative marking of 0.25 per wrong answer.
  • V-B: 50 questions, 50 marks, 120 minutes, pass mark 50 percent, no negative marking.
  • V-A is roughly twice the length and penalises wrong answers, so guessing strategy matters more.
  • V-B's shorter paper — half the questions in the same two hours — and absence of negative marking make it noticeably less stressful for first-time candidates.

How the syllabus differs

V-B covers the basics: what a mutual fund is, simple product types, how investors transact, essential investor services and the regulatory environment at an introductory level. It is designed so that someone with no finance background can follow it.

V-A goes much deeper. Its workbook spans the investment landscape, the legal structure of mutual funds (sponsor, trustee, AMC, custodian, RTA), the SEBI and AMFI regulatory framework, scheme documents such as the SID, SAI and KIM, distribution and channel management, NAV, TER and pricing of units, taxation of mutual fund investments, investor services, risk and return measures, scheme performance evaluation and scheme selection for different investor profiles.

The biggest jumps in difficulty are in three places: numerical questions on NAV, loads and returns; taxation of equity-oriented and other schemes; and scheme selection questions that describe an investor and ask which category fits. None of these is extremely hard, but they need practice rather than one reading.

Who should take V-A

Choose V-A if any of the following describes you. It is the more widely recognised certification and keeps your options open as your business grows.

  • You want to sell equity, hybrid, debt and solution-oriented schemes to retail and HNI clients.
  • You are joining a bank branch, broking firm or wealth management team in a sales or relationship role involving mutual funds.
  • You plan to move later towards NISM Series X-A and X-B (investment adviser) or XXI-A (PMS distributors), where V-A level knowledge is a useful base.
  • Your employer or the AMCs you want to empanel with ask specifically for the V-A certificate.

Who should take V-B

V-B makes sense for candidates who fall within the new-cadre distributor category defined by the regulators and will work only with the limited set of simple schemes that category allows. It also works as a confidence-building first step for someone completely new to finance who wants to understand the basics before tackling V-A.

Keep in mind that V-B does not replace V-A for regular distribution. If there is a reasonable chance you will want to sell the full product range within a year or two, it is usually more efficient to prepare directly for V-A.

Can you prepare for both together?

Yes. The V-B syllabus is essentially a subset of V-A, so a candidate who prepares properly for V-A will find V-B straightforward. The reverse is not true: V-B preparation alone leaves big gaps in regulation, taxation, scheme documents and performance measurement.

If you take V-B first, treat it as the foundation and reuse your notes. Then add the V-A-only chapters — especially Legal and Regulatory Framework, NAV, TER and Pricing, Taxation, and Scheme Selection — and practise numericals and situational questions, which V-B covers lightly.

A simple decision checklist

If you are still unsure after reading the comparison, answer the following questions honestly. Two or more 'yes' answers point clearly towards V-A.

Remember that the certificate is only one step. Registration with AMFI, KYD (know your distributor) formalities, empanelment with AMCs and continuing professional education are separate requirements, and their current procedures are published on amfiindia.com and nism.ac.in. Read those pages before you pay for either exam so that you know the full path from certificate to first transaction.

  • Will you be recommending equity or hybrid funds to clients in the next two years?
  • Does your employer's job description or appointment letter mention NISM V-A by name?
  • Are you planning to pursue investment adviser, PMS distribution or other advanced NISM certifications later?
  • Do you expect clients to ask about taxation, scheme selection or risk-adjusted returns?
  • Are you comfortable studying for around two to three weeks with a few timed practice tests?

Preparation time for each

Most candidates need around one week of steady study for V-B and about two to three weeks for V-A, depending on prior exposure to markets. For V-A, plan to sit a few full-length timed mock tests and spend time reviewing the explanations, because the 0.25 negative marking punishes careless guesses. For V-B, chapter-wise practice and one or two timed tests are usually enough.

Whichever you pick, download the current workbook from nism.ac.in, study from that edition, and confirm registration steps, validity and continuing education requirements on the official NISM and AMFI websites.

One practical tip applies to both exams: do not memorise from summaries alone. The questions are framed around the exact wording of the workbook, so a term such as 'expense ratio', 'exit load' or 'riskometer' needs to be understood as the workbook defines it. Reading the chapter, then practising questions and checking explanations, is the most reliable sequence.

FAQs

Is NISM V-B easier than V-A?

Generally yes. V-B has fewer questions in the same two hours, an introductory syllabus and no negative marking in the indicative pattern. V-A covers regulation, taxation, NAV calculations and scheme selection in far more depth and deducts marks for wrong answers, so it needs more practice.

Can I get an ARN with NISM V-B?

V-B is linked to a new-cadre distributor category that is allowed to sell only a limited set of simpler schemes, subject to SEBI and AMFI rules. For the regular ARN covering the full range of schemes, V-A is the standard certification. Confirm current eligibility on amfiindia.com before applying.

Should I take V-B before V-A?

It is optional. If you are entirely new to finance, V-B can be a gentle start, and its syllabus overlaps heavily with V-A. If you already know you want to sell the full product range, preparing directly for V-A saves time and an extra exam.
