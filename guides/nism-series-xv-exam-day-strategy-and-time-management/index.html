NISM Series XV Exam Strategy and Time Management

Plenty of well-prepared candidates fall just short in the Research Analyst exam, not because they lack knowledge but because they run out of time or lose marks to careless guesses. A clear NISM Series XV exam strategy fixes both problems. The paper mixes quick factual questions with longer ratio, valuation and case-style questions, and the indicative pattern carries negative marking. This guide explains how to split the 180 minutes, the order in which to attack question types, how to handle calculations under pressure and how to decide when to guess and when to skip.

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Start from the pattern

The indicative pattern is 100 questions carrying 100 marks in 180 minutes, with a pass mark of 60 percent and negative marking of 0.25 marks for each wrong answer. Confirm these details in the latest official notification on nism.ac.in, as NISM revises exams periodically.

Two conclusions follow. First, the average time per question is 1.8 minutes, but that average hides a wide spread: a terminology question takes 20 seconds while a valuation question can take four minutes. Second, at 0.25 per wrong answer, four wrong guesses cost one full mark. A good strategy maximises correct answers while keeping wrong ones low.

A three-pass plan for 180 minutes

Rather than answering strictly in order, move through the paper in three passes. This prevents a single difficult calculation from eating into time meant for easy marks later.

  • Pass 1 (about 90 minutes): answer every question you can solve confidently in under two minutes — terminology, regulation, frameworks, short ratio questions. Mark everything else.
  • Pass 2 (about 65 minutes): return to marked questions, starting with calculations you know how to do, then those requiring interpretation.
  • Pass 3 (about 25 minutes): review marked-but-unanswered questions and decide whether to attempt or skip; check that no intended answer was left blank.

Handling ratio and financial analysis questions

Financial analysis questions usually give a few figures from the balance sheet or profit and loss statement and ask for a ratio or an interpretation. The trap is often in the definition — for example, whether a ratio uses total debt or long-term debt, or average or closing equity, as defined in the workbook.

Before calculating, write the formula on rough paper. Then substitute the numbers. This adds a few seconds but prevents the most common error, which is using the wrong denominator. If two options are close, recheck the definition rather than the arithmetic.

  • Profitability ratios: watch whether margins use revenue or net sales.
  • Leverage ratios: confirm which debt figure the definition uses.
  • DuPont questions: multiply the components; do not add them.
  • Interpretation questions: a higher or lower ratio is only 'good' in context — read the question carefully.

Handling valuation questions

Valuation questions are the longest in the paper. Typical ones ask for a value using the dividend discount model, the present value of projected cash flows, or a price implied by a peer multiple. Some are conceptual — which model suits a company with no dividends, or why EV-based multiples help compare firms with different capital structures.

Do the conceptual ones in Pass 1; they are quick if you have studied the chapter. Leave multi-step calculations for Pass 2. When you attempt them, estimate the answer roughly first. If your detailed calculation lands far from the estimate, you have probably mixed up the growth rate and the discount rate or missed a year in the discounting.

Negative marking: when to guess

A simple rule works well. If you can eliminate two of the four options, guessing between the remaining two has a positive expected value, and you should usually attempt it. If you can eliminate only one, or none, skip it unless time remains at the very end and you have a genuine reason to prefer one option.

Track this in your practice. In each full-length timed mock test, note how many guesses you made and how many were correct. If your guesses are right less than about 40 percent of the time, tighten your rule; if they are right more than 60 percent, you can afford to be a little bolder.

Regulation and report-writing questions

The legal and regulatory chapter and the chapter on qualities of a good research report produce some of the fastest marks in the paper, but only if you have read them closely. Questions often hinge on a single word — 'shall' versus 'may', a disclosure that is mandatory versus one that is recommended.

Answer these in Pass 1 and do not overthink them. If two options look correct, choose the one closest to the workbook's own phrasing. Second-guessing regulatory questions in Pass 3 tends to replace right answers with wrong ones.

Practising the strategy before exam day

Strategy only works if you have rehearsed it. Take at least three full-length timed mock tests in the last ten days, each in a single 180-minute sitting, and follow the three-pass plan exactly. After each test, review explanations for every wrong and every guessed question, and note how long each pass took.

Adjust the time split based on the results. If Pass 1 regularly runs over 100 minutes, you are spending too long on questions you should mark. If you finish Pass 2 with 40 minutes spare, you can afford to attempt more calculations in Pass 1.

  • Record score, attempts, accuracy and time per pass for each mock.
  • Keep a list of question types that regularly take you more than three minutes.
  • Practise those types separately in chapter-wise sets until they become routine.

Mistakes that cost marks

Reviewing mock results across many candidates shows the same handful of avoidable errors. Each one is cheap to fix once you know to look for it.

  • Spending five or more minutes on a single valuation question in the first pass.
  • Using closing instead of average figures (or the reverse) when the workbook definition specifies one.
  • Confusing the cost of equity with the overall cost of capital in discounting questions.
  • Changing correct regulatory answers in the final review without a clear reason.
  • Leaving questions unanswered that were solved on rough paper but never clicked on screen.

On the day itself

Reach the test centre early with the identification documents specified in your admit details, and read the on-screen instructions carefully before starting the timer. Use rough sheets generously — write formulas, intermediate results and question numbers you have marked. Glance at the clock at roughly 90 and 155 minutes to check you are on schedule. If panic sets in over a hard question, move on; there are easier marks elsewhere in the paper.

FAQs

How should I divide time in the NISM XV exam?

A three-pass approach works well: around 90 minutes for questions you can answer confidently, about 65 minutes for marked calculations and interpretation questions, and roughly 25 minutes for final review. Adjust the split after practising it in full-length timed mock tests.

Should I attempt every question in NISM Series XV?

Not necessarily. The indicative pattern deducts 0.25 marks for each wrong answer. Attempt a question when you can eliminate at least two options. Skip questions where you would be guessing blindly, unless you have spare time and a genuine reason to prefer one option.

What are the most time-consuming questions in NISM XV?

Multi-step valuation questions, such as discounting projected cash flows or applying the dividend discount model, and ratio questions built on several financial statement figures usually take the longest. Leaving them for a second pass protects time for quicker, scoring questions.
