NISM Series VI Toughest Chapters and How to Score

Depository Operations is not a mathematical exam, yet many candidates are surprised by how hard some chapters feel. The NISM Series VI toughest chapters share one feature: they describe procedures with several similar steps, forms and exceptions, and the questions test those details precisely. With an indicative pass mark of 60 percent and negative marking, weak spots in these chapters can decide the result. This guide identifies the chapters candidates struggle with most, explains why, and gives practical techniques to turn each into a scoring area.

Last updated 16 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Why procedural chapters feel hard

The indicative pattern is 100 questions for 100 marks in 120 minutes, with a 60 percent pass mark and 0.25 negative marking — indicative, so confirm in the latest official notification on nism.ac.in. Time is rarely the problem; precision is.

Procedural chapters are hard because the options in a question are usually all plausible steps from real processes. Only one matches the exact situation described — the right form, the right sequence, the right entity. Reading the chapter once creates a general picture but not the detail needed to pick between near-identical options.

There is also a vocabulary challenge. Terms such as beneficial owner, delivery instruction, freeze, transmission and invocation have precise meanings in the depository context, and questions often use them without explanation. Keeping a short glossary while you study saves time later.

1. DP Functions – Transmission and Nomination

This is the chapter candidates most often name as the hardest. It covers nomination rules for individual and joint accounts, how nomination is made, changed or cancelled, and transmission of securities after the death of a sole holder, one joint holder or all joint holders. Each situation needs different documents and follows a different route, and questions describe the situation briefly and ask what happens next.

How to score: build a grid with situations as rows — sole holder with nominee, sole holder without nominee, death of one joint holder, death of all joint holders — and columns for who can claim, documents required, and where the securities go. Learn the grid until you can reproduce it. Then practise situation-based questions until you can identify the row within seconds.

2. DP Functions – Account Operations

Account operations covers the day-to-day life of a demat account: delivery instruction slips, market and off-market transfers, inter-depository transfers, freezing and unfreezing, change of address, bank details or signatures, and account closure. The volume of rules is large, and many questions turn on small conditions — for example, who must sign an instruction for a joint account.

How to score: group the operations into three families — transfers, account changes, and freeze or closure — and for each family list the instruction used, the signatories and the checks the DP must perform. Practise chapter-wise questions immediately after studying each family, rather than reading the whole chapter first.

  • Know the difference between market and off-market transfers and when each is used.
  • Note who must sign instructions for joint accounts.
  • Learn the conditions under which an account can be closed and what happens to remaining balances.

3. DP Functions – Dematerialisation

Dematerialisation involves several parties — the client, the DP, the RTA or issuer, and the depository — and both physical documents and electronic requests. Questions test the order of steps, who verifies what, the reasons a request may be rejected and how rematerialisation differs.

How to score: draw the demat flow as a diagram with arrows between client, DP, RTA and depository, labelling what moves along each arrow — certificates, the request form or electronic confirmation. Draw it from memory three times on separate days. Then do the same for rematerialisation and compare the two diagrams.

4. Special Services – Pledge & Hypothecation

Pledge questions are tricky because pledge and hypothecation look similar and the process involves both the pledgor's and pledgee's DPs. Questions ask about creation, closure and invocation of a pledge, and the difference in rights between pledge and hypothecation as described in the workbook.

How to score: write the pledge life cycle in four lines — initiation by pledgor, acceptance by pledgee, closure on repayment, invocation on default — with the party acting at each stage. Then add one line on how hypothecation differs. This compact summary handles most questions on the topic.

5. Special Services – Public Offering / Corporate Actions

This chapter covers credit of securities after an IPO and the handling of corporate actions such as bonus issues, rights issues, splits, mergers and dividends in a depository environment. Questions test who initiates a corporate action, how record dates work and how the depository credits or debits accounts.

How to score: make a table with each corporate action, what changes in the client's holding, and the role of the issuer, RTA and depository. Most questions become straightforward once you see that the issuer or RTA initiates and the depository executes.

Easier chapters you should still secure

Spending most of your time on the tough chapters is sensible, but do not neglect the shorter ones. They are largely definitional and offer good marks per hour of study.

  • Introduction to the Indian Capital Market and Introduction to Depository — concepts, benefits and the legal framework.
  • Depository and its Business Partners — roles of DPs, issuers, RTAs and clearing corporations.
  • DP Functions – Account Opening — account types, KYC and documents.
  • Special Services – Debt Instruments & Government Securities, Foreign Portfolio Investors (FPI) and Investor Services — features and rules as described in the workbook.

Putting it together in practice

Allocate roughly 60 percent of your preparation time to the five chapters above and 40 percent to the rest. After every chapter, attempt chapter-wise practice questions; in the final week, take full-length timed mock tests and spend as long reviewing the explanations as you spent on the test. Sort mistakes by chapter, and if transmission or account operations still dominate the list, return to your grids and diagrams rather than re-reading.

On exam day, read each procedural question twice and identify the exact situation — sole or joint, living or deceased, pledgor or pledgee — before looking at the options. With negative marking, attempt a doubtful question only after eliminating at least two options.

Finally, remember that the workbook is the reference for every answer. Depository procedures are updated through SEBI circulars and depository operating instructions, so study from the current edition on nism.ac.in and treat older notes, videos or forum posts with caution, particularly for timelines and document lists.

FAQs

Which is the hardest chapter in NISM Series VI?

Many candidates find Transmission and Nomination the hardest, followed by Account Operations and Dematerialisation. These chapters involve several similar procedures, forms and exceptions, and questions test exact details. Building comparison grids and flow diagrams is the most effective way to master them.

How should I study pledge and hypothecation for NISM VI?

Write the pledge life cycle as four stages — initiation, acceptance, closure and invocation — with the party acting at each step. Then note how hypothecation differs in rights and process. Practise situation-based questions until you can identify the stage quickly.

Are there numerical questions in NISM Series VI?

Very few, if any. The exam is almost entirely procedural and conceptual, testing the steps, forms, responsibilities and rules of depository operations. Precision in reading the question and knowing exact procedures matters far more than calculation skills.
