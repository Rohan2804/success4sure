SBI PO Prelims Exam Strategy and Time Management

A sound SBI PO prelims exam strategy can be worth as many marks as an extra month of study. The paper is short, the sections are separately timed in recent cycles, and difficulty can swing sharply between shifts. Candidates who know exactly how they will split their minutes, which questions they will attempt first and when they will abandon a set tend to outperform those who simply start at question one. This guide explains a practical section-wise time plan, an attempt order for each section, how to handle negative marking, and what to do in the first and last five minutes.

Last updated 11 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Know the pattern you are planning for

Our mock tests use an indicative pattern of 100 questions, 100 marks and 60 minutes with 0.25 negative marking, across English, Quantitative Aptitude and Reasoning, each with its own time limit as used in recent cycles. This is indicative — confirm in the latest official notification on sbi.co.in/careers, because the number of questions and sectional timings can change.

Sectional timing means you cannot save time in English to spend on Reasoning. Each section is a separate sprint, and your strategy must work within each window.

A minute-by-minute plan for each section

Within each sectional window, use a simple three-pass approach. Pass one picks off quick, sure questions. Pass two tackles the one or two sets you judge most solvable. Pass three uses the remaining time on marked questions where you can eliminate options. Practise this structure in every mock until it becomes automatic.

  • English (about 20 minutes, indicative): 4–5 min grammar and fillers, 8–9 min RC, 5–6 min cloze and para jumbles, 1 min review
  • Quant (about 20 minutes, indicative): 3–4 min quadratic equations and series, 8–10 min one or two DI sets, 5–6 min arithmetic, 1 min review
  • Reasoning (about 20 minutes, indicative): 3–4 min inequalities, syllogism and coding, 12–14 min two or three puzzles, 2 min review

Attempt order inside each section

Start every section with the question types you solve fastest and most accurately. This builds momentum and banks marks before you face the harder sets. In Quant, that usually means quadratic equations or approximation; in Reasoning, inequalities and syllogism; in English, fillers or error spotting.

Then move to the sets. Read a puzzle or DI set once and decide within about 45 seconds whether to commit. Signs of a good set: clear conditions, few variables and questions that do not need full arrangements. Signs to skip: many uncertain conditions or calculations with awkward numbers.

  • Quant: equations and series → simpler DI set → arithmetic → second DI set if time permits
  • Reasoning: inequalities, syllogism, coding → easiest puzzle → next puzzle
  • English: fillers, error spotting → RC → cloze and para jumbles

Handling negative marking

With 0.25 negative marking in the indicative pattern, a blind guess among five options roughly breaks even at best and only adds risk, but a guess after eliminating two options usually works in your favour. Make it a rule: attempt only when you have narrowed it down to two choices or are confident.

Track your accuracy in mocks. If it stays above the mid-80s percentage range, you can afford slightly more attempts. If it falls below that, cut attempts and focus on solving fewer questions correctly.

When a section feels tough

SBI papers sometimes include a section that is much harder than mocks. Remember that difficulty applies to everyone in that shift, so a lower attempt count may still be competitive. Panicking and guessing randomly is the most common way candidates lose marks on a hard day.

If the first puzzle you read looks impossible, move on immediately and look for an easier one. Often the second or third set in a section is more approachable. Do not carry stress from one section into the next; each sectional timer is a fresh start.

A practical habit is to keep a mental count of how many questions you are confident about in each section. If that count is lower than usual, do not chase attempts with guesses; instead, look again for the easiest remaining items, such as single questions in a puzzle that can be answered from partial information.

Practising strategy in mock tests

Strategy is a skill you practise, not something you invent on the day. Taking full-length timed mock tests in the same sectional format, and then reviewing the explanations along with your time log, shows you where minutes leak. Record time spent per set in at least five mocks.

Experiment early with attempt orders, then fix one by the last two weeks and stop changing it. A familiar routine reduces decision fatigue in the exam hall.

Treat at least one mock each week as a strategy session rather than a score check. Try a different attempt order or a stricter time cap per set, and compare the result with your usual approach. This is how you discover what works for you before it matters.

  • Log time per set and per section after each mock
  • Mark which sets you should have skipped
  • Practise a hard mock at least once a week to train composure
  • Fix your final attempt order two weeks before the exam

The first and last five minutes

The first few minutes of each section set its tone. Do not start with the first question by default; scroll quickly through the section, note where the quick question types are and where the sets are, and begin with the fastest ones. This short scan usually pays for itself within the first few questions.

The last minutes matter too. Do not try to start a fresh puzzle with two minutes left. Instead, return to questions you marked for review where you had already narrowed the options. If sectional timing applies, the system will move you on automatically, so keep an eye on the clock rather than being cut off mid-question.

  • First minute: quick scan of the section
  • Start with your fastest question types
  • Last two minutes: only marked questions you had narrowed down
  • Watch the section timer, not just the overall time

Exam-day checklist

Reach the centre well before the reporting time with the call letter and the ID documents specified in the official instructions. Use the instruction-reading time to settle and plan your first section.

Eat lightly, carry water if allowed, and avoid discussing the paper with others between shifts or while waiting. Confirm all rules on your call letter from sbi.co.in/careers.

FAQs

How should I manage time in SBI PO prelims?

Treat each section as a separate sprint within its time limit. Spend the first few minutes on quick question types, then commit to one or two puzzle or DI sets you judge solvable, and use the final minute or two on marked questions you can narrow to two options.

How many questions should I attempt in SBI PO prelims?

There is no fixed safe number, because it depends on the difficulty of your shift. Focus on accuracy instead: aim for high accuracy on every attempt and use your mock-test data to know how many questions you can solve reliably in each sectional window.

Should I guess in SBI PO prelims?

With 0.25 negative marking in the indicative pattern, blind guesses usually cost marks. A guess becomes reasonable when you can eliminate at least two options. Practise this rule in mocks and track whether your guesses are helping or hurting your score.
