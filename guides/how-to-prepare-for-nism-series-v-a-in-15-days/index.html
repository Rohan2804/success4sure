How to Prepare for NISM Series V-A in 15 Days

If you are wondering how to prepare for NISM Series V-A without taking weeks off work, a focused 15-day plan is usually enough for most working professionals. The Mutual Fund Distributors exam is not mathematically heavy, but it is detail-heavy: scheme categories, regulatory limits, loads, taxation rules and investor service timelines all appear as tricky one-liners. This guide gives you a day-by-day schedule, an approximate sense of which chapters carry more weight, a revision routine and a mock-test schedule, so that you walk into the test centre knowing exactly where you stand.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Understand the exam pattern first

Before you open the workbook, be clear about what you are preparing for. The indicative pattern is 100 questions carrying 100 marks, to be answered in 120 minutes, with a passing score of 50 percent. There is negative marking of 0.25 marks for each wrong answer. Treat these numbers as indicative — confirm in the latest official notification on nism.ac.in before you book your slot, because NISM revises workbooks and patterns from time to time.

Two things follow from this pattern. First, 120 minutes for 100 questions is generous, so speed is rarely the problem; accuracy is. Second, negative marking means guessing blindly on ten questions can cost you more than two and a half marks, which matters when the pass line is 50. Your preparation should therefore aim at confident recall of facts rather than vague familiarity.

Chapter weightage, approximately

NISM does not publish an official chapter-wise mark split, so the guidance below is approximate and based on the syllabus and the relative length of each workbook chapter. Use it to prioritise, not to skip anything.

  • Higher weight (spend the most time): Concept and Role of a Mutual Fund; Legal and Regulatory Framework; Scheme Related Information; NAV, TER and Pricing of Units; Mutual Fund Scheme Selection.
  • Medium weight: Investment Landscape; Legal Structure of Mutual Funds; Taxation; Investor Services; Risk, Return and Performance of Funds.
  • Lower but scoring: Fund Distribution and Channel Management; Mutual Fund Scheme Performance (largely definitions and benchmark concepts).
  • Numerical questions usually come from NAV, TER, loads, returns (absolute, CAGR, SIP returns) and tax calculations — expect a handful, not dozens.

The 15-day study plan

Plan for roughly two hours on weekdays and three to four hours on weekends. Read the workbook chapter first, make one page of notes, and then attempt chapter-wise practice questions the same day while the material is fresh.

  • Day 1: Investment Landscape — savings vs investment, asset classes, risk profiling basics, life-cycle needs.
  • Day 2: Concept and Role of a Mutual Fund — advantages, limitations, scheme classification as per SEBI categorisation.
  • Day 3: Legal Structure of Mutual Funds — sponsor, trustees, AMC, custodian, RTA; who appoints whom and who reports to whom.
  • Day 4: Legal and Regulatory Framework — SEBI and AMFI roles, code of conduct, due diligence, investor rights.
  • Day 5: Scheme Related Information — SID, SAI, KIM, and what each document must contain; first chapter-wise practice set.
  • Day 6: Fund Distribution and Channel Management — distributor types, ARN, EUIN, commissions and empanelment.
  • Day 7: NAV, TER and Pricing of Units — practise every formula until you can do it on rough paper without notes.
  • Day 8: Taxation — capital gains on equity and debt-oriented funds, dividend (IDCW) taxation, STT. Note that tax rules change through Finance Acts, so read the current workbook edition.
  • Day 9: Investor Services — NFO process, KYC, transaction timelines, SIP/SWP/STP, nomination and transmission.
  • Day 10: Risk, Return and Performance of Funds — standard deviation, beta, Sharpe ratio, risk-o-meter.
  • Day 11: Mutual Fund Scheme Performance and Scheme Selection — benchmarks, tracking error, matching schemes to investor profiles.
  • Day 12: First full-length timed mock test, followed by two hours of reviewing every wrong and every guessed answer.
  • Day 13: Revise the three weakest chapters from the mock; second full-length mock in the evening.
  • Day 14: Formula sheet and regulatory numbers revision; third mock test.
  • Day 15: Light revision of your one-page notes only, sleep early, and check your admit details and centre location.

How to revise so the facts stay

V-A rewards precise recall. Many wrong answers come from confusing two similar-sounding entities — for example, the trustee and the AMC, or the SID and the SAI. On every revision day, test yourself by writing answers from memory before looking at notes.

Keep a running 'error log' in a notebook: the question topic, what you answered, the correct answer and the one-line reason. By Day 13 this log is your most valuable revision material because it reflects your personal blind spots, not a generic summary.

  • Make a single sheet of all numerical limits and timelines mentioned in the workbook, and revise it daily from Day 8.
  • Draw the mutual fund structure as a diagram once; it fixes the roles of sponsor, trustee, AMC, custodian and RTA in memory.
  • Re-solve every NAV and TER question you got wrong rather than just reading the explanation.
  • Read regulatory chapters twice — once for understanding, once for the exact wording.

Mock-test schedule and how to use it

Take at least three full-length timed mock tests in the last four days. Sit them in one stretch, without notes and with a timer set to 120 minutes, so the conditions resemble the real test. After each attempt, spend as long reviewing the explanations as you spent writing the test; the review is where most of the learning happens.

Track three numbers per mock: your score, the number of questions you skipped, and your accuracy on attempted questions. If accuracy on attempted questions is below about 75 percent, slow down and skip more; if it is above 90 percent and you are skipping a lot, you are being too cautious.

Common mistakes to avoid

The commonest mistake is reading the workbook cover to cover once and assuming that is enough. Passive reading creates familiarity, and familiarity feels like knowledge until you face four similar options. The second mistake is ignoring the taxation and investor services chapters because they look dry; they are full of direct, factual questions.

Finally, do not rely on old notes or third-party summaries for numbers. Limits, tax rates and timelines are revised, and the current workbook on nism.ac.in is the reference the exam follows.

What to do on exam day

Do a first pass answering everything you are sure of, marking the rest for review. In the second pass, attempt marked questions only where you can eliminate at least two options. With negative marking at 0.25, an educated guess between two options is reasonable; a blind guess between four is not. Leave ten minutes at the end to check that you have not left questions you meant to answer unmarked.

FAQs

Is 15 days enough to prepare for NISM Series V-A?

For most graduates who can study two to three hours a day, 15 days is a realistic window. Freshers with no exposure to financial markets may want 20 to 25 days, giving extra time to Taxation, NAV calculations and the regulatory chapters, and to take more full-length practice tests before booking.

Which chapters are most important for NISM V-A?

Based on the syllabus, the regulatory framework, scheme related information, NAV and TER, and scheme selection chapters tend to carry the most questions. NISM does not publish an official split, so read every chapter and use weightage only to decide where to spend extra revision time.

How many mock tests should I take before the NISM V-A exam?

Aim for at least three full-length timed attempts in the final week, plus chapter-wise practice after each topic. The number matters less than the review: go through every wrong and guessed answer, note the reason, and revise that concept before your next attempt.
