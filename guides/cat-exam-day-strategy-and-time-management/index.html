CAT Exam Day Strategy and Time Management Tips

A clear CAT exam day strategy often separates candidates with similar preparation. With three sections on separate timers, sets that vary sharply in difficulty and a marking scheme that punishes careless attempts, how you use your minutes matters as much as what you know. This guide sets out a practical time plan for VARC, DILR and Quant, explains how to choose DILR sets and Quant questions, how to treat TITA questions, and how to recover when a section feels difficult. Practise these ideas in mocks before the exam; strategy that has not been rehearsed rarely holds up under pressure.

Last updated 19 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Pattern you are planning for (indicative)

Our mock tests follow an indicative pattern of 66 questions, 198 marks and 120 minutes, with three sections on separate timers, three marks per correct answer and one mark deducted for a wrong multiple-choice answer. TITA questions have traditionally carried no negative marking. Indicative — confirm in the latest official notification on iimcat.ac.in.

Separate timers mean you cannot move between sections. Each section is a 40-minute block (indicative) with its own plan.

VARC: a 40-minute plan

Reading comprehension carries most of the VARC marks, so give RC the larger share of time and handle verbal ability questions efficiently. Start with the passage that looks most readable to you; a good first passage builds rhythm.

For each passage, read actively, summarising each paragraph in a few words. Answer questions by returning to the specific lines rather than relying on memory. In para jumbles and odd-sentence questions, look for linking words and pronoun references first.

If you find a passage unusually dense, it is reasonable to leave it for later and return if time allows. Passages on familiar subjects often take far less time, so the order in which you attempt them can change your score noticeably.

  • About 26–28 minutes: RC passages (choose order by readability)
  • About 10–12 minutes: para summary, para jumbles, odd sentence
  • Last 1–2 minutes: review marked questions
  • Skip an RC question if two options remain equally strong after a re-read

DILR: set selection is everything

DILR is where strategy matters most. You will rarely solve every set, so the first few minutes of scanning are crucial. Spend about four to five minutes reading the opening lines of all sets and ranking them by apparent ease: clear data, fewer variables and questions that do not need the complete arrangement.

Commit to your top two sets and solve them carefully, then move to a third. If a set does not open up within five or six minutes, step away and try the next one. Two fully solved sets often score better than four half-solved ones.

  • Minutes 0–5: scan every set, rank by ease
  • Minutes 5–30: solve two to three chosen sets carefully
  • Minutes 30–38: attempt a further set or partial questions from it
  • Minutes 38–40: check answers in TITA questions

Quant: two passes

Quant rewards selection. In the first pass, move through all questions, solving those that look direct within about two minutes and marking those that need longer. In the second pass, return to the marked questions in order of how promising they looked.

Avoid getting stuck. If a question is still unresolved after three minutes, leave it. Use options wisely in multiple-choice questions — substituting values or checking extremes often saves time.

Distribute your attempts across topics rather than spending all your time on a single area. A mix of arithmetic, algebra and geometry questions in your first pass usually gives a more stable score than relying on one strength.

  • Pass 1 (about 25 minutes): quick and sure questions
  • Pass 2 (about 13 minutes): marked questions, best first
  • Final 2 minutes: re-check TITA entries for typing errors

Negative marking and TITA questions

With one mark deducted for a wrong MCQ answer against three for a right one (indicative), a guess after eliminating two of four options has a positive expected value, while a blind guess is roughly break-even and adds risk. Attempt MCQs when you have a sound basis, not on hope.

TITA questions have traditionally carried no negative marking, so if you have made real progress on one, enter your best answer rather than leaving it blank. Confirm the current marking scheme on iimcat.ac.in before relying on this.

When a section goes badly

Almost every candidate meets a section that feels harder than expected. Remember that everyone in your slot faces the same paper, and the scoring process accounts for that. The worst response is panic-driven guessing; the best is to slow down, find the easiest remaining questions and secure them.

When the timer ends, let the section go completely. The next section starts at zero, and carrying frustration forward is how one bad section becomes two.

Rehearse your strategy in mocks

Taking full-length timed mock tests and reviewing the explanations is how this strategy becomes instinct. After each mock, note time per RC passage, which DILR sets you chose and whether better ones were available, and how many Quant questions you abandoned too late.

Fix your section plans about three weeks before the exam and stop experimenting. In the final week, take mocks at the same time of day as your slot and treat each as a dress rehearsal.

  • Record time per RC passage and per DILR set
  • After each mock, re-solve the sets you skipped to check your selection
  • Keep a one-page 'rules for exam day' sheet and refine it
  • Practise one tough mock a week to build composure

Common time traps in CAT

A few traps catch candidates every year. In VARC, spending too long on one difficult passage leaves no time for an easier one later. In DILR, persisting with a set because you have already invested ten minutes in it is the classic sunk-cost error. In Quant, a single long calculation can quietly consume five minutes.

The common fix is a personal time limit for each unit of work: a passage, a set or a question. When you hit the limit, make a decision — finish quickly, mark and move, or abandon. Practising this rule in mocks makes it automatic on exam day.

  • Set a time cap per RC passage, per DILR set and per Quant question
  • Do not continue a set just because you have invested time in it
  • Keep an eye on the section timer every few minutes

On the day itself

Check your admit card, reporting time, ID requirements and centre rules on iimcat.ac.in in advance. Reach early, use the waiting time to stay calm rather than revising, and settle your plan for VARC before the timer starts.

FAQs

How should I manage time in the CAT exam?

Treat each section as a separate timed block. In VARC, give most of your time to RC; in DILR, spend the first few minutes scanning and ranking sets; in Quant, use two passes, solving quick questions first and returning to marked ones. Rehearse this plan in mocks.

How many DILR sets should I attempt in CAT?

There is no fixed number. Most candidates do better by solving two or three sets accurately than by touching every set. Spend the first few minutes ranking sets by ease, then commit to the best ones and move on if a set does not open up within five or six minutes.

Should I attempt TITA questions in CAT?

TITA questions have traditionally carried no negative marking, so if you have made meaningful progress, enter your best answer. Do not spend excessive time on a TITA question at the cost of easier ones. Confirm the current marking scheme in the official notification before your exam.
