How to Prepare for CAT in 6 Months: Month-by-Month Plan

If you are wondering how to prepare for CAT in 6 months, the good news is that six months is a realistic and common timeline, even alongside a job or final-year studies. CAT tests three areas — Verbal Ability and Reading Comprehension, Data Interpretation and Logical Reasoning, and Quantitative Ability — and rewards judgement as much as knowledge. This guide breaks the six months into clear monthly goals, explains approximately how questions are spread across topics, and sets out when to start mocks and how to analyse them. It assumes about three hours on weekdays and longer sessions on weekends.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

CAT pattern (indicative)

Our mock tests follow an indicative pattern of 66 questions, 198 marks and 120 minutes, with three marks for a correct answer and one mark deducted for a wrong multiple-choice answer. Recent CATs have had three sections with separate time limits, and type-in-the-answer (TITA) questions have traditionally carried no negative marking. All of this is indicative — confirm in the latest official notification on iimcat.ac.in.

Sectional time limits mean every section matters on its own. Many IIMs also look at sectional performance, so a lopsided profile is risky.

  • VARC: about 24 questions (approximately)
  • DILR: about 20 questions (approximately)
  • Quantitative Ability: about 22 questions (approximately)
  • Marking: +3 correct, −1 wrong MCQ; TITA traditionally no negative (indicative)

Approximate topic weightage

Based on the syllabus and recent papers, reading comprehension forms the bulk of VARC, with para summary, para jumbles and odd-sentence questions making up the rest. DILR is organised as sets of four to six questions each, drawn from arrangements, games and tournaments, tables and charts, and mixed logic puzzles.

In Quant, arithmetic (percentages, ratio, profit and loss, time and work, speed and distance, averages, mixtures, interest) has usually been the largest area, followed by algebra (equations, inequalities, functions, logarithms, progressions), then geometry and mensuration, with number system and modern maths (permutations, combinations, probability, sets) contributing the rest.

This spread shifts a little from year to year, so treat it as a guide to where your time should go first rather than a list of topics you can skip. Arithmetic and algebra together deserve the most Quant time, while in DILR the skill of choosing sets matters more than mastering any single type.

The six-month plan, month by month

Work on all three sections every week. A typical weekday: 60 minutes Quant, 45 minutes DILR, 45 minutes VARC and 30 minutes of reading. Weekends: longer practice and, from month three, mocks.

  • Month 1: Quant — arithmetic fundamentals and number system; DILR — two sets a day of simple arrangements and tables; VARC — daily reading habit, two RC passages a day
  • Month 2: Quant — algebra (equations, inequalities, functions); DILR — games, tournaments, Venn and grid puzzles; VARC — para jumbles and summaries, RC on unfamiliar subjects
  • Month 3: Quant — geometry, mensuration, progressions; DILR — mixed sets under time; VARC — inference and tone questions; first full mock and one sectional per week
  • Month 4: Quant — modern maths, logarithms, revisit arithmetic; DILR — set selection practice; VARC — speed with accuracy; one mock per week with analysis
  • Month 5: two mocks per week, topic revision based on mock errors, sectional tests in your weakest area
  • Month 6: two to three mocks per week, revise formulas and error notebook, last week light practice only

Reading habit for VARC

VARC cannot be crammed. Reading dense, unfamiliar material — essays on philosophy, economics, science, art and society — builds the comprehension and stamina that RC demands. Read 30 to 45 minutes daily from month one.

After each article, write a two-line summary and note the author's main argument and tone. This trains exactly the skills RC and para-summary questions test.

Mock tests and analysis

Mocks are where CAT preparation becomes real. Taking full-length timed mock tests and reviewing every explanation teaches you section-wise time management, set selection in DILR and question selection in Quant. Spend two to three hours analysing each mock: re-attempt questions you skipped, check whether wrong answers were concept or reading errors, and note sets you should have chosen instead.

Track sectional scores and accuracy over time. Improvement is rarely linear; focus on trends across five or six mocks rather than any single score.

Keep a mock log with sectional scores, attempts, accuracy and three takeaways from each test. Before every new mock, read the last three entries. This keeps your analysis from becoming a forgotten notebook and ensures each mock builds on the previous one.

  • Months 1–2: topic practice and sectional tests only
  • Month 3: one full mock every two weeks
  • Month 4: one mock per week
  • Month 5: two mocks per week
  • Month 6: two to three mocks per week, tapering in the last week

For working professionals

If you work full-time, keep weekdays for concept learning and practice in two shorter blocks — early morning and late evening — and use weekends for mocks and analysis. Two to three hours on weekdays and six to eight hours across a weekend is enough for this plan.

Protect your mock days from social and work commitments in months five and six. A missed mock can be rescheduled, but a missed analysis loses most of the value.

Common mistakes in CAT preparation

The most common mistake is collecting too many resources. Pick one main source for concepts in each section and a single mock series, and finish them. Another is postponing mocks until the syllabus feels complete; in CAT, mocks teach selection and time management that concept study cannot.

Many candidates also neglect VARC because it feels unpredictable, or DILR because it seems to depend on luck. Both improve steadily with regular, timed practice. Finally, avoid comparing your mock percentiles with others on forums; focus on your own trend and on fixing the specific mistakes your analysis reveals.

  • One concept source per section, one mock series
  • Start mocks by month three
  • Practise VARC and DILR daily, not only Quant
  • Track your own trend, not others' scores

Official details and final weeks

Registration windows, the number of questions, sectional timings and test-centre rules can change from year to year. Check iimcat.ac.in for the latest notification and admit card details. In the final week, avoid new topics, take one or two light mocks, and keep your sleep aligned with your test slot.

FAQs

Is 6 months enough to prepare for CAT?

Yes, six months is a common and realistic timeline for most candidates, including working professionals, if you study consistently and take 20 or more mocks with proper analysis. Candidates with weak maths or reading habits may benefit from starting a little earlier.

How many hours a day should I study for CAT?

About three hours on weekdays and six to eight hours across the weekend works for most candidates over six months. The quality of practice and mock analysis matters more than raw hours, so keep sessions focused and track your progress section by section.

When should I start taking CAT mock tests?

Start with an occasional full mock around the third month, once you have covered core concepts, then increase to one a week and finally two or three a week in the last two months. Analyse each mock thoroughly before taking the next one.
