How to Prepare for IBPS PO Prelims in 60 Days

Sixty days is a realistic window if you want to know how to prepare for IBPS PO prelims seriously while working or studying. The prelims tests three sections — English Language, Quantitative Aptitude and Reasoning Ability — under tight time pressure, so preparation is as much about speed and selection as it is about concepts. This guide lays out an eight-week plan with weekly targets, explains approximately how marks are spread across topics, and shows when to start full mocks and how to analyse them. It assumes three to four hours a day; adjust the pace if you have more or less time.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

IBPS PO prelims pattern (indicative)

Our mock tests follow an indicative pattern of 100 questions, 100 marks and 60 minutes, with 0.25 of a mark deducted for each wrong answer. In recent cycles the paper has been split into three sections with separate time limits. Treat all of this as indicative — confirm in the latest official notification on ibps.in, which also lists eligibility, dates and the number of sections.

Because the overall cut-off and sectional cut-offs both matter, you cannot afford a weak section. The plan below gives each section steady time every week rather than finishing one subject before starting the next.

  • English Language: roughly 30 questions (approximately)
  • Quantitative Aptitude: roughly 35 questions (approximately)
  • Reasoning Ability: roughly 35 questions (approximately)
  • Negative marking: 0.25 per wrong answer (indicative)

Topic weightage: what to master first

Based on the syllabus and the usual shape of the paper, a few topics carry most of the marks. In Quant, Data Interpretation and simplification or approximation give the most questions, followed by number series, quadratic equations and arithmetic. In Reasoning, puzzles and seating arrangements dominate, with inequalities, syllogism, coding-decoding, blood relations and direction sense making up the rest. In English, reading comprehension, cloze test, error spotting and para jumbles are the core.

Start with the high-weight topics and keep the smaller ones for weeks four to six. That way, even if time runs short, the topics that decide your score are already strong.

The 60-day week-by-week plan

Each day, split your time across all three sections: about 75 minutes Quant, 75 minutes Reasoning, 45 minutes English and 15 minutes of revision notes. Read one editorial or long article daily for comprehension practice.

  • Week 1: Quant — simplification, approximation, percentages, ratio; Reasoning — inequalities, coding-decoding, direction; English — reading comprehension basics, grammar rules
  • Week 2: Quant — number series, quadratic equations, averages; Reasoning — linear and circular seating; English — error spotting, cloze test
  • Week 3: Quant — profit and loss, simple and compound interest, time and work; Reasoning — floor and box puzzles, syllogism; English — para jumbles, fillers
  • Week 4: Quant — speed, time and distance, mixtures, data interpretation (tables, bar); Reasoning — blood relations, order and ranking, mixed puzzles; first full-length mock at the weekend
  • Week 5: Quant — DI (pie, line, caselet), probability, mensuration; Reasoning — scheduling puzzles, data sufficiency; English — RC speed work; two mocks this week
  • Week 6: Sectional tests in your weakest section every day; revise all formulas; two to three mocks
  • Week 7: One full mock every alternate day with deep analysis; targeted practice on errors
  • Week 8: Daily full mock in the exam time slot, light revision, no new topics in the final three days

Mock tests and revision schedule

From week four, taking full-length timed mock tests and reviewing the explanations becomes the centre of your preparation. A mock is only useful if you spend at least as long analysing it as taking it. For each wrong or skipped question, ask: was it a concept gap, a calculation slip, or a poor choice to attempt it at all?

Keep an error notebook with three columns: topic, mistake and fix. Revise it every Sunday. Over eight weeks this notebook becomes your most valuable revision material.

  • Weeks 1–3: sectional practice only, 20–25 questions per topic
  • Week 4: 1 full mock
  • Week 5–6: 2–3 full mocks per week
  • Weeks 7–8: 4–6 full mocks per week
  • Every Sunday: error-notebook revision and formula sheet

Section-wise tips

Quant: memorise squares to 30, cubes to 20 and fraction-percentage equivalents up to 1/20; these alone save minutes in simplification and DI. Reasoning: practise drawing puzzle diagrams quickly and learn to spot which puzzle is solvable in under five minutes. English: read actively, summarise each paragraph in a phrase, and learn common grammar rules rather than lists of rare words.

Speed comes from repetition. Time yourself on every practice set, even early on, so that working under the clock feels normal by the time you start mocks.

In Reasoning, practise puzzles in increasing order of difficulty: start with simple linear arrangements, then circular, then floor and box, and finally scheduling puzzles with months or days. In English, keep a short list of grammar rules you get wrong in practice and revise it before every mock. In Quant, learn to recognise DI questions that need only approximate values; calculating exactly when the options are far apart wastes time that you will need later in the section.

If you are a beginner or working full-time

If you are starting from scratch, spend the first two weeks only on basics: arithmetic concepts, simple puzzles and grammar. If you work full-time, two hours on weekdays and six on weekends is enough for a 60-day plan, but you will need to cut sectional practice slightly and protect your mock-test days.

Whatever your situation, consistency matters more than long weekend marathons. A fixed daily slot builds the habit that carries you through eight weeks.

How to analyse a mock test properly

A good analysis follows the same steps every time. First, record attempts, correct answers and accuracy for each section. Second, re-solve every question you skipped without the timer; if you can solve it in under a minute, your selection was the problem, not your knowledge. Third, read the explanation for every wrong answer and write the fix in your error notebook.

Finally, look at time. Note which puzzle or DI set took longest and whether it was worth it. Over several mocks, you will see a pattern — perhaps you over-invest in the first puzzle, or rush English. Fixing that pattern is often worth five to ten marks.

Keep official details in view

Patterns, sectional timings and eligibility can change between cycles. Check ibps.in for the latest notification before you finalise your plan, and download the official information handout once it is released.

FAQs

Can I clear IBPS PO prelims in 60 days?

Sixty days is enough to cover the prelims syllabus and build speed if you study three to four hours daily and take regular mocks from week four. No plan can promise a result, but a steady schedule with honest mock analysis gives you a realistic chance, even as a first-time candidate.

Which section is most important in IBPS PO prelims?

All three matter because sectional cut-offs have applied in recent cycles. Quant and Reasoning usually carry more questions, with DI and puzzles being the highest-weight topics. English is often the most scoring for strong readers. Build a minimum comfort level in each before trying to maximise one.

How many mock tests should I take for IBPS PO?

Aim for around 20 to 25 full-length mocks across the last five weeks, plus sectional tests. The number matters less than the analysis: review every mock carefully, note why you lost marks and fix those topics before the next test.
