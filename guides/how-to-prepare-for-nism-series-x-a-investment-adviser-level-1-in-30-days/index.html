How to Prepare for NISM Series X-A in 30 Days

Anyone working out how to prepare for NISM Series X-A quickly realises that the syllabus is broad. Investment Adviser Level 1 covers financial planning basics, time value of money, every major asset class, portfolio construction, behavioural finance, operations and the SEBI regulations for investment advisers. A 30-day plan gives a working professional enough time to read the workbook properly, practise the calculation chapters and sit several full-length tests. This guide lays out the plan week by week, with approximate chapter weightage, a revision routine and a mock-test schedule built around the three-hour exam.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

The exam pattern and what it means

The indicative pattern is 100 questions for 100 marks in 180 minutes, with a pass mark of 60 percent and negative marking of 0.25 marks for each wrong answer. Treat these as indicative — confirm in the latest official notification on nism.ac.in, since NISM revises workbooks and exam structures.

Three hours for 100 questions suggests that a fair number of questions take more than a minute: case-style questions, time value of money calculations and portfolio return problems. The 60 percent pass line combined with negative marking means you cannot afford large gaps. The plan below therefore spreads effort across the whole syllabus rather than gambling on a few chapters.

Approximate chapter weightage

NISM does not publish an official chapter-wise split. The grouping below is approximate and based on the syllabus and the depth of each workbook chapter. Use it to plan revision time, not to drop chapters.

  • High: Time Value of Money; Equity Investment; Debt Investment; Mutual Funds; Asset Allocation & Portfolio Construction; Regulatory Framework for Investment Advisers.
  • Medium: Investment Advisory & Financial Planning Basics; Client Financial Position & Debt Management; Derivatives; Portfolio Performance Evaluation; Ethics, Conflicts & Grievance Redressal.
  • Lower but direct: Indian Financial Markets; Securities Market Segments; Real Estate, Gold & Other Assets; PMS, AIF & Other Managed Portfolios; Behavioural Finance; Operational Aspects (KYC, Demat, Transactions).
  • Calculation-heavy areas: TVM (future value, present value, annuities, EMI), bond yields and duration basics, portfolio returns and risk-adjusted measures.

Week 1: planning basics and the maths

Front-load the maths. Time value of money appears across the syllabus — in debt, retirement-style goal calculations and loan questions — so getting fluent early pays off for the rest of the month. Study two hours on weekdays and three to four hours on weekends.

  • Days 1–2: Investment Advisory & Financial Planning Basics — the planning process, client life cycle, goal setting, the role of an adviser.
  • Days 3–5: Time Value of Money — compounding, discounting, annuities, EMI, real vs nominal returns; solve at least 25 problems.
  • Day 6: Client Financial Position & Debt Management — personal balance sheet, cash flow statement, liquidity and debt ratios.
  • Day 7: Revise Week 1 and complete chapter-wise practice questions for all three chapters.

Week 2: markets and asset classes

Week 2 builds product knowledge. Questions often ask which product fits a particular investor, so note the risk, return, liquidity and tax features of each one as you go.

  • Day 8: Indian Financial Markets and Securities Market Segments — primary and secondary markets, money and debt markets, participants and regulators.
  • Days 9–10: Equity Investment — valuation basics, ratios, fundamental and technical analysis at an introductory level.
  • Days 11–12: Debt Investment — bond features, yield measures, price-yield relationship, duration, credit risk.
  • Day 13: Derivatives — futures and options basics, uses for hedging.
  • Day 14: Mutual Funds — structure, categories, costs, and how to evaluate schemes.

Week 3: portfolios, behaviour and regulation

This week shifts from products to how an adviser combines them and the rules that govern advice.

  • Day 15: Real Estate, Gold & Other Assets; PMS, AIF & Other Managed Portfolios.
  • Days 16–17: Asset Allocation & Portfolio Construction — strategic vs tactical allocation, rebalancing, diversification.
  • Day 18: Portfolio Performance Evaluation — absolute and annualised returns, Sharpe, Treynor and other measures covered in the workbook.
  • Day 19: Behavioural Finance — common biases and how they affect investor decisions.
  • Day 20: Operational Aspects — KYC, demat, transactions and documentation.
  • Days 21–22: Regulatory Framework for Investment Advisers and Ethics, Conflicts & Grievance Redressal — read twice, these are exact-wording chapters.

Week 4: mocks and revision

The final eight days are about consolidation. Take full-length timed mock tests in a single 180-minute sitting and review every explanation afterwards. The review usually takes as long as the test itself, and it is time well spent.

  • Day 23: First full-length mock and detailed review.
  • Day 24: Rework all TVM and portfolio numericals from your error log.
  • Day 25: Second mock; revise the regulatory chapter.
  • Day 26: Revise asset class comparison notes and behavioural finance.
  • Day 27: Third mock; update the error log.
  • Day 28: Revise weakest three chapters identified from mocks.
  • Day 29: Fourth mock or a mixed practice set of 50 questions.
  • Day 30: Light revision of formula sheet, regulatory notes and error log only.

Revision techniques that work for X-A

X-A mixes two kinds of memory: procedural (how to solve a TVM problem) and declarative (what the regulations say). Revise them differently. For procedures, re-solve problems without looking at worked answers. For regulations, write short summaries in your own words and then compare them against the workbook text to catch subtle differences.

Create a product comparison grid with rows for equity shares, bonds, mutual funds, gold, real estate, PMS and AIFs, and columns for risk, return potential, liquidity, minimum ticket size and typical investor. Many questions boil down to reading one row of that grid correctly.

  • Keep a single formula sheet for TVM, returns and ratios.
  • Revise the regulatory chapter on at least four separate days.
  • Maintain an error log and read it every evening in Week 4.

Common mistakes X-A candidates make

The most frequent mistake is treating X-A as a reading exam. The workbook is long, and candidates who read it once from start to finish without practising questions often score in the low 50s. The second is underestimating the regulatory chapter; questions on adviser obligations, segregation of advice and distribution, fee rules and record keeping are framed around exact provisions, and small wording differences decide the answer. Always read the version in the current workbook, as SEBI amends these regulations from time to time.

Exam-day time management

With 180 minutes, aim to finish the first pass in about 100 minutes, answering every question you are confident about and marking calculations and long case questions. Spend the next 60 minutes on marked questions and keep the last 20 minutes for a final review. Because of the 0.25 negative marking, guess only after eliminating at least two options. Remember that X-A is one of two certifications needed for investment adviser registration, so pace yourself for a good score rather than a narrow pass.

FAQs

Is 30 days enough to prepare for NISM Series X-A?

For most candidates with a finance or commerce background who can study two to three hours daily, 30 days is a reasonable window. If TVM and bond maths are new to you, add a week. Those already working in wealth management may manage in three weeks with consistent mock practice.

Which chapters carry the most weight in NISM X-A?

Based on the syllabus, time value of money, equity and debt investment, mutual funds, asset allocation and the investment adviser regulations tend to carry the most questions. NISM does not publish an official split, so cover every chapter and use weightage only to prioritise revision.

How difficult is NISM Series X-A?

It is moderately difficult because of the breadth of the syllabus, the 60 percent pass mark in the indicative pattern and negative marking. The maths is not advanced, but it needs practice. Regular chapter-wise questions and a few full-length timed tests make the difficulty manageable.
