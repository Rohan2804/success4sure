Syllogism Rules, Venn Diagram Method and Possibility Cases

Syllogism is a near-certain guest in the Reasoning section of IBPS PO, IBPS Clerk, SBI PO, SBI Clerk and RBI Grade B Phase I, usually as a set of three to five questions. SSC CGL and CHSL ask it less often, normally one question at a time. The questions look easy, which is the trap: most marks are lost by reading 'some' as 'all' or by missing an either-or pair. Once you draw the minimum Venn diagram for every statement, each question takes under 40 seconds.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

The four basic statements

'All A are B' puts circle A fully inside B. 'Some A are B' means at least one A is a B, and it may even be that all A are B. 'No A is B' keeps the circles apart. 'Some A are not B' means at least one A lies outside B. Always draw the minimum overlap the statement forces, never more.

Definite conclusions versus possibilities

A definite conclusion must be true in every diagram that satisfies the statements. A possibility conclusion ('All X being Y is a possibility') only needs one valid diagram where it is true without breaking any statement. So 'Some A are B is a possibility' is true when A and B are not forced apart, and false when a 'No' statement directly or indirectly separates them.

Either-or pairs

When neither conclusion is definite on its own, check whether the two together cover every case. 'Some A are B' and 'No A is B' form a complementary pair, as do 'All A are B' and 'Some A are not B'. Both must have the same two terms, and neither may already follow. Then the answer is 'Either I or II follows'.

Only a few

'Only a few A are B' means some A are B and some A are not B. It therefore kills the possibility 'All A are B'. Many students miss this second half and wrongly mark 'All A being B is a possibility' as true.

Shortcuts and tricks

  • Some + All gives Some: Some A are B, All B are C → Some A are C. But All + Some gives nothing: All A are B, Some B are C → no link between A and C.
  • All + No gives No: All A are B, No B is C → No A is C. That also means 'Some C are A is a possibility' is false.
  • Some + No gives 'Some ... not': Some A are B, No B is C → Some A are not C (not 'Some C are not A').
  • 'Some' and 'No' conclusions can be reversed (Some A are B = Some B are A), but 'All' cannot: All mangoes are fruits does not mean all fruits are mangoes.
  • Before choosing either-or, confirm the pair uses the same subject and predicate and that neither part is already definite.

Worked examples

Example 1

Statements: All pens are books. Some books are bags. Conclusions: I. Some pens are bags. II. Some bags are books.

Solution

Draw pens inside books. The 'some' overlap between books and bags can sit in the part of books that has no pens, so I is not definite. II is just the reverse of 'Some books are bags', so it follows. Answer: only II follows.

Example 2

Statements: Some cups are plates. No plate is a spoon. Conclusions: I. Some cups are spoons. II. No cup is a spoon.

Solution

Cups that are plates cannot be spoons, but cups outside plates may or may not touch spoons. So neither I nor II is definite. They have the same terms and are complementary (some versus none). Answer: either I or II follows.

Example 3

Statements: All rivers are lakes. No lake is a pond. Conclusions: I. No river is a pond. II. Some ponds being rivers is a possibility.

Solution

Rivers sit inside lakes and lakes are fully separate from ponds, so rivers are separate from ponds too. I follows. Because the separation is forced, II cannot be a possibility. Answer: only I follows.

Example 4

Statements: Only a few teachers are writers. All writers are poets. Conclusions: I. Some teachers are poets. II. All teachers being poets is a possibility.

Solution

Teachers who are writers are also poets, so I follows. For II, the teachers who are not writers can still be placed inside the poets circle without breaking any statement, so II is a valid possibility. Answer: both follow. Note that 'All teachers being writers' would not have been possible here.

Practice questions

Question 1

Statements: All coins are notes. No note is a cheque. Conclusions: I. No coin is a cheque. II. Some cheques are coins.

  1. Only I follows
  2. Only II follows
  3. Either I or II follows
  4. Neither I nor II follows
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Only I follows

Coins lie inside notes and notes are fully separate from cheques, so no coin can be a cheque. II contradicts I, so only I follows.

Question 2

Statements: Some apples are mangoes. All mangoes are grapes. Conclusions: I. Some apples are grapes. II. Some grapes are apples.

  1. Only I follows
  2. Both I and II follow
  3. Only II follows
  4. Neither follows
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Both I and II follow

The apples that are mangoes are also grapes, so I follows. II is simply I reversed, and a 'some' statement can be reversed, so both follow.

Question 3

Statements: All doors are windows. Some windows are walls. Conclusions: I. Some doors are walls. II. No door is a wall.

  1. Only I follows
  2. Only II follows
  3. Either I or II follows
  4. Both follow
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Either I or II follows

Neither conclusion is forced because the window-wall overlap may or may not include doors. The two conclusions use the same terms and are complementary, so either I or II follows.

Question 4

Statements: No bat is a ball. All balls are stumps. Conclusions: I. Some stumps are not bats. II. All bats being stumps is a possibility.

  1. Only I follows
  2. Only II follows
  3. Neither follows
  4. Both I and II follow
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Both I and II follow

Balls are stumps and are not bats, so those stumps are not bats; I follows. Bats outside balls can still sit inside stumps, so II is a valid possibility. Both follow.

Question 5

Statements: Only a few cars are buses. No bus is a truck. Conclusions: I. Some cars are not trucks. II. All cars are trucks.

  1. Only I follows
  2. Only II follows
  3. Either I or II follows
  4. Neither follows
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Only I follows

The cars that are buses can never be trucks, so some cars are definitely not trucks. That same fact makes 'All cars are trucks' false, so only I follows.

FAQs

Is the Venn diagram method better than rules for syllogism?

For new-pattern questions with 'only a few' and possibility conclusions, the Venn diagram method is safer because you can test each case visually. Rules are faster for simple two-statement questions. Most toppers learn a few rules for speed but always fall back on the minimum diagram when a conclusion looks doubtful.

How many syllogism questions come in IBPS and SBI prelims?

In recent prelims papers syllogism has usually appeared as a set of about three to five questions inside the 35-question Reasoning section, though the number varies by year and shift. Treat it as a scoring area: with practice the full set can be finished in about two minutes.
