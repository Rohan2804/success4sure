Statement and Conclusion Questions: What Really Follows

Statement and conclusion questions give a short passage — often a news-style fact, a survey result or a policy line — and ask which conclusions logically follow. They are tested in IBPS PO and SBI PO mains, RBI Grade B, CMAT, MAT and occasionally SSC CGL. The difficulty is resisting the urge to add your own knowledge. A conclusion follows only if the statement supports it directly, without extra assumptions or broad generalisations.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

A conclusion must be supported by the statement

Treat the statement as the whole truth, even if it sounds odd. A conclusion follows only if a reasonable reader would arrive at it from the statement alone. If the conclusion needs information the statement does not give, it does not follow.

Avoid over-generalisation

'Sales of electric two-wheelers in Pune rose last year' does not support 'Petrol two-wheelers are no longer sold in India.' Watch for conclusions that jump from one city to the country, from some to all, or from a single year to a permanent trend.

Restatements usually follow

A conclusion that says the same thing in other words, or narrows it safely ('Some members of the survey group preferred online banking'), normally follows. Narrowing is safe; widening is not.

Facts versus opinions

When the statement reports someone's view ('The minister said...'), a conclusion stating the view as a fact does not follow. It only follows that the person holds or expressed that view.

Shortcuts and tricks

  • Underline quantity words in the statement (some, many, most, all). A conclusion with a stronger quantity word rarely follows.
  • Conclusions that predict the future from a past fact usually do not follow unless the statement itself talks about a trend.
  • If a conclusion is just common sense but unrelated to the statement, it does not follow.
  • Two contradictory conclusions can form an either-or pair only when the statement makes one of them necessarily true.
  • Policy statements support conclusions about the policy's intention, not about its final success.

Worked examples

Example 1

Statement: 'A survey of 2,000 customers in Mumbai found that 70% preferred UPI over cash for purchases under ₹500.' Conclusions: I. Most surveyed Mumbai customers prefer UPI for small purchases. II. Cash will disappear from India within five years.

Solution

I restates the survey result for the surveyed group, so it follows. II jumps from one city's survey to a national prediction about the future. Only I follows.

Example 2

Statement: 'The RBI governor said that inflation is expected to ease in the coming quarters.' Conclusions: I. Inflation will definitely fall in the coming quarters. II. The RBI governor expects inflation to ease.

Solution

The statement reports an expectation, not a certainty, so I does not follow. II accurately describes what was said. Only II follows.

Example 3

Statement: 'All employees of the branch must complete the cyber-security module by Friday.' Conclusions: I. Some employees of the branch will complete the module. II. The module is meant for the branch's employees.

Solution

If all must complete it, it is reasonable to conclude some will, and the module is clearly intended for them. Both follow.

Example 4

Statement: 'The number of students appearing for CMAT has risen this year compared with last year.' Conclusions: I. CMAT has become easier. II. More students appeared for CMAT this year than last year.

Solution

Nothing in the statement speaks about difficulty, so I does not follow. II is a restatement. Only II follows.

Practice questions

Question 1

Statement: 'Most bank branches in the district now open at 9 a.m. instead of 10 a.m.' Conclusions: I. Some branches in the district open at 9 a.m. II. All branches in India open at 9 a.m.

  1. Only II follows
  2. Both follow
  3. Neither follows
  4. Only I follows
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Only I follows

If most branches open at 9 a.m., certainly some do, so I follows. II widens a district fact to the whole country and does not follow.

Question 2

Statement: 'The company's spokesperson claimed that the new app is the fastest in the market.' Conclusions: I. The new app is the fastest in the market. II. The spokesperson made a claim about the app's speed.

  1. Only II follows
  2. Only I follows
  3. Both follow
  4. Neither follows
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Only II follows

The statement reports a claim, not a proven fact, so I does not follow. It does show that the spokesperson made a speed claim, so II follows.

Question 3

Statement: 'Fewer passengers used the city's metro on weekends than on weekdays last month.' Conclusions: I. Weekday metro use was higher than weekend use last month. II. Metro fares are higher on weekends.

  1. Neither follows
  2. Only I follows
  3. Only II follows
  4. Both follow
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Only I follows

I simply restates the comparison and follows. The statement says nothing about fares, so II introduces new information and does not follow.

Question 4

Statement: 'Every candidate who scored above the cut-off was called for the interview.' Conclusions: I. Candidates below the cut-off were also called. II. Interviews were the final stage of selection.

  1. Only I follows
  2. Only II follows
  3. Neither follows
  4. Both follow
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Neither follows

The statement covers only candidates above the cut-off and does not tell us about others, so I does not follow. Nothing indicates the interview was the final stage, so II also fails.

Question 5

Statement: 'The library extended its timings to 10 p.m. during the exam season.' Conclusions: I. The library stays open later during the exam season. II. The library expected students to use it in the evenings.

  1. Only I follows
  2. Only II follows
  3. Neither follows
  4. Both follow
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Both follow

I restates the extension of timings. II follows as a reasonable conclusion about the purpose of extending hours in exam season.

FAQs

Can I use general knowledge to decide if a conclusion follows?

Only the most basic common sense. If a conclusion depends on facts not in the statement — even well-known ones — it usually does not follow. Examiners design wrong options around true-sounding facts that the statement does not support, so stick closely to the text.

Why do 'possibly' conclusions confuse candidates?

A conclusion like 'The scheme may help some farmers' is weak and cautious, so it often follows if the statement points that way. Strong, certain conclusions ('The scheme will double incomes') rarely follow. Match the strength of the conclusion to the strength of the statement.
