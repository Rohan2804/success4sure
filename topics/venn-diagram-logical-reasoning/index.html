Logical Venn Diagrams: Relationships and Counting Regions

Logical Venn diagram questions come in two forms. In the first, you choose the diagram that best shows the relationship between three groups, like 'Doctors, Women, Mothers'. In the second, a diagram or data is given and you count members of a region. SSC CGL, CHSL and MTS ask one or two such questions in most papers, and bank clerk exams use the counting type. Both depend on judging which groups overlap, which sit inside others and which are separate.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Three relationship types

Inclusion: one group lies fully inside another (all mothers are women). Overlap: some members are shared (some doctors are women). Separation: no members are shared (men and women).

Reading three-group questions

Take groups two at a time. For 'Women, Mothers, Doctors': mothers are inside women; some doctors are women and some are mothers, but not all. So the picture is a mothers circle inside a women circle, with a doctors circle cutting across both.

Counting with two sets

For two overlapping groups, total in either group = A + B − both. Those in neither = total − (A + B − both).

n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B)

Only-one regions

'Only A' = A − both. In a group where 35 like tea, 30 like coffee and 10 like both, 25 like only tea and 20 like only coffee.

Three-set counting

With three groups, use the inclusion–exclusion formula and fill the regions from the centre outward. Start with the number in all three, subtract it from each pairwise overlap to get 'exactly two' regions, then subtract to find 'only one' regions. Check that all seven regions plus 'none' add up to the total.

n(A∪B∪C) = n(A) + n(B) + n(C) − n(A∩B) − n(B∩C) − n(A∩C) + n(A∩B∩C)

Shortcuts and tricks

  • Ask 'Can a member be in both?' for every pair. Yes and always → inclusion; yes but not always → overlap; never → separation.
  • Units of measurement, family members, and categories like 'country, state, city' usually form nested circles.
  • Professions overlap with genders and nationalities; they rarely nest.
  • In counting questions, fill the 'both' region first and work outward.
  • If the counts in each group exceed the total, the overlap must be at least the excess.
  • For pairs like 'Fruits and Vegetables' or 'Men and Women', treat the groups as separate unless the question states a link.

Worked examples

Example 1

Which diagram best shows the relationship between India, Maharashtra and Mumbai?

Solution

Mumbai is a city in Maharashtra, and Maharashtra is a state in India. Each lies fully inside the next: three concentric circles, with Mumbai innermost.

Example 2

Which diagram best shows Doctors, Women and Mothers?

Solution

All mothers are women, so the mothers circle is inside the women circle. Some doctors are women and some are mothers, but not all, so the doctors circle overlaps both and also extends outside (male doctors).

Example 3

In a class of 60 students, 35 like tea, 30 like coffee and 10 like both. How many like neither?

Solution

Either = 35 + 30 − 10 = 55. Neither = 60 − 55 = 5.

Example 4

Of 50 employees, 28 use UPI, 22 use net banking and 12 use both. How many use neither?

Solution

Either = 28 + 22 − 12 = 38. Neither = 50 − 38 = 12.

Example 5

In a hostel of 100 students, 45 play cricket, 30 play football and 15 play both. How many students play exactly one of the two games?

Solution

Only cricket = 45 − 15 = 30. Only football = 30 − 15 = 15. Exactly one game = 30 + 15 = 45. As a check, at least one game = 45 + 30 − 15 = 60, and 60 − 15 (both) = 45.

Example 6

Which diagram best shows Fruits, Mangoes and Vegetables?

Solution

All mangoes are fruits, so the mangoes circle sits inside the fruits circle. Vegetables are a separate food category in such questions, so the vegetables circle stands apart from both. The picture is one circle inside another, with a third circle separate.

Practice questions

Question 1

Which relationship best describes Rupee, Currency and Coin?

  1. All three are separate
  2. Currency is inside coin
  3. Rupee contains currency
  4. Coin and rupee overlap, both inside currency
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Coin and rupee overlap, both inside currency

Rupees and coins are both forms of currency, and some rupee money exists as coins while some does not. So they overlap inside the larger currency circle.

Question 2

In a group of 60 students, 35 like tea and 10 like both tea and coffee. How many like only tea?

  1. 25
  2. 10
  3. 35
  4. 20
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. 25

Only tea equals all tea lovers minus those who like both, so 35 − 10 = 25 students like only tea.

Question 3

Of 40 people, 18 read English newspapers, 15 read Hindi newspapers and 5 read both. How many read neither?

  1. 7
  2. 12
  3. 17
  4. 10
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. 12

Readers of at least one = 18 + 15 − 5 = 28. People reading neither = 40 − 28 = 12.

Question 4

Which diagram fits Men, Women and Teachers?

  1. Three separate circles
  2. Three concentric circles
  3. Men and women separate, teachers overlapping both
  4. Teachers inside women
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Men and women separate, teachers overlapping both

No person is both a man and a woman in this question, so those circles are separate. Teachers can be men or women, so the teachers circle overlaps both.

Question 5

In a survey of 100 people, 60 own a two-wheeler, 50 own a car and 30 own both. How many own neither?

  1. 30
  2. 10
  3. 40
  4. 20
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. 20

People owning at least one vehicle = 60 + 50 − 30 = 80. The rest, 100 − 80 = 20, own neither.

FAQs

How do I decide between overlap and inclusion?

Ask whether every member of one group must belong to the other. If yes, it is inclusion (all sparrows are birds). If only some do, it is overlap (some teachers are singers). Using real-world common sense is fine here, unlike syllogisms, where only the given statements count.

Are three-set counting questions asked?

Yes, occasionally. Use n(A ∪ B ∪ C) = n(A) + n(B) + n(C) − n(A∩B) − n(B∩C) − n(A∩C) + n(A∩B∩C). Fill the centre region first, then the pairwise-only regions, then the only-one regions, checking that all regions add up to the total.
