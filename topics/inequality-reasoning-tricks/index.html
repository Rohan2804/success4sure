Inequality Reasoning: Direct, Coded and Either-Or Cases

Inequality questions appear in almost every IBPS PO, IBPS Clerk, SBI PO, SBI Clerk and RBI Grade B prelims paper, typically as a set of three to five questions. They come in two forms: direct statements like P > Q ≥ R, and coded statements where symbols such as @ or % stand for the signs. Both reward one skill — reading a chain correctly and knowing when two letters have no fixed relation. Accuracy matters more than speed here because the options are designed around typical mistakes.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Combining signs along a chain

Two letters can be compared only if every sign between them points the same way. > with ≥ gives >, ≥ with ≥ gives ≥, and = keeps whatever the other sign is. So A > B ≥ C = D gives A > D. If any sign on the path points the other way, as in A > B < C, there is no definite relation between A and C.

Either-or in inequalities

If the chain proves only X ≥ Y, then neither 'X > Y' nor 'X = Y' follows alone, but together they cover every case. That makes the answer 'Either I or II follows'. The same applies for X ≤ Y split into X < Y and X = Y. Either-or is never possible when the two letters have no relation at all.

Coded inequalities

Here each symbol is defined, for example '@' means '>', '%' means '≥', '$' means '='. Convert the whole statement to real signs first, write it on your rough sheet, and only then read the conclusions. Trying to work directly with the symbols is the most common source of errors.

Combined statements

Some questions give two or three separate statements, such as F ≤ G < H and H ≥ I > J. Join them through the common letter (H here) to form one chain before comparing. If there is no common letter, no relation can be built across the statements.

Shortcuts and tricks

  • Look for the opposite-direction sign first. In P > Q < R, the moment you see > and < meeting at Q, mark 'no relation' between P and R.
  • '=' is neutral: A ≥ B = C behaves exactly like A ≥ C.
  • A strict sign anywhere in a same-direction chain makes the result strict: A ≥ B > C ≥ D gives A > D.
  • For either-or, the two conclusions must use the same pair of letters and one must be '>' while the other is '='.
  • In coded questions, rewrite all five symbols in a small table at the top of your rough sheet once; reuse it for the whole set.

Worked examples

Example 1

Statement: P > Q ≥ R = S < T. Conclusions: I. P > S. II. T > Q.

Solution

For I, the path P > Q ≥ R = S has signs in one direction with a strict sign, so P > S is true. For II, the path from Q to T reads Q ≥ R = S < T; the direction flips at S, so T and Q have no relation. Answer: only I follows.

Example 2

Statement: J ≥ K = L ≥ M. Conclusions: I. J > M. II. J = M.

Solution

The chain gives J ≥ M only. Neither J > M nor J = M is certain, but one of them must be true. Same letters, complementary signs, so either I or II follows.

Example 3

In a code, '@' means '>', '#' means '<', '$' means '=', '%' means '≥' and '&' means '≤'. Statement: M @ N $ O % P. Conclusions: I. M @ P. II. O @ P.

Solution

Decode: M > N = O ≥ P. For I, M > P is true, so M @ P follows. For II, we only know O ≥ P, so O > P is not definite. Answer: only I follows.

Example 4

Statements: F ≤ G < H; H ≥ I > J. Conclusions: I. G < I. II. H > J.

Solution

Join at H: F ≤ G < H ≥ I > J. G to I runs G < H ≥ I, which changes direction, so I does not follow. H to J runs H ≥ I > J, all one way with a strict sign, so H > J follows. Answer: only II follows.

Practice questions

Question 1

Statement: B ≥ C > D = E ≤ F. Conclusions: I. B > E. II. F > C.

  1. Only II follows
  2. Only I follows
  3. Both follow
  4. Neither follows
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Only I follows

B ≥ C > D = E gives B > E, so I follows. Between C and F the chain flips at E, so II has no definite relation.

Question 2

Statements: K < L ≤ M; M = N > O. Conclusions: I. O < L. II. N > K.

  1. Only I follows
  2. Both follow
  3. Only II follows
  4. Either I or II follows
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Only II follows

Combined chain K < L ≤ M = N > O. N and K are linked one way with a strict sign, so N > K holds. L and O meet across a flip at N, so I is not definite.

Question 3

Statement: R ≥ S = T ≥ U. Conclusions: I. R > U. II. R = U.

  1. Only I follows
  2. Neither follows
  3. Only II follows
  4. Either I or II follows
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Either I or II follows

The chain gives only R ≥ U. Neither part is certain alone, but they are complementary with the same letters, so either I or II follows.

Question 4

In a code, '@' means '≥', '#' means '<', '%' means '=', '&' means '>' and '$' means '≤'. Statement: G # H % J & K. Conclusions: I. G # J. II. K # H.

  1. Both I and II follow
  2. Only I follows
  3. Only II follows
  4. Neither follows
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Both I and II follow

Decoded, the statement is G < H = J > K. So G < J is true, and since H = J > K, K < H is also true. Both conclusions follow.

Question 5

Statements: P > Q < R; R ≥ S > T. Conclusions: I. Q > T. II. P > S.

  1. Only I follows
  2. Neither follows
  3. Only II follows
  4. Both follow
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Neither follows

Q to T runs Q < R ≥ S > T, which flips direction, so no relation. P to S runs P > Q < R, also a flip. Neither conclusion follows.

FAQs

What does 'no relation' mean in an inequality question?

It means the given statements allow the two letters to be in any order. For example, in A > B < C, A could be bigger, smaller or equal to C depending on actual values. When two letters have no relation, no conclusion comparing them follows, and they cannot form an either-or pair either.

Should I memorise sign-combination tables for coded inequalities?

You only need two ideas: signs pointing the same way combine, and a strict sign makes the result strict. Instead of a big table, decode each coded statement into real signs on your rough sheet. This takes a few seconds and removes almost all symbol-confusion errors.
