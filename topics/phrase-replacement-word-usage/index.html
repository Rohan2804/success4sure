Phrase Replacement and Word Usage Questions Explained

Phrase replacement questions bold a part of a sentence and ask which option should replace it, while word usage questions give a word and several sentences and ask in which sentence the word is used correctly or incorrectly. IBPS PO, SBI PO, RBI Grade B and clerk exams use both formats, often as sets of three to five questions. They combine grammar, idiom and vocabulary, and they reward slow, careful reading over quick guessing.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Phrase replacement: identify the error type

Read the full sentence and decide what is wrong with the bold phrase: tense, agreement, preposition, idiom, parallelism or word choice. Then pick the option that fixes exactly that error without adding another. Many papers include 'No replacement required' as an option.

Word usage: meaning and part of speech

A word like 'address' can be a noun ('my address') or a verb ('address the issue'). Word usage questions test whether the word fits each sentence in both meaning and grammar. Check each sentence independently rather than comparing them.

Confusable words

Common pairs: affect (verb) and effect (noun), principal (main, or head of a school) and principle (rule), stationary (not moving) and stationery (paper and pens), complement (complete) and compliment (praise), loose and lose, council and counsel.

Formal register

Bank-exam sentences usually come from editorials about economy and policy, so the correct option tends to be formal and precise. Casual words ('a lot of', 'got') in options are often weaker than formal equivalents ('considerable', 'received').

Meaning must stay the same

The replacement must not change the sense of the sentence. If the original says a policy 'may' help, an option saying it 'will certainly' help changes the meaning and is wrong even if grammatical.

Multiple correct sentences

Newer word-usage formats ask which sentences are correct, with options like 'Only A and C'. Judge every sentence carefully, because a single misjudgement changes the answer.

Shortcuts and tricks

  • Read the option inside the sentence, not on its own.
  • If the bold part is idiomatic and correct, do not replace it just because an option looks more formal.
  • 'Affect' is usually a verb and 'effect' usually a noun; 'to effect change' (bring about) is a formal exception.
  • Watch singular/plural carefully: 'one of the reasons' + 'is'.
  • For word usage, try replacing the word with a synonym; if the synonym fits, the usage is probably correct.
  • Eliminate options that are wordy or repeat meaning ('return back', 'combine together').
  • If two options differ only by an article or preposition, the question is testing that small word; check the fixed pairing first.

Worked examples

Example 1

Replace the bold part if needed: 'The new rules will **effect** small traders the most.'

Solution

The sentence needs a verb meaning 'influence', which is 'affect'. Replacement: 'affect'.

Example 2

Replace the bold part if needed: 'The company bought new **stationary** for its offices.'

Solution

Paper and pens are 'stationery'; 'stationary' means not moving. Replacement: 'stationery'.

Example 3

Replace the bold part if needed: 'She **is working here since** 2021.'

Solution

'Since 2021' needs a perfect tense: 'has been working here since'.

Example 4

In which sentence is 'address' used incorrectly? (A) Please address the letter to the manager. (B) The minister will address the gathering. (C) We must address to the problem quickly.

Solution

As a verb meaning 'deal with', 'address' takes a direct object; 'address to the problem' is wrong. Answer: (C).

Example 5

Replace the bold part if needed: 'Inflation, along with rising interest rates, **are hurting** household budgets.'

Solution

'Along with' does not change the subject 'Inflation', which is singular. Replacement: 'is hurting'.

Practice questions

Question 1

Replace the bold part if needed: 'The principal **complemented** the students on their results.'

  1. complemented to
  2. complimented
  3. completed
  4. No replacement required
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. complimented

Praising someone is 'complimenting' them. 'Complement' means to complete or go well with something, so it is wrong here.

Question 2

Replace the bold part if needed: 'He was careful not to **loose** his ID card.'

  1. No replacement required
  2. losing
  3. lose
  4. be loose
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. lose

The verb meaning to misplace is 'lose'. 'Loose' is an adjective meaning not tight, so the sentence needs 'lose'.

Question 3

Replace the bold part if needed: 'The panel **gave a decision** after hearing both sides.'

  1. did a decision
  2. made decision
  3. No replacement required
  4. reached a decision
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. reached a decision

The standard collocations are 'reach a decision' or 'take a decision'. 'Gave a decision' is not natural for a panel's conclusion.

Question 4

In which sentence is 'book' used incorrectly?

  1. They booked about the delay.
  2. She booked a ticket online.
  3. The police booked him for speeding.
  4. He read a book on banking.
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. They booked about the delay.

'Book' as a verb means to reserve or to register a charge. 'They booked about the delay' has no clear meaning and is incorrect.

Question 5

Replace the bold part if needed: 'Honesty is one of his strongest **principals**.'

  1. principal
  2. principles
  3. principled
  4. No replacement required
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. principles

A rule or belief is a 'principle'. 'Principal' means main or the head of a school, so 'principles' is required.

FAQs

How is phrase replacement different from sentence improvement?

They are essentially the same task with different names. SSC tends to say 'sentence improvement' and bank exams 'phrase replacement'. Both require you to find the error in the bold or underlined part and choose a fix that keeps the meaning.

How do I prepare for word usage questions?

Learn common words that work as both nouns and verbs (address, book, check, plant, conduct) and the main confusable pairs. When reading, notice how such words are used in different contexts. Practising mixed sets of sentences builds the judgement these questions need.
