Letter Series Questions: Position Gaps and Paired Patterns

Letter series questions show a sequence of letters or letter groups and ask for the next term or a missing one. SSC CGL, CHSL and MTS include one or two in nearly every reasoning paper, and bank clerk exams occasionally add them. They look like puzzles but are really arithmetic in disguise: once each letter becomes its alphabet position, the pattern is usually a simple difference, an increasing gap or an opposite-letter pairing.

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Convert to positions

Write A = 1 to Z = 26 under each letter. A, C, F, J, O becomes 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, where the gaps grow by one each time: +2, +3, +4, +5. The next gap is +6, giving 21 = U.

Backward series

Series can run backwards: Z, X, U, Q is 26, 24, 21, 17 with gaps −2, −3, −4. The next gap is −5, giving 12 = L. Wrap-around past A (or Z) is allowed in some questions; count on from the other end.

Paired and opposite-letter groups

Two-letter groups often pair a letter with its opposite (sum 27): AZ, BY, CX, so the next is DW. Others move each letter of the pair independently: BD, EG, HJ (each letter +3).

Alternating series

Two series may be interleaved. In C, Z, F, W, I, T, the odd terms C, F, I move +3 and the even terms Z, W, T move −3. Split the series and solve each half separately.

Second-level differences

When the gaps themselves are not constant, look at how the gaps change. In the series B, C, E, H, L the positions are 2, 3, 5, 8, 12 and the gaps are 1, 2, 3, 4, so the next gap is 5 and the next letter is Q (17). If the gaps double instead (1, 2, 4, 8), the series is geometric in its steps. Writing the gaps on a second line under the positions makes either pattern visible in seconds.

Missing-term questions

Sometimes the blank is in the middle, as in A, D, ?, J, M. Use the terms on both sides: positions 1, 4, ?, 10, 13 have a constant gap of 3, so the blank is 7 = G. Always confirm the pattern with the terms after the blank, not only those before it.

Shortcuts and tricks

  • Learn EJOTY (5, 10, 15, 20, 25) to convert letters to positions instantly.
  • If gaps are irregular, write the gaps and look at their differences — a second-level pattern is common.
  • When a series has more than six terms, test the alternate-term idea before anything else.
  • Opposite letter = 27 − position. G (7) pairs with T (20).
  • For letter-group series (like ACE, BDF, CEG), compare first letters across groups, then second letters, and so on.

Worked examples

Example 1

A, C, F, J, O, ?

Solution

Positions 1, 3, 6, 10, 15. Gaps: +2, +3, +4, +5. Next gap +6: 15 + 6 = 21 → U.

Example 2

Z, X, U, Q, ?

Solution

Positions 26, 24, 21, 17. Gaps: −2, −3, −4. Next gap −5: 17 − 5 = 12 → L.

Example 3

AZ, BY, CX, ?

Solution

First letters run A, B, C → D. Each second letter is the opposite of the first (positions add to 27). Opposite of D (4) is 23 = W. Answer: DW.

Example 4

D, G, K, P, ?

Solution

Positions 4, 7, 11, 16. Gaps +3, +4, +5. Next +6: 22 → V.

Example 5

C, Z, F, W, I, T, ?

Solution

Split into two series. Odd terms: C, F, I (3, 6, 9), moving +3. Even terms: Z, W, T (26, 23, 20), moving −3. The next term belongs to the odd series: 9 + 3 = 12 → L.

FAQs

What if the series wraps past Z?

Some series continue from A after Z, like X, A, D, G (positions 24, 1, 4, 7 with +3 each, wrapping around). Treat the alphabet as a circle: subtract 26 when a position goes above 26. Such wrap-around questions are less common but do appear in SSC papers.

How are letter series different from coding-decoding?

Both use alphabet positions, but a letter series asks you to continue a pattern, while coding-decoding asks you to apply a rule from one word to another. The same skills — fast positions and spotting gaps — help with both, so practise them together.
