Sentence Connectors and Linkers: Choosing the Right Joiner

Connector questions give two sentences and ask which word or phrase can join them into one meaningful sentence, or which connector fits a blank. IBPS PO, SBI PO and RBI Grade B have used several variants — 'starters', 'connectors' and 'column-matching' — usually as sets of three to five questions. Success depends on two checks: the logical relationship between the sentences (contrast, cause, addition, condition) and the grammar the connector needs (a clause, a noun phrase or an -ing form).

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Contrast

Although, though, even though, whereas, while + clause; despite, in spite of + noun or -ing; however, nevertheless + new sentence. 'Despite the rain, the match continued' but 'Although it rained, the match continued'.

Cause and result

Because, since, as + clause give the reason; because of, due to, owing to + noun. So, therefore, hence and consequently introduce the result. 'Owing to heavy traffic, he was late.'

Addition

Moreover, furthermore, in addition, besides, not only… but also. These add a point in the same direction and should not join sentences that contrast.

Condition and purpose

If, unless, provided that, as long as, in case + clause for conditions; so that, in order to + purpose. 'Unless' already means 'if not', so it should not be followed by a negative.

Sentence starters

Some questions ask which word could begin the combined sentence. 'Despite' at the start needs a noun phrase next ('Despite his efforts…'), 'Having' needs a participle structure ('Having completed the form, she…'), and 'Not only' needs inversion ('Not only did he…').

Meaning must not change

The combined sentence must keep the meaning of both originals. If the second sentence explains the first, a contrast connector distorts the meaning, even if the grammar works.

Shortcuts and tricks

  • First label the relationship: contrast, cause, result, addition or condition. Then pick from that group only.
  • 'Despite of' is always wrong; use 'despite' or 'in spite of'.
  • 'Although… but' is wrong in standard English; use one connector only.
  • 'Due to' is safest after a form of 'be': 'The delay was due to rain.'
  • When the starter is an -ing word, the subject of both clauses must be the same person or thing.
  • 'Not only' at the start forces question order: 'Not only did the bank cut rates, but it also…'

Worked examples

Example 1

Join: 'The company made record sales. It reported a loss.' Choose: Because, Although, Moreover, So.

Solution

Record sales and a loss contrast with each other. 'Although the company made record sales, it reported a loss.' Answer: Although.

Example 2

Join: 'Heavy rain lashed the city. Schools were closed.' Choose: Despite, Owing to, However, Unless.

Solution

Rain is the cause and closure the result. 'Owing to heavy rain, schools in the city were closed.' Answer: Owing to.

Example 3

Starting with 'Despite', combine: 'He prepared well. He could not clear the cut-off.'

Solution

'Despite' needs a noun phrase: 'Despite preparing well, he could not clear the cut-off' or 'Despite his good preparation, he…'.

Example 4

Starting with 'Not only', combine: 'The bank reduced loan rates. It waived processing fees.'

Solution

'Not only did the bank reduce loan rates, but it also waived processing fees.' Inversion ('did the bank reduce') is required after 'Not only'.

Example 5

Join: 'You submit the documents. Your application will not be processed.' Choose: Unless, If, Although, Since.

Solution

The second sentence describes what happens when documents are not submitted: 'Unless you submit the documents, your application will not be processed.' Answer: Unless.

Practice questions

Question 1

Join: 'She was tired. She finished the report.' Choose the best connector.

  1. Because
  2. So
  3. Moreover
  4. Though
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Though

Being tired and still finishing the report show contrast. 'Though she was tired, she finished the report' keeps the meaning.

Question 2

Which starter correctly combines: 'The train was delayed. Many passengers missed their connecting flights.'

  1. Because of the delay of the train
  2. Despite the delay
  3. Although the train
  4. Unless the train
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Because of the delay of the train

The delay caused the missed flights, so a cause connector with a noun phrase fits. 'Because of the delay of the train, many passengers missed…' works.

Question 3

Which sentence is grammatically correct?

  1. Despite of the rain, the event continued.
  2. In spite of the rain, the event continued.
  3. Although it rained, but the event continued.
  4. Despite it rained, the event continued.
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. In spite of the rain, the event continued.

'In spite of' is followed by a noun phrase. 'Despite of' and 'although… but' are errors, and 'despite' cannot take a full clause.

Question 4

Join: 'The scheme is simple. It is free.' Choose the best connector.

  1. However
  2. Although
  3. Moreover
  4. Unless
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Moreover

Both sentences give positive points in the same direction, so an addition connector fits. 'The scheme is simple; moreover, it is free.'

Question 5

Which sentence correctly begins with 'Having'?

  1. Having completed the training, the branch welcomed her.
  2. Having completing the training, she joined.
  3. Having complete the training, she joined the branch.
  4. Having completed the training, she joined the branch.
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Having completed the training, she joined the branch.

The -ing participle clause must share its subject with the main clause and use 'having' plus past participle. Only 'Having completed the training, she joined the branch' meets both conditions.

FAQs

Why is 'Although… but' wrong?

Each connector already links the two clauses. Using both 'although' and 'but' joins the same pair twice. Standard English uses only one: 'Although it rained, the event continued' or 'It rained, but the event continued.' Indian speakers often use both, so exams test it.

What is a dangling participle in starter questions?

It is an -ing or 'having' clause whose subject does not match the main clause. 'Having completed the training, the branch welcomed her' suggests the branch completed the training. Make sure the person doing the action in the opening clause is the subject right after the comma.
