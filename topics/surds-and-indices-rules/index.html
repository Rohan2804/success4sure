Surds and Indices: Laws of Exponents and Simplification

Surds and indices are a regular part of SSC CGL and CHSL (one or two questions), feed into bank-exam simplification blocks, and form the algebraic base for CAT topics like logarithms and exponential equations. The rules are few and mechanical; the exam challenge is applying them without sign errors, especially with negative and fractional exponents, and recognising when to rationalise a denominator.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Laws of indices

aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ; aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ; (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ; (ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ; a⁰ = 1 for a ≠ 0; a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ. These apply only when the bases are the same; convert 8, 16, 32 to powers of 2 and 9, 27, 81 to powers of 3 before combining.

aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ

Fractional powers

a^(p/q) = (q-th root of a)ᵖ. Take the root first to keep numbers small: 27^(2/3) = (∛27)² = 3² = 9; 16^(−3/4) = 1/(⁴√16)³ = 1/8.

a^(p/q) = (ᵠ√a)ᵖ

Simplifying surds

Take out the largest perfect-square factor: √72 = 6√2, √50 = 5√2, √18 = 3√2. Like surds (same number under the root) can then be added or subtracted like algebraic terms: 6√2 + 5√2 − 3√2 = 8√2.

Rationalising

To remove a surd from a denominator, multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate. 1/(√5 − √3) × (√5 + √3)/(√5 + √3) = (√5 + √3)/(5 − 3) = (√5 + √3)/2.

1/(√a − √b) = (√a + √b)/(a − b)

Comparing surds

To compare roots of different orders, raise all of them to the LCM of the orders. To compare ∛4, √3 and ⁶√10, raise to the 6th power: 16, 27 and 10. So √3 is the largest and ⁶√10 the smallest.

Shortcuts and tricks

  • Equal bases: if aˣ = aʸ then x = y — the key step in exponential equations.
  • Powers of 2 to 2¹⁰ and powers of 3 to 3⁶ should be instant: 1,024 and 729.
  • (√a + √b)(√a − √b) = a − b — the basis of rationalisation.
  • A negative base with a fractional power needs care; exam questions avoid it.
  • Square root of a surd sum: √(a + 2√b) = √x + √y where x + y = a and xy = b. For example, √(5 + 2√6) = √3 + √2 because 3 + 2 = 5 and 3 × 2 = 6.
  • When an equation has different bases such as 4ˣ = 8, rewrite both sides with a common base: 2²ˣ = 2³ gives x = 3/2.

Worked examples

Example 1

Simplify 2⁵ × 2⁻³ ÷ 2⁻¹.

Solution

All terms have base 2, so combine the exponents: multiplication adds and division subtracts. 5 + (−3) − (−1) = 5 − 3 + 1 = 3. Answer = 2³ = 8. The double negative in dividing by 2⁻¹ is where most mistakes happen.

Example 2

Find the values of 27^(2/3) and 16^(−3/4).

Solution

27^(2/3) = (∛27)² = 3² = 9. 16^(−3/4) = 1/(⁴√16)³ = 1/2³ = 1/8.

Example 3

Simplify √72 + √50 − √18.

Solution

√72 = 6√2, √50 = 5√2, √18 = 3√2. Total = (6 + 5 − 3)√2 = 8√2.

Example 4

Rationalise 1/(√5 − √3).

Solution

Multiply by (√5 + √3)/(√5 + √3): numerator √5 + √3, denominator 5 − 3 = 2. Answer = (√5 + √3)/2.

Example 5

If 3ˣ⁺² = 243, find x.

Solution

Write 243 as a power of 3: 3⁵ = 243. Since the bases are equal, the exponents must be equal, so x + 2 = 5 and x = 3. Check: 3⁵ = 243.

Example 6

Which is the largest: ∛4, √3 or ⁶√10?

Solution

LCM of 3, 2 and 6 is 6. Raising each to the 6th power gives 4² = 16, 3³ = 27 and 10. The largest is √3.

Practice questions

Question 1

5⁷ ÷ 5⁴ × 5⁻² = ?

  1. 25
  2. 5
  3. 1/5
  4. 125
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. 5

The exponent is 7 − 4 + (−2) = 1, so the value is 5¹ = 5 using the laws of indices.

Question 2

64^(2/3) = ?

  1. 8
  2. 32
  3. 4
  4. 16
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. 16

Take the cube root first: ∛64 = 4, and then square it to get 4² = 16.

Question 3

√98 − √32 = ?

  1. 3√2
  2. √66
  3. 2√3
  4. 11√2
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. 3√2

√98 = 7√2 and √32 = 4√2, so their difference is 3√2; surds cannot be subtracted under one root.

Question 4

If 2^(2x − 1) = 32, then x = ?

  1. 2
  2. 4
  3. 3
  4. 5/2
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. 3

32 = 2⁵, so 2x − 1 = 5 and x = 3 by equating the exponents of the same base.

Question 5

1/(√3 + √2) equals:

  1. √3 − √2
  2. √3 + √2
  3. (√3 − √2)/5
  4. 1/√5
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. √3 − √2

Multiplying by the conjugate gives (√3 − √2)/(3 − 2) = √3 − √2 as the rationalised form.

FAQs

Why is any number to the power 0 equal to 1?

Because aⁿ ÷ aⁿ = 1, and by the division law it also equals aⁿ⁻ⁿ = a⁰. For consistency a⁰ must be 1. The only exception is 0⁰, which is left undefined in school mathematics and does not appear in exams.

When should I rationalise the denominator?

Whenever options are written without surds in the denominator, or when you need to add fractions with surd denominators. Rationalising makes the forms comparable. In comparison questions, squaring both sides is often quicker than rationalising.
