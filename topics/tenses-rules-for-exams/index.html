Tenses in English: Usage Rules and Exam Error Patterns

Tenses decide whether a sentence sounds right in English, and tense errors are among the most tested in SSC CGL, CHSL, MTS, IBPS and SBI papers through error spotting, sentence improvement, fillers and cloze tests. You do not need to recite all twelve tense forms; you need to know which time markers fix which tense, how the tenses of two clauses relate, and a few habits like 'I am knowing' that examiners target repeatedly.

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Simple past versus present perfect

Use the simple past with a finished time (yesterday, last year, in 2020, ago): 'I met him yesterday.' Use the present perfect for past actions connected to now, without a finished time: 'I have met him.' 'I have met him yesterday' is wrong.

Time markers

'Since' and 'for' with perfect tenses; 'just', 'already', 'yet', 'ever' and 'never' usually with present perfect; 'every day', 'usually' and 'always' with simple present; 'at the moment' and 'now' with present continuous; 'by the time' with perfect tenses.

Sequence of tenses

When the main clause is in the past, the subordinate clause usually moves to a past form: 'He said that he was tired', 'I knew that she would come.' Universal truths are an exception: 'He knew that the sun rises in the east.'

Past perfect for the earlier action

When two past actions are described, the earlier one takes the past perfect: 'The train had left before we reached the station.' Using simple past for both is acceptable when 'before' or 'after' makes the order clear, but exams usually expect the past perfect.

Stative verbs

Verbs of state — know, believe, understand, love, hate, own, belong, seem, consist — are not normally used in continuous forms. 'I am understanding' should be 'I understand'.

Future in time clauses

After 'when', 'if', 'as soon as', 'until' and 'before' referring to the future, use the simple present, not 'will': 'I will call you when I reach home', not 'when I will reach'. The present continuous can describe fixed future plans: 'We are meeting the branch manager tomorrow.' This is correct and should not be marked as an error. 'Will' is used for decisions made at the moment or predictions, while 'going to' shows intention or evidence: 'Look at those clouds — it is going to rain.'

Shortcuts and tricks

  • A finished time marker (yesterday, last week, ago) rules out the present perfect.
  • 'Since' + point in time and 'for' + duration both pair with perfect tenses.
  • 'No sooner had… than', 'Hardly had… when' use the past perfect in the first clause.
  • 'It is high time' + simple past: 'It is high time we started.'
  • 'Would' in both clauses of a conditional is wrong: 'If I would know' → 'If I knew'.
  • Check the verb against the subject and the time marker together; most tense errors fail one of the two.

Worked examples

Example 1

Correct the error: 'I have submitted the form yesterday.'

Solution

'Yesterday' is a finished time, so use the simple past: 'I submitted the form yesterday.'

Example 2

Correct the error: 'I will inform you when the results will be declared.'

Solution

In a future time clause, use the simple present: 'I will inform you when the results are declared.'

Example 3

Correct the error: 'She is knowing the answer.'

Solution

'Know' is a stative verb: 'She knows the answer.'

Example 4

Fill in: 'By the time the police arrived, the thieves ______ (escape).'

Solution

The escape happened before the police arrived (both past), so the earlier action takes past perfect: 'had escaped'.

Example 5

Fill in: 'He has been working at the bank ______ 2019.'

Solution

2019 is a point in time, so 'since'. Answer: since.

Example 6

Correct the error: 'No sooner did he finish his speech when the audience started clapping.'

Solution

'No sooner' pairs with 'than', not 'when'. Correct: 'No sooner did he finish his speech than the audience started clapping.' The form 'No sooner had he finished… than' is also correct.

Practice questions

Question 1

Choose the correct form: 'I ______ him two days ago.'

  1. met
  2. have met
  3. had met
  4. have been meeting
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. met

'Two days ago' is a finished time in the past, so the simple past 'met' is correct, not the present perfect.

Question 2

Choose the correct form: 'We will start the meeting as soon as the chairman ______.'

  1. will arrive
  2. arrives
  3. would arrive
  4. has been arriving
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. arrives

After 'as soon as' referring to the future, English uses the simple present. 'As soon as the chairman arrives' is correct.

Question 3

Choose the correct form: 'The film ______ before we reached the theatre.'

  1. has started
  2. starts
  3. had started
  4. is starting
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. had started

The film started before another past action, our arrival. The earlier past action takes the past perfect 'had started'.

Question 4

Choose the correct form: 'This laptop ______ to my brother.'

  1. is belonging
  2. was belonging
  3. has been belonging
  4. belongs
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. belongs

'Belong' is a stative verb and is not normally used in the continuous form. The simple present 'belongs' is correct.

Question 5

Choose the correct form: 'If I ______ you, I would accept the offer.'

  1. were
  2. am
  3. will be
  4. would be
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. were

An unreal present condition uses 'were' for all subjects in the if-clause. 'If I were you, I would accept' is correct.

FAQs

Is 'I have done it yesterday' acceptable in Indian English?

It is common in speech, but standard grammar and exam answer keys treat it as an error. A finished time like 'yesterday' requires the simple past: 'I did it yesterday.' Present perfect is used only when no finished past time is mentioned.

Why is 'when I will reach' wrong?

In English, clauses beginning with 'when', 'if', 'as soon as', 'before' and 'until' use the simple present to talk about the future. The main clause carries the future meaning: 'I will call you when I reach.' Using 'will' in both clauses is a common exam error.
