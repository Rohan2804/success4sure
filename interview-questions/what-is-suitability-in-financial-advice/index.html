How to Decide Product Suitability: Finance Interview

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

The question: “How do you decide which financial product is suitable for a client?”

Why interviewers ask this

Suitability is the backbone of ethical selling in Indian financial services. SEBI's investment adviser rules, AMFI's code of conduct for distributors and IRDAI's guidelines all expect that the product matches the client's needs. Interviewers ask this to see whether you will sell based on what the client needs or on what earns the highest commission. They also check your process: collecting information, risk profiling, understanding goals and time horizon, and matching product features like liquidity, risk and lock-in. A structured answer with a short client example shows you can be trusted with real customers.

How to answer

  1. Say you begin with information: age, income, dependants, existing investments, loans, emergency fund and insurance cover.
  2. Explain risk profiling, covering both risk capacity based on finances and risk tolerance based on attitude, often through a questionnaire.
  3. Link each goal to a time horizon and liquidity need, since money needed in two years cannot go into the same product as retirement money.
  4. Match product features such as risk level, lock-in, costs and tax treatment to the goal, and explain both benefits and risks to the client.
  5. Document the reasoning and review periodically, because a client's situation and suitability change over time.

Sample answers

Fresher (CFP aspirant, wealth management interview)

I would start by understanding the client fully. Take a 30-year-old IT employee earning ₹1 lakh a month, with a home loan and a two-year-old child. Before investing, I would check if he has an emergency fund of a few months' expenses and adequate term and health insurance. Then I would do a risk profile, which looks at his capacity, which is high given his age and income, and his tolerance, which might be moderate if he has never seen a market fall. For his child's education in fifteen years, equity-oriented funds through SIP may be suitable. For a car purchase in two years, a lower-risk debt option fits better. I would explain risks of each, record why I recommended them, and review every year.

Insurance advisor moving to a bank wealth desk

In insurance I learnt suitability the hard way. Early on, I saw colleagues sell high-premium ULIPs to customers who needed only term cover and could not sustain the premium, and many policies lapsed. Now my first question is always what the client needs the money for and when. I use the firm's risk profiling tool, but I also talk, because some clients answer the questionnaire aggressively and then panic in a fall. I prefer simple products the client understands. If a product does not fit, I say so, even if it is the one with a target this month.

Use these as a structure, not a script — replace the placeholders with your own facts.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Jumping straight to products without asking about the client's situation.
  • Treating the risk profile questionnaire as a formality.
  • Recommending one product for all goals.
  • Ignoring insurance and emergency funds before investments.
  • Choosing products based on commission or monthly targets.

  • What is the AMFI risk-o-meter?
  • How is a distributor's duty different from an investment adviser's?

FAQs

What if the client wants a product that is unsuitable?

Explain the risks clearly and in writing. Distributors can execute client-initiated transactions, but should record that it was the client's choice after being informed. An investment adviser must follow SEBI's suitability rules more strictly. Never push the product further.

Is risk profiling mandatory?

SEBI-registered investment advisers must do risk profiling and suitability assessment under SEBI's regulations. Distributors are expected to act in clients' interest under AMFI's code of conduct. Check the latest SEBI and AMFI guidelines for the exact requirements of your role.
