How to Cross-Sell Ethically: Bank and Insurance Interviews

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

The question: “How would you cross-sell to an existing customer without being pushy?”

Why interviewers ask this

Cross-selling insurance, mutual funds, credit cards and loans to existing customers is a major revenue source for banks and NBFCs, and also a major source of complaints. Regulators have repeatedly warned against forced bundling, such as making insurance a condition for a loan when it is not required, or selling insurance to elderly depositors as FDs. Interviewers ask this to check both your selling skill and your ethics. They want to hear a needs-based approach, clear disclosure, respect for the customer's choice and an understanding of the difference between cross-selling and mis-selling.

How to answer

  1. Explain that good cross-selling starts with the customer's life events and needs, such as a new home loan, a new child, or a large maturing deposit.
  2. Show how you would ask questions and listen, instead of offering every product to every customer.
  3. Describe clear disclosure: what the product is, its costs, lock-in and risks, and that it is optional.
  4. Mention specific red lines, such as never tying an optional product to loan approval and never calling insurance a deposit.
  5. Say you would take the customer's written consent, give time to decide, and respect a no.

Sample answers

Fresher (bank PO or sales officer interview)

I would look for a real need first. Suppose a young couple has just taken a home loan from us. A natural question is, 'If something happens to one of you, how will the EMI be paid?' That opens a conversation about term insurance. I would explain the options, the premium and that it is completely optional, and that the loan does not depend on it. If they want time, I would give them the brochure and follow up. I would never tell a customer that the loan will be delayed without insurance, or describe a market-linked plan as a fixed deposit. If they say no, I note it and do not keep pushing.

Experienced (bancassurance specialist interview)

In my last role I saw how cross-selling can go wrong, with senior citizens being sold long-term ULIPs instead of renewing their FDs. That led to complaints and refunds. My approach is to review the customer's profile and ask about their plans before suggesting anything. I disclose charges and lock-ins upfront and confirm their understanding by asking them to explain it back to me. I also make sure the product matches their age and income. My conversion rate is a little lower than some colleagues', but my persistency and complaint record are much better.

Use these as a structure, not a script — replace the placeholders with your own facts.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Saying you would offer all products to every customer.
  • Linking an optional product to loan approval.
  • Describing insurance or market-linked products as deposits.
  • Ignoring the customer's age, income or existing coverage.

Likely follow-up questions

  • What is the difference between cross-selling and mis-selling?
  • What is the free-look period?
  • What is persistency in insurance?
  • What would you say if your manager asks you to bundle insurance with every loan?

FAQs

Is it wrong to cross-sell?

No. Cross-selling is useful when a product genuinely meets a customer's need, and existing customers often prefer dealing with an institution they trust. It becomes mis-selling when the product does not suit the customer or terms are hidden or misrepresented.

What is persistency and why mention it?

Persistency measures how many policies are still being renewed after a period, such as 13 months. Low persistency often signals mis-selling. Mentioning it shows you understand that a sale is only good if the customer keeps the product because it suits them.
