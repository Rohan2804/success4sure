You Notice a Compliance Breach at Work: Interview Answer

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

The question: “You notice a colleague has uploaded client forms with signatures that do not look genuine. What will you do?”

Why interviewers ask this

Forged signatures, fake KYC documents and unauthorised transactions are serious issues in financial services, and they often come to light through colleagues. Interviewers ask this to see whether you will act responsibly or look away to avoid trouble. They also check judgement: you should not accuse anyone on a hunch, you should not gossip, and you should not try to investigate alone. The right answer shows you understand escalation through supervisors, compliance or whistle-blower channels, the importance of documenting what you saw, and protecting clients. Firms want employees who will raise concerns early.

How to answer

  1. Say you would not ignore it, because forged documents can harm clients and expose the firm to regulatory action.
  2. Mention checking what you can within your role, such as comparing with original records, without confronting or accusing the colleague publicly.
  3. Explain that you would report it to your supervisor or the compliance team with factual details, following the firm's policy.
  4. If the supervisor is involved or does not act, use the whistle-blower or vigil mechanism, which offers protection for genuine reports.
  5. Add that you would keep the matter confidential and not discuss it with other colleagues.

Sample answers

Fresher (operations role at an AMC)

First I would make sure I am not mistaken. If I have access to the original signature records as part of my job, I would compare them. If the mismatch is clear, I would not hold the forms for processing and I would report it to my team lead with the specific form numbers and what I noticed. I would not confront the colleague or discuss it with others, because it could be a genuine mistake, or it could be serious, and that is for compliance to decide. If my team lead did not act, I would use the company's whistle-blower policy. Processing a forged form could cause a client to lose money, and that would be on all of us.

Experienced (insurance sales manager)

I would treat it seriously. In insurance, forged signatures on proposal forms can lead to claim disputes and mis-selling complaints later. I would speak to my senior manager and the compliance team, stating facts only: which files, what looked wrong. I would avoid assumptions about the colleague's intention. Sometimes pressure from targets leads people to take shortcuts, and that pressure itself may need to be addressed. But the client's protection comes first, and the firm needs to verify those policies with the clients directly.

Use these as a structure, not a script — replace the placeholders with your own facts.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Ignoring it because it is not your job or to avoid conflict.
  • Confronting the colleague publicly or spreading it in the team.
  • Trying to fix the forms yourself or calling clients without authorisation.
  • Reporting directly to the media or social media.

Likely follow-up questions

  • What if the colleague is your close friend?
  • What if your manager tells you to ignore it?
  • What is a whistle-blower policy?
  • What could happen to the firm if this goes undetected?

FAQs

Will reporting a colleague harm my career?

Reporting factual concerns through proper channels is expected in regulated financial firms, and genuine whistle-blowers are protected under company policies. Ignoring a known breach is far riskier for your career if it is discovered later.

What if I am not sure it is a forgery?

You do not need to be sure. Report what you observed as a concern, not as an accusation. Compliance teams are trained to verify. Your job is to raise it promptly and factually, not to prove it.
