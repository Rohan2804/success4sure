Client's Fund Fell 10%: What to Say (Interview Answer)

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

The question: “A client's mutual fund has fallen 10% in a month and he is panicking. What do you tell him?”

Why interviewers ask this

Market falls test advisers and distributors more than rising markets do. Interviewers ask this to see whether you can stay calm, communicate honestly and keep the client's interest first. They watch for two wrong instincts: promising that the market will recover soon, and pushing the client to 'buy more' without checking his situation. They also want to know if you will review whether the fall is market-wide or fund-specific, revisit the client's goal and time horizon, and respect his final decision. Strong answers show empathy, data, suitability thinking and proper documentation.

How to answer

  1. Begin by listening and acknowledging the worry; a client who feels heard is more open to reasoning.
  2. Check facts: is the fall in line with the benchmark and category, or is the fund underperforming because of something specific?
  3. Revisit the client's goal, time horizon and risk profile, and remind him why this fund was chosen, using the original plan if documented.
  4. Explain options honestly, such as staying invested, continuing SIPs, rebalancing, or exiting if his needs or risk tolerance have changed, without promising recovery.
  5. Let the client make the final decision, record the discussion and his instructions, and follow up after a few weeks.

Sample answers

Fresher (relationship manager role at a wealth firm)

First I would let him talk and acknowledge that seeing a 10 percent fall is stressful. Then I would check whether the whole market or the fund's category has fallen similarly, or whether this fund has done much worse than its benchmark. If it is a market-wide fall, I would remind him that he invested for [goal] which is [number] years away, and that equity funds go through such phases. I would show him the fund's history, including past falls, but I would not promise that it will recover by a certain date. If his need for the money has come closer, or he realises he cannot handle this volatility, we should consider moving part of it to a lower-risk option. Whatever he decides, I would note it and follow up.

Mutual fund distributor (interview at a broking firm)

In the 2020 fall I had several such calls. What worked was preparation: I had each client's goal and risk profile documented, so I could say, 'You invested for your retirement in fifteen years, and we agreed you could handle short-term falls.' I showed them that their SIPs were now buying more units. For one client who had wrongly invested emergency money in an equity fund, I actually recommended moving part of it to a liquid fund, because suitability had been wrong from the start. I learnt that calming clients is not about predictions; it is about reconnecting them to their plan.

Use these as a structure, not a script — replace the placeholders with your own facts.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Promising the market will bounce back soon.
  • Telling every client to invest more without checking liquidity needs.
  • Dismissing the client's fear as foolish.
  • Ignoring the possibility that the fund itself is underperforming.
  • Not documenting the advice or the client's decision.

Likely follow-up questions

  • What if the client wants to redeem everything anyway?
  • How do you know if a fund is underperforming or just in a market fall?
  • What is portfolio rebalancing?
  • How often should you review a client's portfolio?

FAQs

Should I advise the client to stop his SIP?

It depends on his situation. If his goal and income are unchanged, continuing usually fits the original plan. If his cash flow has changed, pausing may be reasonable. Avoid blanket advice; base the discussion on his needs and remember distributors and advisers have different regulatory roles.

Is it okay to compare with the 2008 or 2020 crashes?

Yes, as context, showing that markets have fallen sharply before. But add that past recoveries do not guarantee a future one and that timeframes varied. Use data from reliable sources, not memory.
