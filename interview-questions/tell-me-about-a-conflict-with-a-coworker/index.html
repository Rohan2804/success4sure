Describe a Conflict With a Coworker: Interview Answer

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

The question: “Tell me about a conflict you had with a colleague.”

Why interviewers ask this

Workplaces in banking and finance involve constant coordination between sales, operations, credit and compliance teams, and friction is normal. The interviewer is checking whether you handle disagreements maturely: whether you address problems directly but respectfully, look for solutions instead of winners, and protect working relationships. They also listen for how you describe the other person. Candidates who make their colleague the villain, or who claim they never have conflicts, both raise doubts. A balanced story with a constructive resolution shows emotional maturity.

How to answer

  1. Pick a professional disagreement about work, such as priorities, process or workload, not a personal fight.
  2. Describe the other person's view fairly. Show that you tried to understand their side.
  3. Explain the specific steps you took: a private conversation, finding facts, proposing a compromise, or involving a manager only when needed.
  4. Share the outcome and how the working relationship was afterwards.

Sample answers

Experienced (sales vs operations at an NBFC)

In my current role, I am in sales, and I often needed the operations team to process loan files quickly at month-end. One operations executive kept returning my files for small document issues, and I felt she was being too strict. I was frustrated, but instead of complaining to my manager, I sat with her for half an hour to understand her checklist. I realised most of the returns were for issues I could have caught myself. I made a pre-submission checklist for my team, and our return rate dropped. We now coordinate much better, and she often flags issues to me informally before rejecting a file.

Fresher (college project group)

In our final-year project, one group member wanted to change the topic halfway through because he found a more interesting dataset. I disagreed because we had already spent six weeks on the current topic. The discussion was getting heated in our group chat, so I suggested we meet in person. We listed what each option would require in time and effort. We agreed to keep our topic but use part of his dataset as an extension. It made the project stronger and we finished on time.

Experienced (shared workload in accounts)

A colleague and I disagreed about who should handle vendor reconciliations after a team restructure. Instead of letting it drag on, I suggested we both list our current tasks and take it to our manager together. The manager split it by vendor category, and the confusion ended within a day.

Use these as a structure, not a script — replace the placeholders with your own facts.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Describing the colleague as lazy, rude or incompetent.
  • Saying you have never had a conflict.
  • Telling a story where you escalated immediately without trying to resolve it yourself.
  • Choosing a story where the conflict was never resolved and you learnt nothing.

Likely follow-up questions

  • What would you do if the colleague did not cooperate?
  • Have you ever had a conflict with a manager?
  • How do you handle someone you dislike personally?

FAQs

Should I choose a conflict where I was wrong?

It can work well if you show how you realised it and what you changed. Stories where you were partly wrong are often more believable than ones where you were entirely right. Just make sure the mistake was minor and that the ending shows maturity.

What if I have only college examples?

College examples are fine for freshers. Group projects, fest committees, hostel committees and sports teams all produce real disagreements. Use the same structure: the issue, both views, what you did and the outcome.
