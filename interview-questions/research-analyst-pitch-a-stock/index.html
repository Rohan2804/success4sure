Pitch Me a Stock: Research Analyst Interview Framework

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

The question: “Pitch me a stock.”

Why interviewers ask this

Equity research, buy-side and PMS interviews frequently ask candidates to pitch a stock. The panel is not looking for the 'right' stock. They want to see your thinking process: can you explain the business simply, identify what drives earnings, use financial data properly, value the company, and, critically, name the risks that could make you wrong. They will probe your numbers, so they also test your honesty about what you know. A disciplined two-minute structure stands out against the common answer of listing reasons a stock went up recently.

How to answer

  1. Open with a one-line thesis: the company, your view, and the single main reason, for example market share gains in a growing segment.
  2. Explain the business model briefly: what it sells, to whom, how it earns, and what gives it an edge over competitors.
  3. Support with key numbers you have checked from annual reports, such as revenue growth, margins, return on capital and debt levels.
  4. Discuss valuation compared to peers or history, explaining why the current price may not reflect your view.
  5. End with the key risks and what would make you change your mind, plus any catalysts you are watching.

Sample answers

Fresher (MBA Finance, research analyst trainee interview, framework answer)

I would like to pitch [Company], a [sector] company. My view is positive over two to three years because [main thesis, for example rising share in a fast-growing segment]. The company earns mainly from [products or services], sold to [customers], and its edge is [brand, distribution or cost advantage]. Over the last [period], revenue has grown at about [x] percent and operating margins have been around [y] percent, based on the annual reports. It has [low or manageable] debt. On valuation, it trades at about [multiple] compared with peers at [range]; I think that gap is not justified if [thesis] plays out. The main risks are [risk one] and [risk two]. If [specific metric] weakens for two quarters, I would revisit my view.

Experienced (credit analyst moving to equity research)

Coming from credit, I tend to start with the balance sheet. The stock I am tracking is [Company] in [sector]. What I like is that it has [deleveraged steadily and now generates free cash flow], which gives it room to [invest or pay dividends]. I built a simple model using five years of financials; my base case assumes [growth assumption]. Valuation looks [reasonable or cheap] on EV to EBITDA relative to peers. But I am cautious about [specific risk such as raw material prices or regulatory change], and the stock has already [moved recently], so I see this as a watchlist idea rather than a strong conviction call.

Use these as a structure, not a script — replace the placeholders with your own facts.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Pitching a stock only because it has gone up a lot recently.
  • Quoting numbers you have not verified and cannot defend.
  • Ignoring risks or saying there are none.
  • Choosing a very obscure company you cannot explain, or a huge one with nothing new to say.
  • Presenting the pitch as investment advice or a sure thing.

Likely follow-up questions

  • What is the biggest risk to your thesis?
  • Why has the market not priced this in already?
  • How did you value it, and what are your key assumptions?
  • What would make you sell?
  • Pitch me a stock you would short.

FAQs

Should I pick a famous large-cap stock?

A well-known company is fine if you have a specific, well-researched angle. Mid-sized companies can also work if you know them deeply. Avoid tiny, illiquid stocks or companies you cannot explain in plain words. The quality of reasoning matters more than the name.

How many numbers should I memorise?

Know five to eight key figures well: revenue growth, margins, return on capital, debt, valuation multiple and peer comparison. Be ready to explain where they came from. If asked something you do not know, say so rather than guessing.
