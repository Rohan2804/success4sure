Mutual Fund or FD? How to Answer a Client in an Interview

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

The question: “A client asks: should I put my money in a mutual fund or a fixed deposit?”

Why interviewers ask this

This is one of the most common real client questions at bank branches and distributor offices, so interviewers use it as a role-play. They are checking product knowledge, balance and honesty. A biased answer, such as 'FDs are useless, go for mutual funds', or the opposite, suggests you will sell rather than advise. The panel wants you to compare features fairly: safety, return variability, liquidity, taxation and suitability for different goals. They also want you to say 'it depends' and then explain what it depends on, with a practical example, rather than leaving it vague.

How to answer

  1. Avoid giving a one-word answer; say the right choice depends on the client's goal, time horizon and comfort with fluctuations.
  2. Explain the FD side: fixed interest, high safety, predictability, deposit insurance up to the DICGC limit per depositor per bank, but returns may barely beat inflation after tax.
  3. Explain the mutual fund side: many categories from liquid to equity, market-linked returns, no fixed return, potential for higher long-term growth with volatility.
  4. Mention taxation and liquidity in general terms, noting that tax rules change and should be checked with current income tax rules or a tax professional.
  5. Close with a practical example of how both could be used together for different goals.

Sample answers

Fresher (bank sales officer interview)

I would tell the client, 'Both are useful, but for different purposes.' If he needs the money in a year, for example for a wedding, an FD is better because the amount is predictable and the bank pays a fixed rate. Deposits are also covered by DICGC insurance up to a limit per bank. If the goal is five or ten years away, like retirement or a child's education, mutual funds, especially equity-oriented ones, have the potential to grow faster than inflation, but the value can go up and down and there is no fixed return. I would also mention that tax treatment is different for both, and he should check current rules. Many people keep their emergency money and near-term needs in FDs and long-term savings in mutual funds.

Experienced MF distributor (wealth firm interview)

I never answer this without knowing the client's situation. For a retired client who depends on interest income, I might keep a large share in FDs and government-backed schemes, and perhaps add a conservative hybrid or debt fund for diversification. For a young salaried client with surplus income, I would suggest building an emergency fund first, then starting equity SIPs for long-term goals. I explain that mutual funds are not 'better' FDs; they are different products with different risks. I also show them that the post-tax, post-inflation return is what matters.

Use these as a structure, not a script — replace the placeholders with your own facts.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Declaring one option always better than the other.
  • Saying mutual funds 'always beat FDs in the long run'.
  • Quoting current FD rates or tax rates without checking them.
  • Forgetting liquidity and emergency needs.

Likely follow-up questions

  • What is DICGC insurance?
  • What is a debt fund and how is it different from an FD?
  • How would you advise a senior citizen depending on interest income?
  • What is real return?

FAQs

What is the DICGC insurance limit?

DICGC insures bank deposits up to a set limit per depositor per bank, covering principal and interest together. As of 2026 the limit is ₹5 lakh; check the DICGC website for the current figure, since it can be revised.

Should I bring up tax in this answer?

Briefly, yes, because tax affects real returns. But tax rules on interest and capital gains have changed several times, so describe the concept and suggest checking current rules or a tax professional, rather than quoting rates in an interview.
