Client Wants High Returns With No Risk: How to Respond

Last updated 19 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

The question: “A client says he wants high returns with no risk. How will you respond?”

Why interviewers ask this

Every adviser meets this client. The question checks whether you understand the basic risk and return trade-off and whether you will be honest even when it may cost a sale. Interviewers look for red flags: candidates who say 'I will show him our best product' or who hint that some scheme gives high returns safely. They also want to see communication skill, educating without talking down, and the ability to find a sensible middle path such as a diversified portfolio aligned to the client's goals. Remember that many frauds, including Ponzi schemes, target exactly this desire.

How to answer

  1. Acknowledge the wish politely; everyone wants high returns with no risk, so avoid making the client feel foolish.
  2. Explain the trade-off simply, with an example comparing a bank FD, a debt fund and an equity fund over different periods.
  3. Warn gently that any product promising high returns with no risk is a red flag for fraud or mis-selling.
  4. Bring the conversation back to goals and time horizon, and suggest a mix that balances safety and growth.
  5. Commit to transparency: show the risks in writing and let the client choose with full information.

Sample answers

Fresher (bank wealth desk interview)

I would say, 'Sir, I completely understand. Everyone wants that, but in investing, higher returns usually come with higher risk.' Then I would explain with a simple example. A fixed deposit gives a fixed rate with very low risk, but the return may barely beat inflation after tax. Equity funds have historically offered higher growth over long periods, but they can fall 20 or 30 percent in a bad year. I would also mention that if anyone offers him high returns with no risk, he should be careful, because that is how many frauds work. Then I would ask what he is saving for and when. Based on that, we can create a mix, for example some money in safer options and some in growth options, so he gets a balance.

Insurance and mutual fund advisor (interview at an NBFC wealth arm)

I hear this almost every week, especially from retired clients after a deposit rate cut. I first respect their need, because for them safety is important. I show them a simple chart of different asset classes and their best and worst years. Then I ask how much of their money they can leave untouched for five years or more. Usually we arrive at a plan where most money stays in low-risk options and a small part goes to growth assets. I always tell them which part can fall and by roughly how much in bad times, so there are no surprises later.

Use these as a structure, not a script — replace the placeholders with your own facts.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Saying you would recommend a product that offers both, implying such a product exists.
  • Talking down to the client or calling the request unrealistic in a rude way.
  • Overloading the client with technical terms like standard deviation and Sharpe ratio.
  • Pushing a high-risk product by focusing only on past returns.

Likely follow-up questions

  • What would you say if a client mentions a friend earning 5 percent a month in a scheme?
  • What is the risk-return trade-off?
  • How would you explain inflation risk to a retired person?
  • Which low-risk options are available for senior citizens?

FAQs

How should I respond if a client mentions an 'assured high-return' scheme?

Advise caution without accusing anyone. Suggest checking whether the entity is registered with SEBI, RBI or IRDAI, and point out that very high fixed promises are a common sign of Ponzi or unregulated schemes. Refer to the regulators' investor awareness material.

Can I mention small savings schemes?

Yes, schemes like the Senior Citizens' Savings Scheme or PPF are government-backed options many clients value. Interest rates are revised periodically by the government, so say rates should be checked on the official website rather than quoting a number.
