Questioning Financial Claims and Market "Tips"

Evaluate return claims, track records and market tips with healthy scepticism, and help clients do the same. You will learn to spot red flags, survivorship bias and misuse of past performance, and to check whether an adviser is registered.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

If it sounds too good, test it

Clients regularly bring you messages like “Guaranteed 3% a month in this trading scheme” or “This stock will double by Diwali.” Your first job is to slow the conversation down and test the claim with them, not to argue.

Put the number in context. 3% a month compounds to over 40% a year, far above what diversified equity has delivered over long periods, and the claim says nothing about risk. The rule of 72 is a quick check: divide 72 by the yearly return to estimate how many years it takes money to double. At 12% a year that is about six years; a promise to double money in one year implies roughly 100% a year.

Watch for the classic red flags: guaranteed high returns on market-linked products, pressure to invest quickly, payment to personal accounts, returns paid out of new investors' money and reluctance to share documents. Higher potential return always comes with higher risk; anyone who denies that is selling something.

Do
  • Convert monthly claims into yearly figures
  • Use the rule of 72 as a quick sense check
Don't
  • Dismiss the client's interest rudely
  • Accept “guaranteed” on a market-linked product

Survivorship bias and selective track records

Track records can be quietly improved by what is left out. Survivorship bias happens when you only see the winners because the losers have disappeared. If a fund house launched 20 schemes a decade ago and later merged or closed 8 weak ones, the average return of the 12 survivors will look better than what investors actually experienced across all 20.

The same bias powers tip-sellers on Telegram and social media. Someone who sends opposite calls to different groups will be right for some of them by pure chance, and will then advertise only the winning screenshots. A wall of profit screenshots tells you nothing about the losing trades that were never shown.

When you review any performance claim, ask: What is the full record, including closed products or deleted calls? Who verified it? Over what period, and was that period chosen to look good?

Do
  • Ask for the complete record, not highlights
  • Check how the performance period was chosen
Don't
  • Treat profit screenshots as evidence

Past performance is not a promise

Last year's top-performing fund is often not next year's. Strong recent returns may come from a sector rally, a concentrated bet that happened to pay off or simple market timing. Use past performance to understand a fund's behaviour, such as its consistency across market cycles, its falls in bad years and how it compares with its benchmark and category, not to forecast returns.

With clients, make this concrete. Instead of “This fund gave 35% last year,” say: “It did well last year, mainly because mid-caps rallied. Over five years it has been close to its category average, and in a bad year it can fall sharply. Does that fit your goal and your comfort with risk?” This keeps suitability, not recent returns, at the centre.

Never say or imply that any return is assured. Be open about what you earn, whether commission or fee, so the client can weigh your recommendation fairly.

Do
  • Compare returns over several periods and against the benchmark
  • Disclose your commission or fee
Don't
  • Sell on one year's top return
  • Imply that past returns will repeat

Verify who is giving the advice

Before trusting any adviser, tip provider or platform, check whether it is registered with the relevant regulator. In India, SEBI-registered investment advisers, research analysts and other intermediaries can be looked up on SEBI's website, and mutual fund distributors hold an ARN that can be checked on AMFI's website. Unregistered “advisory” groups selling paid tips are a common source of losses.

Encourage clients to check the exact name and registration number, not just a logo or a certificate image in a chat, because these are easy to copy. Payments should go to the registered entity's official account, never to a personal UPI ID belonging to a “mentor”.

If a client has already been drawn into a doubtful scheme, stay non-judgemental. Help them stop further payments, gather their records and use the official complaint channels of the regulator and the platform involved. Clients remember the adviser who helped them out of a mess.

Do
  • Verify registration on the regulator's official website
  • Help clients use official complaint channels
Don't
  • Trust a certificate image shared in a chat
  • Shame a client who has been misled

Key takeaways

  • Convert return claims into yearly figures and sense-check them.
  • Treat “guaranteed” high returns on market products as a red flag.
  • Ask for the full track record to avoid survivorship bias.
  • Use past performance to understand risk, never to promise returns.
  • Verify registration on the regulator's website before trusting any adviser.

Quick check

A scheme promises 3% a month, guaranteed. Roughly what yearly return is that, compounded?

  1. About 12%
  2. About 30%
  3. Over 40%
  4. About 3%
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Over 40%

1.03 multiplied by itself 12 times is about 1.43, so over 40% a year: a major red flag.
