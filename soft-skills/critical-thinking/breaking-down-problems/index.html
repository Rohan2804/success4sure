Breaking Down Big Problems

Turn a vague, overwhelming problem into a clear question and smaller parts you can actually work on. You will learn to define the problem, split it with an issue tree, find the root cause and choose where to start.

Last updated 5 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Define the problem before solving it

Most big problems feel big because they are vague. “My preparation is not going well” or “Sales are bad this quarter” cannot be solved as stated, because there is nothing specific to act on. The first step is to rewrite the problem as a clear, measurable question.

A useful problem statement says what is happening, by how much, since when and what a good outcome looks like. Compare “Sales are bad” with “New savings accounts at our Pune branch fell from 120 a month to 80 between July and September; we want to get back to 110 by December.” The second version tells you what to measure and how you will know you have succeeded.

Also separate the problem from your favourite solution. “We need more ads” is a solution dressed up as a problem. Ask “What exactly is going wrong, and how do we know?” before anyone proposes a fix.

Do
  • Write the problem as one sentence with a number and a date
  • Agree on what success looks like before starting
Don't
  • Start with a solution disguised as a problem
  • Accept words like “bad”, “slow” or “low” without a measure

Split it into smaller pieces with an issue tree

Once the problem is clear, break it into parts. An issue tree is simply the main question at the top with branches below it, each branch a smaller question. For the Pune branch, fewer new accounts could come from fewer people walking in, fewer walk-ins being converted, or more applications getting stuck in processing.

Try to make branches that do not overlap and that together cover all the possibilities. Consultants call this MECE: mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive. You do not need perfection; the aim is to avoid counting the same cause twice and to avoid missing a whole area.

The same method works for personal problems. Rohit keeps missing the cut-off in bank PO prelims. His tree splits his score into English, Quantitative Aptitude and Reasoning, and each section into accuracy and number of questions attempted. After reviewing three mock tests this way, he sees that his real gap is attempting too few Quant questions, not English.

Do
  • Split with a simple formula where you can, such as walk-ins × conversion
  • Check that your branches do not overlap
Don't
  • Brainstorm 20 random causes with no structure

Dig for the root cause

A branch tells you where the problem is; it does not always tell you why. To get from symptom to cause, keep asking “Why?” until you reach something you can act on. This is often called the “5 Whys”, though the number is a guide, not a rule.

Example: applications are stuck in processing. Why? Many are sent back for missing documents. Why? Customers do not know that their address proof must be recent. Why? The checklist handed out at the counter is an old version. Now the fix is obvious and cheap: print the updated checklist.

Check each “why” against evidence. If you cannot show that applications are actually being sent back, you are guessing, and a guess can send the whole team in the wrong direction. Data from the branch system, five customer calls or a week of simple counting can confirm or rule out an idea quickly.

Do
  • Back each “why” with a fact, not an assumption
Don't
  • Stop at the first convenient answer
  • Blame a person when the process is the real cause

Prioritise and take the first step

You will usually find several causes, and you cannot fix them all at once. Rate each possible fix on impact (how much of the problem it solves) and effort (time, money, approvals). Do the high-impact, low-effort fixes now; plan the high-impact, high-effort ones; and park the low-impact ones for later.

Then write down one next action that someone can start this week, with an owner and a date. “Improve the process” is not an action. “Meena replaces the counter checklist by Friday and tracks returned applications for two weeks” is.

Finally, check whether the fix worked by going back to the number in your problem statement. If new accounts have not recovered after a month, return to your tree; the root cause may sit in another branch.

Do
  • Give every action an owner and a date
  • Measure the original number again after the fix
Don't
  • Try to fix everything at the same time

Key takeaways

  • Rewrite a vague problem as one specific, measurable question.
  • Break the problem into non-overlapping parts with an issue tree.
  • Ask “why” repeatedly, and check each answer against evidence.
  • Rank fixes by impact and effort, then start with one owned action.
  • Measure again to confirm the fix actually worked.

Quick check

Which is the most useful problem statement?

  1. “Our team's performance has been poor for months and everyone in management agrees something must change.”
  2. “Weekly complaints at the Nagpur centre rose from 40 to 90 since August; target: under 50 by November.”
  3. “We need to hire more call centre staff immediately to handle all the complaints.”
  4. “Customers seem quite unhappy lately, and senior management is worried about the brand.”
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. “Weekly complaints at the Nagpur centre rose from 40 to 90 since August; target: under 50 by November.”

It states what changed, by how much, since when and what success looks like.
