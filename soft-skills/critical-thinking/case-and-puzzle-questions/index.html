Solving Case and Puzzle Questions in Interviews

Approach guesstimates, business cases and puzzles in interviews with a clear structure and calm, out-loud reasoning. You will learn to clarify the question, break it down, calculate simply and sanity-check your answer.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

What interviewers are really testing

Case and puzzle questions appear in campus placements for consulting, analytics, banking and product roles, and sometimes in management trainee interviews. “How many cups of tea are sold in a college canteen each day?” has no exact answer, and the interviewer knows it. They want to see how you think: whether you structure the problem, make sensible assumptions, calculate carefully and stay calm.

So think out loud. A silent minute followed by “around 1,000” tells the interviewer nothing. Walking through your steps lets them follow you, nudge you if you drift and give credit for a sound method even if your final number differs from theirs.

Start every question by restating it and asking one or two clarifying questions: “Do you mean a single canteen on a typical working day? Should I include staff?” This shows care and narrows the problem.

Do
  • Restate the question and clarify its scope
  • Think out loud at every step
Don't
  • Jump straight to a number
  • Go silent for long stretches

Structuring a guesstimate

Break the estimate into a chain of simple factors you can reasonably guess. For the canteen question: the college has 2,000 students. On a typical day about 60% are on campus, which is 1,200. Perhaps 40% of them buy tea, which is 480 students, and they average 1.5 cups each, giving 720 cups. Add 150 staff drinking about 2 cups each, which is 300 cups. The total is roughly 1,020 cups, so you might say “about 1,000 a day”.

State each assumption clearly and use round numbers so the arithmetic stays easy under pressure. If the interviewer says, “Assume 3,000 students instead,” you can update one step rather than starting over.

Finish with a sanity check. Does 1,000 cups a day seem possible for one canteen counter? If tea is served for about eight hours, that is around two cups a minute: busy, but believable.

Do
  • Use round numbers and state every assumption
  • Sanity-check the final figure against real life
Don't
  • Insist your assumption is the only right one

Cracking business case questions

In a business case, you are given a situation such as “A café chain's profits have fallen. Why?” Use a simple framework as your starting tree. Profit is revenue minus costs, and revenue is price multiplied by the number of units sold. Ask for data on each branch before guessing causes.

Suppose the interviewer tells you a café's monthly profit fell from ₹50,000 to ₹30,000 while revenue stayed at ₹2,00,000. Then costs must have risen from ₹1,50,000 to ₹1,70,000, an increase of ₹20,000. Next you ask which cost rose: rent, staff, ingredients or electricity? If rent went up by ₹20,000, you have explained the whole gap, and you can discuss options such as renegotiating, relocating or raising prices on popular items.

End with a short summary and a recommendation: “The fall is entirely due to a ₹20,000 rent increase. I'd first try to renegotiate the lease, and meanwhile test a small price increase on coffee.”

Do
  • Start from profit = revenue − costs
  • Close with a clear recommendation
Don't
  • List causes before checking the numbers

Logic puzzles and staying calm

Pure puzzles test structured reasoning. A classic: you have 8 identical-looking coins, one slightly heavier, and a balance scale. What is the fewest number of weighings that always finds the heavy coin? Instead of weighing 4 against 4, split the coins into groups of 3, 3 and 2. Weigh 3 against 3. If they balance, the heavy coin is one of the 2; weigh them against each other. If not, take the heavier 3 and weigh 1 against 1; if those balance, it is the third coin. Either way, two weighings are enough.

The lesson is to think about how each step splits the possibilities, not just the obvious first move. If you are stuck, say so and try a smaller version: “Let me think about it with 3 coins first.”

Practise with a friend, giving yourself 5–10 minutes per question. Speaking your reasoning aloud during practice is what makes it feel natural in the real interview.

Do
  • Try a smaller version when stuck
  • Practise aloud with a timer
Don't
  • Panic or give up when your first idea fails

Key takeaways

  • Clarify the question before you start solving.
  • Think out loud so the interviewer can follow your method.
  • Break guesstimates into simple factors with round numbers.
  • Use profit = revenue − costs to structure business cases.
  • Sanity-check your answer and end with a clear recommendation.

Quick check

An interviewer asks how many ATMs there are in your city. What should you do first?

  1. Clarify the scope, such as whether to include all banks
  2. Say you don't know because you have never counted
  3. Give a number immediately to look confident
  4. Ask the interviewer for the exact answer
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Clarify the scope, such as whether to include all banks

Clarifying scope shows care and keeps your estimate focused.
