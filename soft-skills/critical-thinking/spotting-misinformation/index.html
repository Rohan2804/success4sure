Spotting Fake News, Bad Data and Misleading Charts

Check forwards, statistics and charts before you believe or share them. You will learn quick verification habits and how to spot cherry-picked numbers and misleading graphs.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Pause before you forward

Misinformation spreads because it is designed to make you feel something, such as fear, anger, hope or excitement, and act before you think. A forward claiming “Your bank account will be frozen on Monday unless you update KYC through this link” or “This kitchen spice cures diabetes in 7 days” works on emotion, not evidence.

Build the habit of a ten-second pause. Ask: Who is the original source? Is it named and checkable? Does the message push me to act or share immediately? Is it asking for money, an OTP or a click on an unknown link? A yes to either of the last two is a strong warning sign.

WhatsApp marks messages that have been forwarded many times. That label tells you the message has travelled far from its original sender, not that it is true. Forwarding something “just in case” still spreads it.

Do
  • Pause and look for the original, named source
  • Treat urgency, unknown links and OTP requests as red flags
Don't
  • Forward a message “just in case it's true”

Verify with official and independent sources

For anything about exams, jobs or money, go straight to the official source. A viral PDF saying an exam has been postponed, or a job offer asking for a ₹5,000 “registration fee”, should be checked on the organisation's own website or by calling a number you looked up yourself, not the number in the message.

For news, see whether several established, independent outlets are reporting the same thing. If only one unknown website or a single screenshot carries the claim, wait. Screenshots of “news” are easy to fake, and real headlines are sometimes paired with old photos from a different event.

For images, a reverse image search, available in common search engines, often shows whether a photo is old or from another place. Check dates carefully: many forwards recycle a true story from years ago as if it happened today.

Do
  • Check exam and job news on the official website
  • Look up contact numbers yourself
Don't
  • Trust a screenshot as proof
  • Pay a “fee” to secure a job offer

Question the numbers

Numbers feel objective, but they can mislead. Cherry-picking means choosing only the data that supports a point. A stock “up 60% since March” may simply be measured from its lowest point of the year; over three years it might be flat. Always ask: compared with what, and over what period?

Percentages without base numbers are another trap. “Complaints doubled” sounds alarming, but if they went from 3 to 6 at a branch serving 10,000 customers, it is a small change. Ask for the actual counts.

Also check who was measured. “9 out of 10 users recommend this app” means little if only 10 people were asked, or only happy paying customers. And remember that two things rising together does not mean one causes the other: ice-cream sales and drowning cases both rise in summer because of the hot weather, not because of each other.

Do
  • Ask “compared with what, and since when?”
  • Ask for the counts behind every percentage
Don't
  • Assume that things moving together cause each other

Read charts carefully

Charts can be accurate and still mislead. The most common trick is a vertical axis that does not start at zero. If one bar shows 96% customer satisfaction and another 98%, starting the axis at 95 makes the second bar look three times taller, though the real gap is just two points.

Other warning signs include uneven time gaps on the horizontal axis (2015, 2020, 2022, 2023 spaced equally), two lines drawn on different scales so they appear to move together, missing labels or units, and 3D pie charts where the slice nearest you looks bigger than it is.

Whenever you see a dramatic chart, read the axis labels and numbers first, then the picture. Ask what the chart would look like if it were drawn fairly. If you build charts at work, start bar charts at zero and label everything; your credibility depends on it.

Do
  • Read the axes and numbers before the shape
  • Start bar charts at zero when you make them
Don't
  • Judge a trend by how steep the line looks

Key takeaways

  • Pause before forwarding and find the original, named source.
  • Treat urgency, fees, unknown links and OTP requests as red flags.
  • Verify exam, job and money news on official websites.
  • Ask “compared with what?” and for the counts behind every percentage.
  • Read a chart's axes and labels before trusting its shape.

Quick check

A forward says your bank account will be blocked today unless you update KYC via a link. What should you do first?

  1. Click the link quickly to avoid the block
  2. Contact the bank through its official app or a number you look up
  3. Forward it to your family group so everyone stays safe in time
  4. Reply to the sender asking whether it is genuine
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Contact the bank through its official app or a number you look up

Official channels you find yourself are the safe way to check; the link may be a phishing trap.
