About this Critical Thinking & Problem Solving course

This critical thinking course online gives you practical tools for thinking clearly when problems are messy and information is incomplete. You will turn vague problems into clear questions with an issue tree, ask sharper questions at work and check forwards, statistics and charts before you share them. The course builds real problem solving skills, from finding root causes to making decisions under uncertainty. If you want to know how to improve critical thinking for interviews, there is a lesson on guesstimates and case questions, plus one on questioning financial claims and market tips.

What you will learn

  • Define a vague problem clearly and split it using an issue tree
  • Use open and closed questions on purpose and clarify tasks before starting
  • Check viral forwards, statistics and charts for cherry-picked or misleading information
  • Make sound decisions with incomplete information by matching effort to the stakes
  • Structure guesstimates, business cases and puzzles calmly in placement interviews
  • Spot survivorship bias, misuse of past performance and unregistered advisers

Who this course is for

  • Students preparing for case, guesstimate and puzzle interview rounds
  • Freshers who feel stuck on large or unclear tasks
  • Professionals who must decide quickly without complete data
  • Finance professionals helping clients judge return claims and tips

How can I improve my critical thinking skills?

Practise defining problems before solving them, asking why until you reach a root cause, and checking sources before believing a claim. Think in scenarios rather than certainties, and review decisions by their reasoning, not only their results. Applying these habits to everyday tasks at work builds the skill faster than puzzles alone.

What is an issue tree in problem solving?

An issue tree breaks one big question into smaller parts that together cover the whole problem without overlapping. For example, "Why are sales down?" splits into fewer customers or lower spend per customer, and each branch splits further. It helps you see where to look first instead of guessing.

How do I solve guesstimate questions in interviews?

Clarify the question and any assumptions, break the estimate into logical parts such as population, share and frequency, round numbers to keep maths simple and talk through your reasoning out loud. Finish with a sanity check. Interviewers care more about your structure and calm than the exact figure.

How can I tell if a news forward or chart is misleading?

Pause before forwarding and check whether official or independent sources report the same thing. Look at who made the claim, the date and the original data. For charts, check where the axis starts, the time period chosen and whether key numbers are missing or cherry-picked.
