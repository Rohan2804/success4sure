Storytelling on Social Media

Write short posts on LinkedIn and similar platforms that people read to the end. You will learn to open with a strong first line, tell one small story honestly and protect other people's privacy.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

The first line decides everything

On LinkedIn, Instagram or X, people scroll fast. Most posts show only the first line or two before a "see more" link, so that opening has one job: make the right reader want to continue.

A strong hook is specific and promises something. "I'm happy to share that I completed a course" is a statement many people make every day. "I failed my first mock interview because I couldn't explain my own project" is a moment — the reader wants to know what happened next.

Hooks can be a surprising result, a mistake, a question or a small scene: "At 9:40 pm, the client called to cancel." Avoid tricks such as fake cliffhangers ("You won't believe what my boss said...") followed by an ordinary post. Readers notice, and it costs you trust.

Do
  • Open with a specific moment, result or mistake
Don't
  • Use clickbait openings the post doesn't deliver on

One post, one small story

The three-part story works well in posts: a quick situation, a challenge and what you learnt. Keep it to one idea. A post that covers your internship, your hobbies and your views on AI will be remembered for none of them.

Short lines help on a phone. Write in two- or three-line paragraphs and leave a blank line between them. Lists can work for practical tips, but a post made only of bullets often loses the story.

End with your lesson or a genuine question — "How do you prepare for mock interviews?" — not with a demand like "Comment YES if you agree!" Ask only if you plan to read and reply to the answers. Three to five relevant hashtags are plenty; a block of twenty looks like spam.

Do
  • Keep one idea per post
  • Use short paragraphs with space between them
Don't
  • Pack several unrelated stories into one post
  • End with engagement bait like "Comment YES"

Stay honest about what happened

Social media rewards dramatic stories, which creates pressure to exaggerate. A story where you "almost gave up" or where a stranger's single sentence "changed your life" may get likes, but colleagues and recruiters who know the real facts will notice.

Stick to what actually happened. If a result was a team effort, say so and tag the team (with their permission). If you are sharing numbers — "grew my page to 5,000 followers" or "cleared the exam with 92 percentile" — make sure they are accurate and not rounded up.

Be careful with claims about money, investments or careers. Saying "this one habit made me rich" or presenting one person's outcome as typical can mislead readers who make real decisions based on it. Share your experience as your experience: "This worked for me; it may not suit everyone."

Do
  • Credit the team for team results
  • Share your experience as one person's experience
Don't
  • Add drama or numbers that didn't happen
  • Present one outcome as what everyone can expect

Protect other people's privacy

The best stories often involve other people — a customer, a manager, a candidate you interviewed. Their privacy matters more than your post. Before sharing, ask: would this person be comfortable if they read this and recognised themselves?

Never post a client's name, account details, screenshots of chats or emails, or anything your employer treats as confidential. For finance professionals this is especially important; client information must stay private. Change or remove identifying details: "a retired teacher I met last year" rather than "Mrs. Kulkarni from our Dadar branch".

If you want to tell a story about a colleague or a specific moment at work, ask first. Check your company's social media policy too — many firms have rules about what employees can say about clients, results or internal matters.

Quick exercise: draft a post about something you learnt this month. Read it once as the person most involved in the story. If anything would embarrass them, change it.

Do
  • Remove names and identifying details
  • Ask before posting about a colleague
  • Check your company's social media policy
Don't
  • Share screenshots of client chats or emails
  • Post confidential results or numbers

Key takeaways

  • Write a specific first line that earns the "see more" click.
  • Tell one small story per post in short paragraphs.
  • Keep stories and numbers honest; credit the team.
  • Never share client details, screenshots or confidential information.
  • Ask before posting about others, and follow your company's policy.

Quick check

Which first line is most likely to make the right reader click "see more"?

  1. "I'm happy to share that I completed a course."
  2. "Hello connections! Hope everyone is doing well and having a really productive week."
  3. "I failed my first mock interview because I couldn't explain my own project."
  4. "You won't believe this!!!"
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. "I failed my first mock interview because I couldn't explain my own project."

A specific moment creates curiosity and promises a real story.
