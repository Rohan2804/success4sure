Telling Stories with Data

Turn a page of numbers into a clear message people remember. You will learn to choose one key number, add context, use simple comparisons and check that every figure is correct.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Find the one number that matters

When people present data, they often show everything they have: twelve columns, five charts and a percentage for every row. The audience does not know where to look, so they remember nothing. A data story starts by picking the one number that answers the listener's question.

Ask yourself: if my manager remembers only one figure from this meeting, which should it be? For a branch review it might be "complaints resolved within 48 hours"; for a campaign it might be "cost per new account". Lead with that number, then use other figures only if they explain it.

Write your message as a sentence before you make any chart: "Our average complaint resolution time fell from 6 days to 4 days this quarter." If you cannot write that sentence, you are not ready to present the data.

Do
  • Write your key message as one sentence first
  • Lead with the number that answers the listener's question
Don't
  • Show every column "just in case"

Context turns a number into meaning

A number on its own means very little. "We opened 150 accounts" — is that good? Context answers that question. The three most useful kinds are: compared with what (last month, target, another branch), out of how many, and over what time.

"We opened 150 accounts this month, against a target of 120 and 110 last month" tells a clear story: above target and growing. "We opened 150 accounts" tells nothing.

Be careful with percentages on small bases. If complaints went from 2 to 4, that is technically a 100% increase, but saying "complaints doubled" can alarm people unnecessarily. Give the actual numbers alongside the percentage: "Complaints rose from 2 to 4 this month — small numbers, but we're checking the cause."

Do
  • Compare with target, last period or a similar team
  • Show actual numbers alongside percentages
Don't
  • Present a big percentage from a tiny base without the counts

Make comparisons people can picture

Large or abstract numbers are hard to feel. Translating them into something familiar helps. "We cut paper use by 60,000 sheets a year" is fine; "that's about 120 reams of paper we no longer buy" is easier to picture, since a ream has 500 sheets.

Simple ratios also work well. "Resolution time fell from 6 days to 4 days" can be said as "a third faster" — 2 days saved out of 6. "Footfall rose from 120 to 150 visitors a day" is "a 25% increase", because the extra 30 visitors is a quarter of 120. "One in five customers" is often clearer than "20%".

Whatever comparison you choose, do the arithmetic yourself and check it twice. One wrong figure — saying "50% faster" when it was a third faster — makes people doubt every other number you show.

Do
  • Convert big numbers into familiar units
  • Use "one in five" style ratios when they are clearer
Don't
  • Use a comparison you haven't checked

Keep charts simple and honest

If you use a chart, give it a title that states the message, not just the topic. "Complaint resolution time fell from 6 to 4 days" is a better title than "Resolution time Q1–Q2". The reader gets the point even if they glance for two seconds.

Choose the simplest chart that works: a bar chart to compare categories, a line chart to show change over time. Highlight the one bar or line that matters and keep the rest in grey. Avoid 3D effects and pie charts with many slices.

Honesty matters more than impact. A bar chart whose axis starts at 90 instead of 0 can make a small change look huge. Do not leave out the months that spoil the trend. If the data has a weakness — a small sample, a one-off event — say so. People forgive imperfect data; they do not forgive being misled.

A quick exercise: take one table from your last report and write the single sentence it supports. Then decide which one number proves it.

Do
  • Use the chart title to state the message
  • Highlight one bar or line and grey out the rest
Don't
  • Start bar-chart axes above zero to exaggerate a change
  • Drop inconvenient months from a trend

Key takeaways

  • Write your message as one sentence before building any chart.
  • Lead with the one number that answers the listener's question.
  • Add context: compared with what, out of how many, over what time.
  • Use familiar comparisons, and double-check the arithmetic.
  • Keep charts simple and never distort the data.

Quick check

Before making slides from a large data set, what should you do first?

  1. Write the key message as one sentence
  2. Pick a colourful chart template
  3. Include every column so nothing is missed
  4. Calculate as many percentages as possible
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Write the key message as one sentence

A one-sentence message tells you which numbers and charts you actually need.
