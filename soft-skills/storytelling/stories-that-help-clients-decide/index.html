Stories That Help Clients Understand Investing

Use clearly labelled illustrative stories to explain goals, time horizons and risk to clients. You will learn to make concepts relatable without cherry-picking returns, implying guarantees or skipping suitability and cost disclosure.

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Why stories help clients understand

Many clients find terms like "volatility", "asset allocation" or "time horizon" hard to connect with their lives. A short story about someone with a similar goal can make the idea click: "Imagine a couple saving for their daughter's college fees in twelve years..." The client starts thinking about their own situation, not about jargon.

But stories are powerful, which is exactly why they must be used carefully. A story about one investor who made a lot of money can create unrealistic expectations faster than any brochure. The goal of a client story is understanding, not persuasion to buy a product.

The principles in this lesson follow the spirit of the conduct expectations for distributors and advisers: put suitability first, never promise or imply returns, disclose costs and commission, and avoid anything that could mislead.

Do
  • Use stories to explain concepts, not to push a product
Don't
  • Use a story to create urgency or excitement about returns

Label illustrations clearly

Every example you share should be clearly marked as an illustration, not a real client's result or a forecast. Simple phrases work: "This is just an illustration to explain the idea" or "These are made-up numbers to show how it works, not what any fund will return."

Use composite or imaginary characters rather than real clients. "Take an imaginary investor, say Meera, who is 35..." protects real clients' privacy and makes it clear you are explaining a concept.

Where you use numbers, keep them simple and focus on what the client controls. For example: "If Meera invests ₹5,000 a month for ten years, she will have put in ₹6 lakh of her own money. What it grows to depends on the market — it could be more or less, and in some years the value may fall below what she invested." The arithmetic is correct, and no return is promised.

Do
  • Say "This is an illustration" every time
  • Use imaginary characters instead of real clients
Don't
  • Present a past return as what the client can expect
  • Share a real client's story or portfolio

Stories about goals and time

The most useful client stories connect investments to goals and timelines. A story about two goals can explain why different products suit different needs: "Imagine Arjun needs ₹2 lakh for a wedding in eighteen months, and also wants to build a retirement fund over twenty-five years. Money needed soon usually shouldn't be in something that can fall sharply just before he needs it. Money for twenty-five years away can generally handle more ups and downs."

This kind of story helps the client think about their own goals before you discuss any product. It naturally leads into a suitability conversation: "What are you saving for, and when will you need it?"

Avoid stories that compare a client with a richer friend or neighbour ("Your colleague already has ₹50 lakh in equity"). Comparison creates pressure, not understanding, and that colleague's risk profile is not your client's.

Do
  • Link stories to the client's own goals and timelines
  • Use the story to start a suitability conversation
Don't
  • Compare the client with friends or neighbours to create pressure

Stories about risk — told honestly

Risk stories matter most, because clients who don't expect falls are the ones who panic and sell at the worst time. Explain falls before they happen: "Imagine your ₹10 lakh portfolio shows ₹8 lakh after a bad few months — a 20% fall. How would you feel? What would you do?" This is an illustration, but it helps you judge whether the client's risk appetite matches the product.

Avoid cherry-picking. Showing only the best five-year period of a fund, or the story of one investor who "made 3x in five years", gives a false picture. If you show past performance, use the full, standard figures from official documents, include the weak periods, and say clearly that past performance does not indicate future returns.

Finally, be open about costs and your own earnings. A story about how an investment works is incomplete without: "There's an expense ratio built into the fund, and I earn a commission from the fund house when you invest through me." (If you are a fee-based adviser, explain your fee in the same plain way.) Clients who learn this later feel misled; clients who hear it upfront tend to trust you more.

Exercise: rewrite one example you often use with clients. Add a clear illustration label, remove any return figure you can't support, and add one line about a possible fall.

Do
  • Describe a possible fall before the client invests
  • Disclose expense ratio and your commission upfront
Don't
  • Show only the best period of a fund's history
  • Say or imply that returns are guaranteed or "safe"

Key takeaways

  • Use stories to build understanding, not to push a product.
  • Label every example as an illustration and use imaginary characters.
  • Connect stories to the client's goals and timelines, then check suitability.
  • Explain possible falls before the client invests.
  • Never cherry-pick returns or imply guarantees; disclose costs and commission.
