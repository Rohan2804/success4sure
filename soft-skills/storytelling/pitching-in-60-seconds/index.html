Pitching an Idea in 60 Seconds

Pitch an idea to a manager, team or panel in about a minute. You will learn to lead with the problem, state your idea in one sentence, give one proof point and end with a clear ask.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Start with the problem, not the idea

Most people open a pitch with their solution: "I think we should build a WhatsApp chatbot." The listener's first thought is "Why?" — and they start looking for reasons to say no. If you start with a problem they already feel, they lean in instead.

Compare: "Our helpdesk gets around 300 calls a week, and nearly half are just people asking for their account statement." Now the listener is thinking about the problem with you. When you then share your idea, it sounds like an answer rather than an extra task.

Pick the problem that matters to this listener. A finance head cares about cost, a branch manager about customer complaints, a team lead about wasted hours. Same idea, different opening.

Do
  • Open with a problem the listener already cares about
Don't
  • Open with "I have an idea" and the solution

A simple 60-second shape

A one-minute pitch has room for about 130 to 150 spoken words. That is enough for five short parts: the problem (10–15 seconds), your idea in one sentence (10 seconds), how it works or one example (15 seconds), one proof point (10 seconds), and your ask (10 seconds).

Here is the helpdesk pitch in full: "Our helpdesk gets around 300 calls a week, and nearly half are people asking for their account statement. I'd like to let customers download statements by replying 'STATEMENT' on our WhatsApp number. It would send a password-protected PDF to the registered number only. A branch in our network tried this for a month and statement calls there fell by about 60%. Could I have two weeks and one developer to build a pilot for the Thane branch?"

Read it aloud and it takes under a minute. Every sentence earns its place.

Do
  • Write your pitch out and time it aloud
  • Describe the idea in a single sentence
Don't
  • Try to cover every feature and risk in one minute

One proof point beats five claims

Listeners believe evidence more than enthusiasm. But in sixty seconds you have room for only one piece of proof, so pick the strongest: a small trial, a number from your own data, a customer quote or a comparable example.

Weak: "This will be very useful, customers will love it, it is easy and modern." Strong: "When we tried it with 50 customers last month, 41 used it at least once." One fact like this does more than five adjectives.

Be honest about the size of your proof. "In a small trial" or "in one branch" is fine — it signals you are realistic. Overclaiming ("this will double our revenue") invites tough questions you cannot answer and makes the rest of the pitch less believable.

Do
  • Use a small trial, a real number or a customer quote
  • Say how big or small your evidence is
Don't
  • Stack adjectives like "amazing" and "game-changing"
  • Promise outcomes you cannot back up

End with a clear, small ask

Many pitches end with "So, what do you think?" That hands all the work back to the listener. A good pitch ends with a specific, easy-to-approve request: time, budget, people or a next meeting.

Small asks get approved more often than big ones. "Can I run a two-week pilot in one branch?" is easier to say yes to than "Can we roll this out across all branches?" Once the pilot works, the next pitch is much stronger.

After your ask, stop talking. Silence feels awkward, but filling it with extra points weakens the request. If the listener has questions, answer briefly and come back to the ask. Prepare two or three likely questions — cost, risk, who will do the work — so you are not caught off guard.

Practice tip: record your pitch on your phone. Listen once for length and once for filler words like "basically" and "actually".

Do
  • Ask for something specific and small, like a pilot
  • Pause after the ask and let the listener respond
Don't
  • End with "So, what do you think?"
  • Keep talking after you've made your ask

Key takeaways

  • Open with a problem your listener already feels.
  • State your idea in one sentence.
  • Use one honest proof point instead of many claims.
  • End with a specific, small ask — then stop talking.
  • Write it, time it and practise it aloud.

Quick check

Which opening is most likely to make a branch manager lean in?

  1. "I have a great idea for a new app."
  2. "Technology is changing everything about how our customers bank these days."
  3. "Nearly half our helpdesk calls are just for account statements."
  4. "Can I take five minutes of your time?"
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. "Nearly half our helpdesk calls are just for account statements."

It starts with a concrete problem the manager already feels.
