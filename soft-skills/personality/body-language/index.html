Body Language That Builds Trust

Use posture, eye contact, gestures and greetings that match your words, so colleagues and clients read you as attentive and trustworthy, in person and on video.

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Why signals matter, and their limits

People read your body before they process your words. If you say "I'm happy to help" while scrolling your phone, the listener believes the phone. Trust builds when your words and signals match.

Two cautions keep this practical. First, no single gesture has one fixed meaning. Crossed arms can mean defensiveness, or simply that the air conditioning is too cold. Read clusters of signals and context, not isolated movements. Second, you may have heard that 93 per cent of communication is non-verbal. That figure comes from narrow experiments about feelings conveyed in single words and is widely misquoted. Words matter enormously; body language decides whether people believe them.

India also adds variety. Norms on eye contact with elders, physical distance and greetings differ across regions, generations and families. Observe first, then adapt.

Posture and stillness

Aim for open and settled, not stiff. When seated, sit back in the chair with your feet on the floor, shoulders relaxed and hands resting on the table or your lap. Lean forward slightly when the other person is making an important point; it signals that you are listening.

Restless movements leak nervousness: tapping a pen, jiggling a leg, adjusting your watch, clicking a mouse. Notice your own habit by recording a short practice explanation on your phone and watching it once with the sound off.

Your phone is part of your posture. Placing it face down, or away, at the start of a meeting says "you have my attention" more clearly than any sentence.

Do
  • Sit back with relaxed shoulders and visible hands
  • Put your phone face down before a conversation
Don't
  • Fidget with pens, rings or your watch
  • Turn your body towards the door while someone is talking

Eye contact and expression

Comfortable eye contact means looking at the person most of the time while they speak, and a little less while you speak, breaking away naturally to think. Staring without breaks feels intense; constantly looking away feels evasive. If direct eye contact with a senior feels uncomfortable for you, looking at the area around their eyes works well.

In a group, share your gaze. Freshers often look only at the most senior person, which makes others feel excluded. When you answer a question, start with the person who asked, then include the rest of the table.

Let your face match the message. A neutral or warm expression while listening, a brief nod to show understanding, and a genuine smile when greeting someone do more than any rehearsed gesture. Smiling while delivering bad news, however, reads as careless.

Do
  • Share eye contact across everyone in a group
  • Nod to show you are following
Don't
  • Speak only to the most senior person in the room
  • Smile through serious or bad news

Greetings, handshakes and personal space

Indian workplaces use several greetings: a namaste, a handshake, a nod, or a mix. A simple rule is to follow the other person's lead. If they extend a hand, offer a firm but not crushing handshake for a second or two with eye contact. If they fold their hands in a namaste, return it. Some people prefer not to shake hands for personal, cultural or religious reasons; respond warmly with a namaste or a nod and move on without comment.

Keep a comfortable distance, roughly an arm's length, in conversation. Avoid touching shoulders or backs of colleagues and clients, however friendly your intent.

When someone walks up to your desk, stand or turn fully towards them. It takes a second and signals respect.

Body language on video calls

Much client and team work now happens on video, where your frame is your body language. Place the camera at eye level by raising the laptop on a few books, so you are not looking down at people. Sit facing a light source, not with a bright window behind you.

Look at the camera when making a key point, and at the faces when listening. Keep your head and shoulders in frame, and let your nods be visible. If you must check another window, say so: "Let me pull up your portfolio statement." Silent glancing away looks like distraction.

Before an important call, do a ten-second check: camera height, light, background tidy, notifications off.

Key takeaways

  • Trust grows when your words and signals match.
  • Read clusters of signals in context, never a single gesture.
  • Sit settled, keep hands visible and phone face down.
  • Share eye contact across the group, not just the senior.
  • Follow the other person's lead on greetings and respect preferences.
  • On video, camera at eye level and narrate when you look away.

Quick check

A client sits with crossed arms during your presentation. What is the most sensible interpretation?

  1. They are definitely rejecting your proposal
  2. They are bored and you should wrap up early
  3. Look at other signals and the context first
  4. They are angry and you should apologise
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Look at other signals and the context first

Single gestures have many possible meanings; the room may simply be cold.
