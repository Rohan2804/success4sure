Investing Basics: Compounding, Risk and Time

Understand the core ideas behind investing: compounding, inflation, risk versus return, diversification and time horizon. You will learn to judge claims critically and see why starting early and investing regularly matter.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Educational content only — not investment, tax or financial advice. Consider your own situation or consult a SEBI-registered investment adviser before making financial decisions.

Why money needs to grow: inflation

Prices tend to rise over time. This is inflation, and it quietly reduces what your money can buy. For example, if prices rise by 6% a year, something that costs ₹100 today would cost about ₹179 in ten years.

So money that sits idle for long periods loses buying power, even though the number in your account stays the same. That is the main reason people invest money they will not need for many years: to try to grow it faster than prices rise.

This does not mean all savings must be invested. Your emergency fund and money for near-term needs belong somewhere stable. Investing is for goals that are several years away, after the basics such as clearing expensive debt are in place.

Do
  • Separate short-term money from long-term money
  • Clear high-interest debt before investing
Don't
  • Assume money in an account keeps its value over decades

Compounding: growth on growth

Compounding means your returns start earning returns of their own. Purely for illustration, assume ₹1,00,000 grows at a steady 8% a year. After 10 years it would be about ₹2,15,892; after 20 years about ₹4,66,096; after 30 years about ₹10,06,266. The growth in the last ten years is bigger than in the first twenty, because the base keeps getting larger.

A quick shortcut is the rule of 72: divide 72 by the yearly growth rate to estimate how many years money takes to double. At an example 8%, that is about nine years.

Time matters more than most people expect. For example, assume ₹5,000 a month invested at a steady 8% a year. Starting at 25 and stopping at 60, you invest ₹21 lakh and end with roughly ₹1.15 crore. Starting at 35, you invest ₹15 lakh and end with roughly ₹48 lakh. These are illustrations only: real returns are never steady, can be negative, and are not promised by anyone honest.

Do
  • Start early, even with small amounts
  • Label every growth number you see as an assumption
Don't
  • Treat an example rate as a promise

Risk and return go together

Every investment involves a trade-off. Options that aim for higher returns usually come with bigger ups and downs in value, and sometimes losses. Options that are more stable usually aim for lower returns. There is no option that is high-return, low-risk and always available at short notice.

Risk is not only about losing money. It also includes the chance that your money is not available when you need it, or that it grows more slowly than inflation.

Be very wary of anyone who claims high returns with no risk, or who calls an investment guaranteed when it is linked to markets. In India, investment advisers must be registered with SEBI, and genuine professionals talk about risks as clearly as they talk about returns.

Do
  • Ask what could go wrong before asking about returns
  • Check that anyone advising you is SEBI-registered
Don't
  • Believe claims of high returns with no risk

Diversification, time horizon and regular investing

Diversification means not putting all your money in one place. Spreading money across different types of assets, sectors and issuers reduces the damage if any single one does badly. It does not remove risk, but it smooths the ride.

Time horizon is how long until you need the money. Money for a goal two years away usually needs more stability; money for a goal twenty years away can generally ride out more ups and downs, because there is time to recover from falls. Matching the investment to the goal's timeline is one of the most important decisions.

A systematic investment plan, or SIP, is a method rather than a product: you invest a fixed amount at regular intervals, often monthly. It builds discipline and means you buy at different prices over time instead of trying to time the market. Before choosing where to invest, read the official documents, understand the costs, and consider talking to a SEBI-registered investment adviser.

Do
  • Match each investment to the goal's time horizon
  • Read official documents and understand costs
Don't
  • Put all your money into one stock or one idea
  • Stop investing in panic after a short fall

Key takeaways

  • Inflation reduces what idle money can buy over time.
  • Compounding rewards starting early and staying invested.
  • Higher potential return comes with higher risk; nothing is high-return and risk-free.
  • Diversify, and match investments to each goal's time horizon.
  • A SIP is a method of regular investing, not a product or a promise.

Quick check

For example, prices rise 6% a year. What is roughly true of ₹100 kept idle for ten years?

  1. It will buy more than it does today
  2. It will buy about the same
  3. It will buy about what ₹56 buys today
  4. It will roughly double in value, as bank balances grow automatically
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. It will buy about what ₹56 buys today

At 6% a year, ₹100 has the buying power of about ₹56 in today's money after ten years.
