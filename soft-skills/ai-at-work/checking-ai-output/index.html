Checking AI Output Before You Use It

Spot made-up facts, wrong figures and one-sided answers in AI output before they reach your manager or client. You will learn a practical way to verify claims and to match the depth of checking to the stakes.

Last updated 16 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Why AI can sound sure and still be wrong

AI chat tools generate text that is likely to fit your question. They are very good at sounding fluent and confident, but fluency is not the same as accuracy. A tool can produce a statistic, a quote, a book title, a court case or a web link that looks perfectly real and does not exist. This is often called a "hallucination".

Tools can also be out of date. Rules, tax slabs, company results and product details change, and a tool may describe an older version as if it were current. Arithmetic can slip too, especially in long calculations or when converting units such as lakh and crore.

None of this means AI is useless. It means the output is a draft from a fast but unreliable assistant. Once you use it, it becomes your work, and your name is on it.

Verify facts, figures and sources

Check every specific claim that matters: names, dates, numbers, quotes and rules. Go to the original source rather than another summary: the official website of a regulator or government department, a company's annual report, the actual policy document or the original study.

If the tool gives you links or references, open each one. Confirm the page exists and actually says what the tool claims. A real link to an unrelated page is as unhelpful as a fake one.

Recalculate any figure you will use. If the AI says sales grew from ₹42 lakh to ₹51 lakh, "an increase of 18%", work it out yourself in a spreadsheet: it is about 21%. Small errors like this are easy to miss and embarrassing when a client or manager spots them.

Do
  • Check claims against the original source
  • Open every link and recalculate every figure
Don't
  • Treat a confident tone as proof
  • Cite the AI tool itself as your source

Look for bias and missing views

AI tools learn from huge amounts of text written by people, so they can repeat the biases in that text. A job description drafted by AI may use language that subtly favours one gender. An answer about "typical customers" may assume everyone lives in a metro city, speaks English or has a salaried job.

Watch for answers that give only one side. If you ask "Why is remote work bad for productivity?", you will get arguments for that view, because the question assumed it. Ask neutrally instead, or follow up with "What are the strongest arguments against this?" and "Who might this advice not suit?"

Read the output as if you were the person it describes. If a customer, colleague or candidate would find it unfair or stereotyped, rewrite it.

Do
  • Ask neutral questions and request counter-arguments
  • Read the text from the point of view of the people it describes
Don't
  • Accept assumptions about gender, region or background

Match your checking to the stakes

Not every output needs the same level of checking. Brainstorming ten titles for a team newsletter needs a quick read for sense and tone. A client presentation, a report to senior management, anything involving money, health, law or a person's reputation needs every fact verified.

A useful rule: the more people who will rely on it, and the harder it is to take back, the more carefully you check. A message to a colleague can be corrected in a minute; a figure in a published report or a client email cannot.

Before you use AI output, ask one question: could I explain and defend every line of this if my manager asked? If the answer is no for any line, check it or remove it.

Do
  • Check thoroughly when money, people or reputation are involved
  • Remove anything you cannot explain or defend
Don't
  • Forward AI output you have not read in full

Key takeaways

  • Fluent and confident does not mean accurate.
  • Verify names, dates, figures and rules against original sources.
  • Open every link and recalculate every number you use.
  • Ask neutral questions and look for bias or missing views.
  • Check more deeply when the stakes are higher.
  • If you cannot defend a line, check it or cut it.

Quick check

An AI tool gives you a statistic with a neat source link for your report. What should you do?

  1. Use it, since a link is included
  2. Open the link and confirm the page exists and contains that statistic
  3. Ask the AI tool whether it is sure
  4. Round the number slightly so that it looks less precise and harder to question
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Open the link and confirm the page exists and contains that statistic

Links can be fake or point to pages that say something else; only the source itself confirms the figure.
