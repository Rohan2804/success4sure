Using AI for Emails, Research and Summaries

Use AI chat tools to draft emails, summarise long documents and start research faster, while keeping your own judgement in charge. You will learn practical prompts for each task and when doing it yourself is quicker.

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Drafting emails in your own voice

AI is good at turning rough notes into a clean draft. Give it the key points, the recipient and the tone you want: "Draft a polite email to our vendor, Mr. Shetty, saying the delivery was two days late, asking for a revised schedule by Friday, and keeping the relationship friendly. Under 120 words."

Then edit the draft so it sounds like you. AI drafts often use long, overly polished phrases such as "I hope this email finds you in excellent health" or "I wanted to take a moment to reach out". Cut them. A short, direct email in your own words reads as more genuine.

Always check names, dates, amounts and promises before you send. The AI may add a commitment you never intended, such as "we will waive the charges", simply because it sounded helpful.

Do
  • Give the key points, recipient and tone
  • Edit the draft into your own voice
Don't
  • Send a draft containing promises you did not make
  • Keep flowery openings that add nothing

Summaries that are actually useful

A vague request like "Summarise this" gives a vague summary. Ask for what you will use: "From these meeting notes, list the decisions made, the action items with owner and deadline, and any open questions." For a long report: "Give me the five main findings in one line each, and any figures that affect our branch."

Only paste documents your company's policy allows, using an approved tool for internal material. Remove personal or client details if they are not needed for the summary.

Check the summary against the original before sharing it. Summaries can drop important conditions ("subject to approval" becomes "approved"), merge two people's tasks into one or invent action items that were never agreed. For anything you forward to others, skim the original sections the summary relies on.

Do
  • Ask for decisions, actions, owners and deadlines
  • Compare key points with the original before sharing
Don't
  • Paste documents your policy does not allow
  • Forward a summary you have not checked

Research: a starting point, not the answer

AI tools are useful at the start of research. They can explain unfamiliar jargon, outline the main aspects of a topic and suggest questions you had not thought of. If you are new to a project on electric vehicle financing, you might ask: "What are the key questions a lender should consider when financing electric two-wheelers in India?"

Treat the answer as a map, not the destination. Use it to decide what to look up, then go to reliable sources: official websites, industry reports, company filings and reputable news outlets. Note your sources as you go so you can cite them.

If the tool provides facts, figures or references, verify them before they enter your work. In a report, cite the original source, never the AI tool.

Do
  • Use AI to explain jargon and suggest questions
  • Cite original sources in your work
Don't
  • Treat AI answers as final research
  • Copy AI-provided figures without checking

Know when AI saves time and when it doesn't

AI tends to save time on first drafts, rewording, turning rough notes into a table, brainstorming options and drafting a translation into Hindi, Marathi or another language. A translation should still be checked by someone fluent before it goes to customers.

It saves less time, and can cause harm, on tasks that depend on judgement or relationships: final numbers, condolence messages, difficult feedback to a colleague, or a reply to an angry customer whose situation you know well. These deserve your own words, even if AI helps you think through structure.

A practical rule: if fixing the AI's draft takes longer than writing it yourself, write it yourself. Over a few weeks you will learn which of your tasks benefit and which do not.

Do
  • Use AI for first drafts, reformatting and brainstorming
  • Have translations checked by a fluent speaker
Don't
  • Outsource sensitive personal messages to AI

Key takeaways

  • Give AI the key points, recipient and tone, then edit into your own voice.
  • Check AI drafts for names, figures and promises you did not make.
  • Ask for summaries of decisions, actions, owners and deadlines.
  • Use AI to start research, then verify with original sources.
  • Write sensitive or judgement-heavy messages yourself.

Quick check

Which summary request will be most useful after a project meeting?

  1. "Summarise this."
  2. "Make these notes shorter."
  3. "Rewrite these notes in formal English."
  4. "List the decisions, action items with owner and deadline, and open questions."
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. "List the decisions, action items with owner and deadline, and open questions."

Asking for exactly what you will act on makes the summary immediately useful.
