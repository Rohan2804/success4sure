Creating Win–Win Offers

Build offers that give both sides more of what they value, by bringing several issues to the table and trading instead of conceding. You will learn to present options and close with a clear written agreement.

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Put more than one issue on the table

When only one thing is being discussed — usually price — every rupee you gain is a rupee the other side loses. That makes the talk feel like a fight. Win–win outcomes become possible when you add more issues: timing, quantity, quality, payment terms, delivery, support or length of contract.

Imagine your team is choosing a caterer for a 120-person office Diwali lunch. Instead of arguing only over the per-plate rate, list everything that matters: menu size, serving time, cutlery, leftover packing, advance amount and whether they can also handle the quarterly town hall.

With six issues instead of one, you can give ground where it costs you little and gain where it matters most to you. The caterer may care far more about guaranteed repeat business than about ₹20 a plate.

Do
  • List every issue that could be part of the deal
  • Ask what else matters to the other side
Don't
  • Let the whole negotiation shrink to price alone

Trade, don't simply give in

A concession given for nothing teaches the other side to ask for more. A trade links what you give to what you get. The simplest tool is an "if… then…" sentence: "If we confirm the town hall order as well, could you bring the per-plate rate to ₹420?"

Look for items that are cheap for you but valuable to them, and the reverse. For the caterer, a confirmed second booking is valuable and costs your team nothing if you need one anyway. For you, an earlier serving time may be important while it makes little difference to them.

Keep a quick note of what you have offered and received. It helps you avoid giving away the same thing twice and shows you whether the overall deal is still balanced.

Do
  • Use "if… then…" to link what you give to what you get
  • Offer things that are cheap for you but valuable to them
Don't
  • Make one-sided concessions to seem nice
  • Agree to items piece by piece without seeing the whole deal

Offer choices, not ultimatums

People resist a single take-it-or-leave-it offer. They engage more with a choice. Prepare two or three packages that are roughly equal in value to you but differ in shape.

For example: Option A — ₹450 a plate, 40% advance, standard menu. Option B — ₹420 a plate, 25% advance, with the town hall order confirmed. Option C — ₹435 a plate, full payment after the event, with one extra dessert.

Their choice tells you what they value most, even if they pick none of them. If the caterer says, "B looks good but I need a bigger advance", you have learned that cash in hand matters to them, and you can adjust.

Keep the options honest. Packages built to trick someone into picking a poor deal damage trust once they notice, and they usually do.

Do
  • Prepare two or three packages of similar value to you
  • Treat their reaction as information
Don't
  • Design a fake "decoy" option to push them into one choice

Check fairness and write it down

Before you close, ask yourself whether the other side could explain the deal to their boss or family and feel fine about it. A deal that only you are happy with tends to come back as poor service, delays or a broken agreement.

Then confirm everything in writing, even with people you know well. A short WhatsApp or email summary works: "To confirm: 120 plates at ₹420, veg and Jain options, serving by 12:30 pm on 30 Oct, 25% advance on booking, balance within seven days of the event, town hall lunch on 15 Jan at the same rate."

Ask them to reply "confirmed" or correct anything wrong. Clear written terms prevent most of the disputes that start with "But I thought you said…".

Do
  • Send a written summary of every agreed point
  • Ask the other side to confirm or correct it
Don't
  • Rely on a handshake for anything with money or dates

Key takeaways

  • Bring several issues to the table, not just price.
  • Link every concession to something you receive.
  • Offer two or three honest packages and learn from the choice.
  • Aim for a deal the other side can feel fine about.
  • Confirm every agreed point in writing.

Quick check

Why does negotiating only on price often feel like a fight?

  1. "Price is always the least important issue in any deal"
  2. Every gain for one side is a loss for the other
  3. It takes too long
  4. People dislike numbers

With a single issue there is nothing to trade, so the talk becomes a tug of war.
