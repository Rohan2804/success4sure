Negotiating Fees and Terms with Clients Ethically

Discuss fees, commissions and service terms with clients openly, negotiate only what you are allowed to change, and never let a sale override suitability. You will learn phrases for fee conversations and how to record what you agree.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Put every cost on the table first

Clients negotiate badly — and trust you less — when they discover costs later. Before discussing anything else, explain how you are paid and what the client will pay. If you are a distributor earning commission from the product provider, say so plainly. If you charge an advisory fee, explain the amount, how it is calculated and when it is billed.

Also cover the product's own costs: the expense ratio of a fund, any exit load and its time period, transaction charges or account fees. A simple line helps: "Here are all the costs you'll bear, in one place. Please ask me about anything that isn't clear."

Put this in a short written note the client can keep. Transparency doesn't weaken your position; it removes suspicion so the real conversation can happen.

Do
  • Explain how you are paid before recommending anything
  • Give the client all costs in writing, in one place
Don't
  • Leave commissions or charges for the client to discover later
  • Use jargon to make costs sound smaller

Know what is and isn't negotiable

Some things are yours to negotiate; others are not. Product costs set by a fund house, statutory charges and regulatory requirements such as KYC and risk profiling are not open for bargaining, and pretending otherwise misleads the client.

What you can usually shape is your own service: how often you review the portfolio, whether meetings are in person or on video, how quickly you respond, what reports you send and — where your business model and rules allow — your own advisory fee. Offering service tiers is often fairer than cutting your fee: "For ₹X a year, I review quarterly and meet twice. If you prefer, a half-yearly review costs less."

If a client asks for a discount you can't give, say so clearly and explain why, rather than making vague promises or quietly cutting corners on service later.

Do
  • Offer service tiers that match different needs
  • Say clearly when something is not negotiable and why
Don't
  • Offer to "adjust" regulated charges or skip required steps
  • Agree to a fee you can't sustain and then cut service quietly

Never trade suitability for a sale

The hardest pressure often comes from clients themselves: "Skip the risk questionnaire, I know what I want", "Just put everything in the fund that gave 40% last year" or "I'll invest only if you promise 15%." Agreeing might close the deal today, but it is wrong and can hurt the client.

Hold the line politely and explain why it protects them: "I need to understand your goals and comfort with risk before I can recommend anything — it's there to protect you." On returns, be direct: "No one can promise returns on market-linked investments. I can show you how this fund has behaved in good and bad years."

If a client insists on something unsuitable, document your advice and their decision, or decline the business. Losing one sale is better than a product that doesn't fit and a complaint later.

Do
  • Complete risk profiling before any recommendation
  • Say plainly that returns can't be promised
Don't
  • Promise or hint at guaranteed returns
  • Recommend a product because it closes the sale faster or pays more commission

Agree terms in writing and review them

Once you and the client agree, summarise it in writing: the services you will provide, the fee or commission arrangement, review frequency, how to reach you and how either side can end the arrangement. Ask the client to confirm.

Revisit the terms at least once a year or when the client's situation changes — a new job, marriage, a child, retirement. A client who first wanted monthly calls may now prefer a half-yearly review, or may need more support than before.

When terms change, write that down too. A clear record protects the client, protects you, and makes it easy for anyone in your firm to see what was promised. Long-term client relationships rest less on any single negotiation than on a track record of doing exactly what you said.

Do
  • Send a written summary of services, fees and review frequency
  • Review terms when the client's life changes
Don't
  • Change terms verbally without a record

Key takeaways

  • Explain how you are paid and every cost before recommending anything.
  • Negotiate your own service, not regulated charges or required steps.
  • Offer service tiers rather than quiet cuts in quality.
  • Never skip suitability or promise returns to close a sale.
  • Put agreed terms in writing and review them as life changes.

When should you explain how you are paid?

  1. Only if the client asks you directly about it
  2. Before recommending any product
  3. After the client invests
