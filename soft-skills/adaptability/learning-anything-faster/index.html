Learning Anything Faster

Use three well-researched techniques — testing yourself, spacing your practice and explaining to others — to learn new skills in less time. You will be able to replace rereading and cramming with a simple weekly learning plan.

Last updated 5 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Why rereading feels useful but teaches little

Most of us learned to study by rereading notes and highlighting textbooks. It feels productive because the material starts to look familiar. But familiarity is not the same as being able to recall something when you need it — in a client meeting, an interview or an exam hall.

Research on learning has repeatedly found that the techniques which feel easiest are often the least effective. Rereading gives you a sense of fluency; the moment you close the book, much of it is gone. The methods that work better feel slightly harder, because they make your brain do the work of pulling information out rather than just taking it in.

The good news is that you do not need special talent or expensive courses. You need to change how you spend the hours you already put in.

Do
  • Notice when studying feels easy — it may not be sticking
Don't
  • Mistake highlighting for learning

Active recall: test yourself before you feel ready

Active recall means trying to remember something without looking. Close the book, then write down or say aloud everything you can recall. Only then check what you missed. The struggle to remember is what strengthens memory.

Say you have joined a bank and need to learn five new loan products. Instead of reading the product sheets again, take a blank page and write, for each product, who it is for, the key documents and the main eligibility conditions. Then compare with the sheet and mark the gaps in a different colour.

Other forms of recall include flashcards (paper or an app), answering practice questions, and asking yourself "Why does this work?" after each topic. If you prepared for a competitive exam with mock tests, you already know this works — mocks are recall practice.

Do
  • Use a blank page or flashcards after each study session
  • Check answers only after you have tried
Don't
  • Peek at the answer the moment you feel stuck

Spaced practice beats one long cramming session

Cramming can get you through tomorrow's test, but the knowledge fades quickly. Spacing means revisiting the same material over increasing gaps — for example, on day 1, day 3, day 7 and day 14. Each time you return just as you are starting to forget, the memory becomes more durable.

You can also mix related topics in one session instead of practising one type of problem fifty times in a row. If you are learning Excel, alternate between lookups, pivot tables and charts. Mixing feels messier, but it trains you to choose the right method — which is what real work demands.

A practical way to do this is a small calendar note: "Revise SIP vs lump sum notes — 3 Nov, 5 Nov, 9 Nov, 16 Nov." Ten focused minutes on each date will do more than a three-hour session the night before.

Do
  • Schedule short reviews at growing intervals
  • Mix related topics within a session
Don't
  • Rely on one long session the night before

Teach it to learn it

Explaining a topic to someone else quickly exposes what you do not understand. When you try to teach, you have to organise ideas, choose examples and answer "why" questions.

You do not need a classroom. Explain a new process to a colleague over chai, record a two-minute voice note as if teaching a junior, or write five plain-language lines that a newcomer could follow. Wherever you stumble or reach for jargon, that is your next thing to study.

A useful test: could you explain the concept to a family member who has never worked in your field? If not, simplify until you can.

Do
  • Write a five-line explanation for a beginner
Don't
  • Hide behind jargon when explaining

Skip the myths and build a routine

You may have heard that people are "visual" or "auditory" learners and should study only in that style. Careful studies have not found that matching teaching to a preferred style improves results. It is better to match the method to the content: diagrams for processes, speaking practice for language, hands-on practice for software.

Sleep, short breaks and fewer distractions matter more than any shortcut. A simple routine works: 20 minutes of focused study with the phone in another room, five minutes of recall on a blank page, and a calendar reminder for the next review. Repeat it four or five days a week and track what you can now do that you could not do last month.

Do
  • Match the method to the content
  • Protect sleep during heavy learning weeks
Don't
  • Limit yourself to one 'learning style'

Key takeaways

  • Familiarity from rereading is not the same as being able to recall.
  • Test yourself with a blank page, flashcards or practice questions.
  • Revisit material over growing gaps instead of cramming.
  • Explain a topic simply to find the gaps in your understanding.
  • Match the method to the content, not to a 'learning style'.

Quick check

You have read a new product manual three times and it now feels familiar. What is the best next step?

  1. Read it a fourth time, slowly and carefully, to be safe
  2. Close it and write the key points from memory
  3. Highlight the most important lines in yellow
  4. Watch a video summary of the same manual
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Close it and write the key points from memory

Recalling without looking is what builds durable memory; familiarity alone can be misleading.
