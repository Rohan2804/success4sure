A Growth Mindset in Practice

Turn the idea that abilities can grow into everyday habits: rewriting self-limiting thoughts, using feedback as data and reviewing mistakes calmly. You will be able to pick stretch goals and track real progress.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

What a growth mindset is — and what it is not

A growth mindset is the belief that your abilities can improve through effort, good strategies and help from others. A fixed mindset assumes abilities are set: you are either "good at maths" or not, "a natural speaker" or not.

It is easy to turn this into a slogan. A growth mindset is not positive thinking, and it does not mean anyone can become anything with enough willpower. Effort alone is not enough either — working hard with a poor method just tires you out. Research suggests simply believing in growth has modest effects; what matters is what you do differently because of that belief.

So treat this lesson as a set of habits, not a personality type. Most people are a mix: confident about growing in some areas and stuck in others.

Do
  • Pair effort with a better strategy
  • Ask for help when you are stuck
Don't
  • Treat growth mindset as just 'thinking positive'

Catch and rewrite your fixed scripts

Fixed-mindset thinking often shows up as short, confident sentences: "I'm not a numbers person." "My English isn't good enough for presentations." "I'm just bad at networking." They feel like facts, but they are predictions.

Try a three-step rewrite. First, notice the sentence. Second, add "yet" or "right now" to make it about the present. Third, add one concrete next step. "I'm not a numbers person" becomes "I'm not comfortable with financial ratios yet — I'll practise five ratio questions each evening this week."

Keep a note on your phone of the scripts you catch most often. Over a month you will see patterns, and the rewrites will start to come faster.

Do
  • Add 'yet' and a next step to self-limiting sentences
Don't
  • Treat a current weakness as a permanent label

Use feedback as data, not a verdict

Feedback stings most when we hear it as a judgement on who we are. A growth approach treats it as information about one piece of work. "This report is hard to follow" is about the report, not about you.

When you get feedback, ask a narrowing question: "Which section was hardest to follow?" or "What one change would make this stronger?" Then repeat back what you will do: "So I'll add a summary at the top and cut the second table. I'll send it by 4 pm."

Seek feedback before it is forced on you. Ask a senior to review a draft early, when changes are cheap. People who ask regularly tend to improve faster, simply because they get more useful information.

Do
  • Ask 'What one change would make this stronger?'
  • Seek feedback early on drafts
Don't
  • Argue or explain away every point immediately

Review mistakes without beating yourself up

Mistakes are where most learning happens, but only if you look at them. A short, calm review works better than either ignoring the mistake or replaying it all night.

Use three questions: What happened? What did I control? What will I try next time? For example, a pitch to a client went poorly. What happened: you ran out of time before the key slide. What you controlled: the slide order and your rehearsal. Next time: put the recommendation on slide two and rehearse with a timer.

Being kind to yourself is not the same as lowering standards. You can say "That was a poor result" and "I know what to change" in the same breath.

Do
  • Use What happened / What I controlled / What next
Don't
  • Confuse self-criticism with improvement

Stretch sensibly and track progress

Growth comes from tasks slightly beyond your current comfort — not so easy that you learn nothing, not so hard that you panic. Volunteer to present a five-minute update before you aim for the quarterly town hall. Take one new client segment before asking for a whole region.

Track progress in concrete terms, because improvement is hard to feel day to day. Note your mock score, your presentation timing or the number of calls before you closed a query. Looking back at last month's numbers is often the best motivation there is.

Finally, encourage growth in others. Praise a junior's approach ("You checked the data twice before sending — good habit") rather than calling them "a genius". It helps them focus on what they can repeat.

Do
  • Choose stretch tasks one step beyond comfort
  • Praise the process, not the talent
Don't
  • Jump straight to the hardest challenge and give up

Key takeaways

  • A growth mindset is a set of habits, not just a belief.
  • Rewrite fixed scripts with 'yet' and one next step.
  • Treat feedback as information about the work, not about you.
  • Review mistakes with: what happened, what I controlled, what next.
  • Pick stretch tasks one step beyond comfort and track progress.

Quick check

Which statement best reflects a growth mindset in practice?

  1. "Anyone can become anything at all if they believe hard enough."
  2. "Some people are just born good at this."
  3. "I can improve at this with practice, better methods and help."
  4. "Effort alone guarantees success."
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. "I can improve at this with practice, better methods and help."

Growth comes from effort combined with good strategies and support, not belief or effort alone.
