Common HR Questions and the STAR Method

Recognise what common HR questions are testing and answer behavioural questions with clear, evidence-based STAR stories. You will build a small bank of real examples you can adapt on the spot.

Last updated 16 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

What HR rounds are looking for

The HR round is not a formality. While the technical or functional panel checks whether you can do the job, HR checks whether you will do it well with other people, stay long enough to be worth training, and handle pressure without creating problems.

Most HR questions fall into a few families: motivation ("Why this company?"), self-awareness ("What is your weakness?"), behaviour ("Tell me about a time you handled conflict"), and practicalities (relocation, notice period, shifts). Once you know which family a question belongs to, it becomes much easier to answer.

Honesty matters more than polish here. HR interviewers hear rehearsed answers all day and are good at spotting them.

The STAR method

Behavioural questions usually begin with "Tell me about a time when…" or "Give an example of…". The idea is that past behaviour is the best clue to future behaviour. STAR gives your story a shape: Situation, Task, Action, Result.

Situation and Task should be short, two or three sentences together, just enough context. Action is the heart of the answer: what you specifically did, using "I" rather than "we". Result closes the story with an outcome, ideally measurable, and one line on what you learned.

Example for "a time you handled a difficult customer": Situation: during my internship at a bank branch, a senior citizen was upset that his pension had not been credited. Task: I had to calm him down and find out what had happened. Action: I took him to a quieter desk, checked his passbook entries with the operations officer, and found that his life certificate had not been updated. I helped him complete it the same day and explained when the credit would reflect. Result: the pension was credited within the week, and he came back to thank the branch manager. I learned that most anger at a counter comes from not knowing what is happening.

Do
  • Spend most of your time on the Action
  • Say "I" for your own contribution
  • Close with a result and a lesson
Don't
  • Spend a minute on background before getting to the point
  • Describe a team success without saying what you did

Handling the tricky regulars

"What is your biggest weakness?" Choose a real, non-critical weakness and show what you are doing about it. "I used to hesitate to speak in large meetings, so I volunteered to present our weekly team update for three months; I am more comfortable now, though I still prepare notes." Avoid disguised boasts such as "I work too hard"; interviewers find them evasive.

"Why should we hire you?" Match two or three of your strengths to what the job description asks for, with one piece of evidence each. Do not compare yourself to other candidates you know nothing about.

"Where do you see yourself in five years?" Show ambition that fits the organisation, for example growing into a team lead or specialist role, without implying you will leave as soon as a government result is declared. If you are still appearing for exams, it is better to be honest when asked directly than to be found out later.

"Why are you leaving your current job?" Keep it brief and forward-looking: growth, learning, a better match for your skills. Never criticise your current employer in detail.

Build your story bank

You cannot predict every question, but you can prepare six to eight real stories that cover the usual themes: a challenge, a conflict, a failure, teamwork, leadership, working under pressure, a mistake you corrected, and going beyond your role.

One good story can answer several questions. The senior-citizen pension story above works for "difficult customer", "problem solving" and "showing empathy". Note each story in STAR form in a notebook and practise telling it in under two minutes.

For the failure question, choose a genuine failure with a real lesson. A story where nothing actually went wrong will not convince anyone, and owning a mistake calmly is itself a sign of maturity.

Do
  • Prepare 6–8 stories covering common themes
  • Include at least one honest failure
Don't
  • Invent stories or borrow a friend's experience
  • Use the same story for every question in one interview

Key takeaways

  • HR questions fall into families: motivation, self-awareness, behaviour and practicalities.
  • STAR keeps behavioural answers focused: short Situation and Task, detailed Action, clear Result.
  • Use "I" to make your own contribution visible.
  • Pick a real weakness and show what you are doing to improve it.
  • A bank of six to eight real stories covers most behavioural questions.

Quick check

In a STAR answer, which part should usually take the most time?

  1. Situation
  2. Task
  3. Action
  4. Result
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Action

The Action shows your own behaviour and decisions, which is what the interviewer is assessing.
