Answering "Tell Me About Yourself"

Build a 60–90 second answer to "Tell me about yourself" that links your background to the role in front of you. You will also learn how to adjust it for campus panels, HR rounds and lateral moves.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

What the interviewer is really asking

"Tell me about yourself" sounds like small talk, but it is usually the first real test. The panel wants to know three things: can you organise your thoughts under mild pressure, do you understand what this role needs, and is there a thread that connects your past to this job?

It is not a request for your life story or a spoken copy of your resume. Your interviewer already has the resume on the table. What they do not have is your explanation of why the pieces fit together and why you are sitting in this chair today.

Treat the answer as a short trailer for the rest of the interview. Whatever you mention here will often become the next question, so you are quietly choosing the topics you want to be asked about.

Do
  • Keep it to 60–90 seconds when spoken aloud
  • Mention two or three points you are happy to be questioned on
Don't
  • Start with your date of birth, hometown or parents' occupations unless asked
  • Recite your resume line by line

A simple structure: Present, Past, Future

A reliable way to organise the answer is Present–Past–Future. Present: who you are right now in one line (final-year B.Com student, two years as a relationship manager, and so on). Past: one or two experiences that built the skills this role needs, with a concrete detail. Future: why this role and this organisation are the logical next step.

Freshers often worry that they have no "past". Projects, internships, college committees, part-time tutoring, a family business you helped with, or the discipline of preparing for a competitive exam all count, as long as you connect them to the job.

Professionals have the opposite problem: too much to say. Pick only the experiences that matter for this specific role. A bank RM moving to a wealth firm does not need to narrate every branch posting; the client portfolio, the review process and one measurable outcome are enough.

Before and after: a fresher's answer

Weak version: "I am Priya, I am from Nagpur. I did my schooling from St. Joseph's and then B.Com from a local college with 72 percent. My father is in government service. My hobbies are reading and listening to music. I am hard-working and a quick learner."

Stronger version: "I recently completed my B.Com from Nagpur University, where I focused on accounting and banking subjects. During my final year I did a two-month internship at a co-operative bank, mainly verifying KYC documents and helping customers fill account-opening forms. That showed me I enjoy work where accuracy and dealing with people go together. Since then I have been preparing for bank exams, which has sharpened my speed with numbers and my awareness of banking news. I am applying for this clerical role because it puts me exactly where I want to start: the front line of customer service in a bank."

The second answer is not longer by much, but every sentence earns its place. It gives the panel concrete hooks such as the internship, KYC and exam preparation, and it ends by pointing straight at the job.

Tailoring for professionals and lateral moves

If you already have work experience, open with your current role and scope, then show progression. For example: "I have spent four years as a relationship manager with a private bank, handling around 150 mass-affluent families. My main work is portfolio reviews, mutual fund and insurance needs analysis, and keeping clients informed when markets are volatile. I have cleared the relevant NISM certifications for distribution. I am now looking for a role where advice and long-term relationships matter more than monthly product targets, which is why this advisory position interests me."

Notice that the reason for moving is framed positively, as moving towards something, not as escaping a bad manager. Interviewers listen carefully for bitterness about a current employer.

If you are changing fields, say so openly and give one bridge: a skill that transfers, a course you completed, or a project that tested your interest.

Practise without memorising

Write your answer once, then reduce it to five or six bullet words: degree, internship, KYC, exam prep, why this role. Practise speaking from the bullets, not the script. A memorised paragraph sounds flat and falls apart if you are interrupted.

Record yourself on your phone and time it. Most first drafts run well over two minutes. Cut adjectives you cannot prove ("dynamic", "passionate") and keep facts you can.

Finally, prepare a 30-second version. Some panels interrupt early, and video interviews often feel slower, so a shorter answer that you can expand on request is useful.

Do
  • Practise from keywords, not a full script
  • End with a clear link to this role
Don't
  • Use self-praise words you cannot back with an example
  • Claim experience or skills you do not have

Key takeaways

  • "Tell me about yourself" is a test of focus and fit, not a request for your biography.
  • Present–Past–Future keeps the answer short and logical.
  • Every point you mention is a likely follow-up question, so choose them deliberately.
  • Freshers can use internships, projects and exam preparation as genuine experience.
  • Frame career moves as moving towards something, never as complaints.
  • Practise from keywords and keep both a 90-second and a 30-second version.

Quick check

An interviewer says, "Tell me about yourself." Which opening is most effective for a fresher applying to a bank?

  1. "I was born and brought up in Pune, where my father teaches in a school."
  2. "I recently graduated in B.Com and interned in a bank's operations team."
  3. "As you can see in my resume, I have done many courses and activities."
  4. "I am a hard-working, honest and dynamic person who learns very quickly."
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. "I recently graduated in B.Com and interned in a bank's operations team."

Opening with your current status and a relevant experience immediately connects you to the role; family details and generic adjectives do not.
