Time Management and Prioritisation

Sort your work by urgency and importance, plan realistic weeks and days, and protect time for focused work. You will also learn how to handle new requests and flag delays without losing trust.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Sort tasks before you start them

Most people do not run out of time; they spend it on whatever shouts loudest. A simple way to stop this is the urgent–important grid. Urgent means there is a near deadline or someone is waiting. Important means the task moves a real goal forward: your targets, your learning, a client's outcome. Every task falls into one of four boxes: urgent and important (do it now), important but not urgent (schedule it), urgent but not important (delegate, batch or keep it short), and neither (drop it).

The trap is the second box. Preparing for a certification, cleaning up a client list or learning Excel properly never feels urgent, so it keeps sliding until it becomes a crisis. People who seem calm at work usually protect time for important-but-not-urgent work before it turns red.

Try it on Monday morning. Write down everything on your plate, then mark each item: A for do now, B for schedule, C for delegate or batch, D for drop. It takes ten minutes and usually reveals two or three things you can simply stop doing.

Plan the week, then the day

A weekly plan answers one question: which three outcomes would make this week a success? Write outcomes, not activities: 'send the revised MIS to the branch head' rather than 'work on MIS'. Put them in your calendar as blocks before meetings fill the week.

Each evening, or first thing in the morning, pick the day's top three from that list and estimate how long each will take. Then add a buffer. Most tasks take longer than expected, especially when they depend on replies, approvals or a system that goes down at month-end. A plan that fills every minute is a wish list, not a plan.

Keep one trusted list, whether a notebook, a notes app or a task tool, instead of scraps spread across WhatsApp, sticky notes and memory. When everything lives in one place, you stop spending energy just remembering what is pending.

Do
  • Write outcomes you can tick off, not vague activities
  • Leave 20–30% of the day unplanned for the unexpected
  • Review your list at the same time every day
Don't
  • Plan your day only from whatever is in your inbox
  • Treat every request as same-day by default

Protect your focus

Deep work, such as drafting a report, reviewing a portfolio or studying for an exam, needs uninterrupted stretches. Every jump to a notification costs several minutes of getting back into the task. Block 60–90 minutes for focused work, mute non-essential groups and keep your phone face down or out of reach.

Batch the small stuff. Instead of answering every email and message the moment it arrives, check them at fixed times, say 10:30, 1:30 and 4:30. Tell your team the pattern so nobody thinks you are ignoring them, and agree on a channel for genuine emergencies, such as a phone call.

Notice your own energy through the day. If you think most clearly in the morning, put analytical work there and keep routine calls and data entry for the post-lunch dip.

Saying 'not now' without saying 'no'

New requests will keep arriving. Accepting all of them silently is how deadlines get missed. The professional move is to make the trade-off visible: 'I can take this up. Right now I'm finishing the branch report due Thursday. Should I pause that, or can this wait until Friday?' You stay helpful, and the person who sets priorities makes the call.

When you cannot meet a deadline, say so early, ideally the moment you know rather than on the due date. Give the reason in one line, a realistic new date and what is already done: 'The data from operations came in late, so the reconciliation will be ready by Wednesday noon instead of Tuesday. The first two sections are complete.' Early warning builds trust; last-minute surprises erode it.

Don't
  • Promise a time you have not checked against your list
  • Wait until the deadline to mention a delay

Key takeaways

  • Sort tasks by urgency and importance before you start working on them.
  • Schedule important-but-not-urgent work before it turns into a crisis.
  • Plan three outcomes for the week and three for the day, with buffer time.
  • Batch messages at fixed times and protect blocks of focused work.
  • Make trade-offs visible when new work arrives, and flag delays early.

Quick check

Your manager wants a client report by 5 pm today, and you also need to prepare for a certification exam six weeks away. Using the urgent–important grid, what is the best approach?

  1. Do the report now; schedule study slots for the coming weeks
  2. Study first, because long-term growth matters more than one report
  3. Alternate between the report and studying every 15 minutes
  4. Leave exam preparation until the final week, when it becomes urgent
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Do the report now; schedule study slots for the coming weeks

The report is urgent and important, so it comes first; the exam is important but not urgent, so it should be scheduled rather than left to chance.
