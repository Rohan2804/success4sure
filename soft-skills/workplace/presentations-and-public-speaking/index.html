Presentations and Public Speaking

Build a presentation around one clear message, design slides that support your listeners, and deliver with confidence. You will also learn to handle tough questions and the unexpected.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Start with the one message

Before opening PowerPoint, write one sentence: what should the audience think or do after your talk? For example: 'The branch should move two relationship managers to SIP renewals this quarter, because lapsed SIPs are our biggest leak.' Everything in the presentation should support that sentence; anything that doesn't is a candidate for cutting.

Next, think about the audience. A regional head wants the conclusion and the numbers behind it. New joiners need context and examples. Clients need plain language and what it means for them. The same data can be framed very differently for each.

A reliable structure for short talks is: the point, two or three reasons, evidence for each, then the point again with a clear next step. Audiences forgive a plain structure; they rarely forgive confusion.

Slides that help, not hide

Slides are there to support the listener, not to hold your script. Aim for one idea per slide, with a headline that states the takeaway, such as 'Lapsed SIPs doubled in Q2' rather than 'SIP data'. Use a simple chart instead of a dense table when you want people to see a trend.

Keep text short enough to read in a few seconds. Put detail in an appendix or handout. Check that numbers match across slides, that units such as lakh, crore and per cent are clear, and that charts are readable from the back of the room.

Before: a slide with fourteen bullet points copied from a report. After: a headline, one chart and a single line of interpretation. The second version asks less of the audience and is far more memorable.

Do
  • Headline every slide with its takeaway
  • Label units and data sources
Don't
  • Read your slides word for word
  • Squeeze a whole report onto one slide

Rehearse and manage nerves

Feeling nervous before speaking is normal, even for experienced presenters. Preparation is the most reliable way to reduce it. Rehearse out loud at least twice, once alone and once with a colleague or a phone recording. Time yourself; most people overrun.

Know your first minute well. A confident opening steadies you for the rest. Arrive early to check the projector, the clicker or your screen-share on a video call. On the day, slow breathing and a glass of water help, and so does looking at a few friendly faces for the first few sentences.

If you lose your place, pause, glance at the slide headline and carry on. A short silence feels much longer to you than it does to the audience.

Delivery and handling questions

Speak a little slower than in conversation, and pause after key numbers so they land. Make eye contact across the room, or look at the camera on video calls. Use your natural voice and a language your audience is comfortable with; you don't need a special 'presentation' style.

Prepare for the three questions you are most likely to get. When asked something, listen fully, repeat or rephrase it if the room is large, then answer briefly. If you don't know, say so and commit to a follow-up: 'I don't have the scheme-wise split with me; I'll share it by tomorrow.' Guessing in front of seniors or clients is far riskier than an honest follow-up.

Close with your main message and a clear next step. The last thing people hear is often what they remember.

Don't
  • Guess answers to appear knowledgeable
  • End with a flat 'That's all from my side'

Key takeaways

  • Write your one-sentence message before you build any slides.
  • Give each slide a headline that states its takeaway.
  • Rehearse out loud and know your opening minute well.
  • Answer questions briefly, and follow up rather than guess.
  • End by repeating your message with a clear next step.
