Handling Client Objections and Difficult Conversations

Handle client concerns about market falls, fees and unsuitable requests with a calm, structured approach. You will learn to stay transparent, avoid promising returns and say no when a request isn't right for the client.

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

A framework for any objection

Objections are normal. A client who questions your recommendation is engaged, not hostile. A simple five-step sequence keeps you calm and useful: Listen, Acknowledge, Clarify, Respond, Confirm. Listen fully without jumping in. Acknowledge the concern: 'It's understandable to feel uneasy when the portfolio is down.' Clarify what lies behind it: 'Is it the overall fall, or a worry about a particular goal?' Respond with facts relevant to their situation. Confirm what happens next and record it.

The aim is not to win the conversation or close a sale. It is to help the client make an informed decision that fits their goals and risk profile, even if that decision is to do nothing or invest less.

Keep notes of significant conversations, especially when a client acts against your suggestion. Clear records protect both the client and you.

When markets fall

During corrections, calls go up and emotions run high. Reach out before clients call you; silence during a fall can feel like abandonment. Bring the conversation back to their plan: goals, time horizon and asset allocation. For a long-term equity goal, a fall may not change the plan at all. For money needed within a year, the conversation is about why short-term needs and volatile assets don't mix, and how to manage the position now.

Never promise recovery dates or returns. Avoid phrases such as 'markets always come back within a year' or 'this fund can't lose money.' Say instead: 'I can't predict when markets will recover. What we can do is check whether your plan still fits your goals and the risk you are comfortable with.' Mutual fund investments are subject to market risk, and clients deserve to hear that plainly.

If a client wants to exit everything, don't pressure them to stay. Explain the trade-offs, such as tax, exit loads, the possibility of missing a recovery and the goal's timeline, then respect their decision once it is informed.

Do
  • Call clients early in a correction
  • Anchor every discussion in the client's goal and time horizon
Don't
  • Predict when markets will recover
  • Use guarantees or 'safe' language for market-linked products

Questions about fees and how you are paid

Clients increasingly ask, 'How do you earn from this?' Treat it as a fair question and answer directly. If you are a mutual fund distributor, explain that you receive commission from the fund house through regular plans, which is reflected in their higher expense ratio, and that direct plans without distributor commission also exist. If you are a registered investment adviser, explain your fee and what it covers.

Then describe the value you provide without overstating it: goal planning, asset allocation, regular reviews, help with paperwork and support in staying disciplined through volatile periods. Let the client decide whether that is worth it to them.

Avoid being defensive or vague. 'Don't worry about that, it's very small' undermines trust; 'Here is what I earn and what you get for it' builds it. Transparency about costs is part of the conduct expected in this industry, not a favour to the client.

Pushy clients and unsuitable requests

Sometimes a client insists on something that doesn't fit them: putting all their retirement savings into a sectoral fund a friend recommended, moving money set aside for a daughter's college admission next year into a small-cap scheme, or asking you to 'just guarantee' a return. Agreeing simply to keep the client happy is mis-selling in all but name.

Say no to the request, not to the person: 'I understand why that fund looks attractive. Since you need this money in twelve months, I can't recommend a high-risk scheme for it. Here's what I'd suggest instead.' Explain the risk in plain language and offer suitable alternatives.

If the client still wants to go ahead with a decision that is legitimately theirs, document that it is against your advice, following your organisation's process. Some requests must be refused outright, including anything that breaks the rules, such as promising returns, splitting transactions to get around KYC requirements or investing in someone else's name without proper authority. Stay polite, hold the line and involve compliance if the pressure continues. Losing one transaction is far better than losing your registration or your integrity.

Don't
  • Agree to unsuitable investments to keep a client happy
  • Bend KYC or documentation rules under pressure

Key takeaways

  • Use Listen, Acknowledge, Clarify, Respond, Confirm for any objection.
  • In market falls, return to the client's goals and never predict recoveries or returns.
  • Explain plainly how you are paid and what the client receives for it.
  • Say no to unsuitable requests, offer alternatives and document the outcome.
  • Refuse rule-breaking requests outright and escalate to compliance if pushed.

Quick check

A client calls during a sharp market fall and asks, 'Should I sell everything?' What is the best first step?

  1. Acknowledge the worry; ask about the goal and timeline
  2. Assure them the market will recover within a few months
  3. Advise them to sell to avoid further losses
  4. Tell them to stop checking their portfolio until things improve
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Acknowledge the worry; ask about the goal and timeline

Understanding the goal and time horizon is the basis for any sound response; predictions and reflex advice are not.
