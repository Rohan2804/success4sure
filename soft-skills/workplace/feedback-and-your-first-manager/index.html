Taking Feedback and Working with Your First Manager

Receive feedback without getting defensive, turn vague comments into specific actions using the SBI model, and build a strong working relationship with your first manager.

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Why feedback feels different at work

During exam preparation, feedback is clear: a mock test score tells you exactly where you stand. At work, comments are quicker, less structured and sometimes delivered in passing, such as 'This needs more work' or 'Be more proactive.'

It is natural to feel defensive, especially if you were a top performer in college or cleared a competitive exam. But early feedback is information, not a verdict on your ability. A manager who takes time to correct you is usually investing in you; the more worrying sign is silence.

Your aim in the first six months is to shorten the gap between doing something and learning how it landed.

Receiving feedback well

When someone gives you feedback, listen to the end without interrupting or explaining. Then ask questions to make it specific. 'Your emails are unclear' becomes useful when you ask: 'Could you show me one email where that happened, and what you would have expected instead?'

A helpful lens is the SBI model: Situation, Behaviour, Impact. If feedback is missing a part, ask for it. Situation: when and where did this happen? Behaviour: what exactly did I do or not do? Impact: what effect did it have on the client, the team or the task? For example: 'In Tuesday's client call (situation), you quoted fund returns without the risk disclosure (behaviour), which could have misled the client (impact).'

Thank the person even if you don't fully agree, and say what you will try next. If you do disagree, take some time, then come back with facts: 'I checked the timeline again. The delay came from the approval step. Could we discuss how I should handle that next time?'

Do
  • Listen fully before responding
  • Ask for one concrete example
  • Say what you will do differently
Don't
  • Explain or justify mid-sentence
  • Argue in the moment when you feel stung

Understanding your first manager

Every manager has preferences about communication, detail and independence. In your first couple of weeks, ask directly: 'How would you like updates, a short daily message or only when something changes?' 'Which decisions can I take on my own, and which should I check with you first?' 'What would a great first three months look like?'

Observe as well. Does your manager reply to long emails, or prefer a quick call? Do they want options or a recommendation? Adapting to these preferences is not flattery; it makes your work easier to use.

Bring a possible solution along with each problem. Instead of 'The client data isn't matching', try: 'The client data doesn't match for 12 folios. It looks like a PAN mismatch. Should I raise it with operations, or would you like to check first?'

Asking for feedback and tracking growth

Don't wait for the annual appraisal. After a significant task, ask one specific question: 'What's one thing I could have done better in that client presentation?' Specific questions get specific answers; 'Any feedback?' usually gets 'All good.'

Keep a simple log of the date, the feedback and what you changed. Over months it shows patterns, perhaps repeated comments about attention to detail, and gives you concrete examples for your review conversation.

Ask for a short monthly check-in about progress and goals. If your manager is very busy, propose a 15-minute slot and bring a brief agenda. Managing upward in this way is a professional skill, not pushiness.

Key takeaways

  • Treat early feedback as information about how your work landed.
  • Use Situation–Behaviour–Impact to turn vague comments into specifics.
  • Ask your manager early how they like updates and what you can decide alone.
  • Bring a possible solution along with every problem you raise.
  • Ask specific feedback questions often and keep a simple log.

Quick check

Your manager says, 'Your reports need to be more detailed.' What is the best follow-up?

  1. Add more pages to every report from now on
  2. Explain why you kept the reports short
  3. Ask for an example and what they expected instead
  4. Ask colleagues whether they agree with the manager
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Ask for an example and what they expected instead

A concrete example shows you exactly what 'more detailed' means to this manager.
