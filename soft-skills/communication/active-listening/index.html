Active Listening: Hear What People Actually Mean

Use attention, clarifying questions and paraphrasing to understand what colleagues and clients actually need. You will learn to listen for concerns behind the words and confirm your understanding before acting.

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Listening is more than waiting to talk

In many conversations we are not really listening; we are preparing our reply. You can spot this in yourself when you interrupt, finish other people's sentences, or jump to a solution before they have finished describing the problem.

Active listening means keeping your attention on the speaker's message until they finish, then showing them you understood. It sounds simple, but it changes outcomes. A client who feels heard is more open to your advice. A manager whose instructions you confirm is less likely to find surprises later.

Start with the basics: put your phone face down, stop typing, and look at the speaker. On a call, close other tabs. Small signals such as "okay", "I see" or a nod show you are following without taking over.

Do
  • Let the speaker finish before you respond
  • Remove distractions such as phone and email
Don't
  • Finish other people's sentences
  • Offer a solution while they are still explaining

Ask questions that open the conversation

Closed questions get a yes or no: "Is the report fine?" Open questions invite detail: "What would you change in the report?" When you are trying to understand a situation, open questions surface information that the speaker might not think to mention.

Useful open questions at work include: "Can you walk me through what happened?", "What matters most to you here?", "What would a good outcome look like?" and "What have you already tried?"

Clarifying questions are just as important. If a manager says "Make the deck more impactful", you could guess, or you could ask, "Do you mean fewer slides, stronger numbers on the first slide, or something else?" One question now saves a second round of revisions.

Do
  • Use "what" and "how" questions to understand needs
  • Clarify vague words like "better", "urgent" or "impactful"
Don't
  • Ask several questions in one breath
  • Use questions to sneak in criticism, like "Why would you do that?"

Paraphrase to confirm understanding

Paraphrasing means restating what you heard in your own words and checking it. For example: "So if I've got this right, the main issue isn't the fee itself but that nobody told you about it before the deduction. Is that correct?"

This does three things. It shows the speaker you listened. It catches misunderstandings early. And it often helps the speaker clarify their own thinking, because hearing their concern played back lets them add what they missed.

Keep paraphrases short and neutral. Avoid repeating their words exactly like a recording, and avoid adding your interpretation too strongly, such as "So you're saying the team is useless." If you got it wrong, that is fine; the speaker will correct you, and you are now closer to the real issue.

Do
  • Start with "So if I understand correctly..." or "Let me check I've got this..."
Don't
  • Paraphrase in a way that changes or exaggerates their meaning

Listen for the concern behind the words

People often state a surface request while the real concern sits underneath. A client who asks "Can I withdraw my money any time?" may actually be worried about a family expense next year. A colleague who says "This deadline is tight" may be telling you they are overloaded with another project.

Notice tone, pauses and repetition. If someone mentions the same point twice, it matters to them. You can gently name what you notice: "You mentioned your daughter's admission twice; is having that money ready a big priority?"

Be careful not to assume. Naming a possible concern as a question gives the person room to agree or correct you. The aim is to understand their situation better, not to read their mind.

Do
  • Notice what people repeat or emphasise
  • Check your guesses as questions
Don't
  • Assume you know someone's feelings or motives

Key takeaways

  • Give full attention: stop typing and put the phone away.
  • Use open questions to learn more and clarifying questions to remove vagueness.
  • Paraphrase briefly and ask if you got it right.
  • Listen for repeated points and the concern beneath a request.
  • Offer solutions only after the speaker feels understood.

Quick check

A client says, "I don't like these market-linked things." What is the best next step?

  1. Ask, "What has made you uneasy about them?"
  2. Explain at length why market-linked products are good
  3. Move straight to recommending a fixed deposit
  4. Say, "Everyone feels that way at first."
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Ask, "What has made you uneasy about them?"

An open question uncovers the real concern, which might be a past loss, a need for liquidity, or something else.
