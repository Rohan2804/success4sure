Delegating Well

Decide what to hand over, brief people so they can deliver, and check progress without micromanaging. You will learn a simple briefing structure and how to match the level of freedom to the person.

Last updated 19 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Why new managers hold on too tightly

Most first-time managers delegate too little, not too much. The usual reasons sound sensible: "It's faster if I do it", "They'll make mistakes", "I don't want to burden them." Each one is true for a single task and wrong over a quarter.

If a weekly report takes you one hour and teaching someone takes three hours once, you get your time back within a month. More importantly, your team learns. People who are never given stretch work stop growing, get bored and start looking at job portals.

A useful test: list everything you did last week. Mark tasks only you can do (such as appraisals or decisions your manager expects from you), tasks someone else could do with a little help, and tasks someone already could do. The second and third lists are your delegation candidates.

Do
  • List last week's tasks and sort them into three groups
Don't
  • Keep a task only because you enjoy it or are good at it

Choose the right task for the right person

Delegation is not dumping. Handing over only the boring work tells people you see them as spare hands. Mix routine tasks with work that builds a skill the person wants, such as presenting to a client or analysing data.

Match the task to both ability and interest. Ritu, who is great with Excel and wants to move into analytics, is a natural fit for building the new dashboard. Faizan, who is new, might start by preparing the data for it while Ritu designs it.

Be careful with tasks that carry high risk, such as a key client renewal or anything involving compliance sign-off. You can still delegate parts of these, but stay closer and agree checkpoints.

Do
  • Link delegated work to someone's growth goals
  • Delegate parts of high-risk work with closer checkpoints
Don't
  • Give away only the dull tasks
  • Delegate a task you cannot explain clearly yourself

Brief so people can actually deliver

Most delegation fails at the handover. "Can you look into the vendor issue?" leaves the person guessing what, why, by when and how much freedom they have.

A good brief covers five things. The outcome: what finished looks like. The why: how it fits the bigger goal. The deadline and checkpoints. The authority: what they can decide alone and what needs your approval, including any spending limit. The resources: people, files and past examples.

Before: "Handle the Diwali gifting for clients." After: "Could you own Diwali gifting for our top 40 clients? Goal is to have gifts delivered by 25 October. Budget is ₹1,500 per client; you can choose the vendor yourself under that. Last year's list and feedback are in the shared drive. Let's review your shortlist on Friday."

Finish by asking them to play it back in their own words. "Just so we're aligned, how are you planning to approach it?" catches misunderstandings early.

Do
  • Cover outcome, why, deadline, authority and resources
  • Ask them to play back the plan
Don't
  • Hand over work in a corridor with one vague line

Match the freedom to the person

There is no single right level of control. Think of a range. At one end: "Do it exactly this way and check with me at each step." In the middle: "Recommend an approach, and go ahead once I agree." At the other end: "Handle it and update me when it's done."

New joiners and new tasks need the first end. Experienced people on familiar work need the last. Problems come when the level does not match: a fresher left alone flounders, while a senior person checked hourly feels insulted.

Say the level out loud so there is no guessing: "For this one, decide the vendor yourself; just loop me in before you sign anything above ₹50,000."

Do
  • State the level of freedom explicitly
Don't
  • Use the same level of control for everyone

Follow up without micromanaging

Agree checkpoints at the start, then use them. A five-minute check on Friday is support; messaging "Any update?" three times a day is micromanagement.

When something goes wrong, resist taking the task back. Ask: "What's blocking you?" and "What options do you see?" Offer help, but leave ownership with them unless the risk is serious.

When the work is done, give credit in public. "Ritu built this dashboard" in the regional review costs you nothing and tells the whole team that delegated work is valued.

Do
  • Use agreed checkpoints
  • Credit the person who did the work
Don't
  • Take the task back at the first problem
  • Present delegated work as your own

Key takeaways

  • Delegating takes time upfront and saves far more later.
  • Mix routine tasks with work that helps people grow.
  • Brief with outcome, why, deadline, authority and resources.
  • Say clearly how much freedom the person has.
  • Check in at agreed points and give public credit.

Quick check

Which is the best brief for delegating a task?

  1. "Please look into the vendor issue whenever you get some free time over the next week or so."
  2. "Sort out the vendor thing for me; you already know what needs to be done."
  3. "Compare three vendors, recommend one by Thursday; you can approve up to ₹50,000."
  4. "Do whatever you think is right about the vendors; no need to update me."
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. "Compare three vendors, recommend one by Thursday; you can approve up to ₹50,000."

It names the outcome, deadline and limit of authority.
