Giving Feedback as a Manager

Give specific praise, useful corrective feedback and handle difficult conversations calmly. You will learn a simple situation-behaviour-impact structure and phrases you can use the same day.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Feedback is part of the job, not an extra

Many new managers avoid feedback because it feels awkward, especially with people who were recently their peers. So they save it all for the annual appraisal. The result is a team member who hears in March about a problem that started in July, and feels ambushed.

Good managers give small, frequent feedback, both positive and corrective, close to the moment it happens. A two-minute conversation on Tuesday about Monday's client call is more useful than a paragraph in a review form months later.

Aim for a healthy balance. If the only time people hear from you is when something goes wrong, they will start to dread your messages. If you only ever praise, your praise loses value and real problems grow.

Do
  • Give feedback within a day or two of the event
  • Give both recognition and correction regularly
Don't
  • Save all feedback for the annual appraisal

Use situation, behaviour, impact

Vague feedback does not help. "Good job" and "You need to be more professional" leave the person unsure what to repeat or change. A simple structure fixes this: describe the situation, the specific behaviour you saw, and its impact.

Corrective example. Before: "You're always careless with client data." After: "In yesterday's update to the Mehta account (situation), the spreadsheet had last quarter's figures (behaviour). The client noticed and asked whether our other numbers were reliable (impact)."

Then move to the future with a question: "What could help you catch this before sending?" Listen to their answer. Often they already know the fix, and a solution they choose is one they will follow.

Describe behaviour, not personality. "You interrupted Neha twice in the meeting" can be discussed; "You're arrogant" only starts an argument.

Do
  • Name the situation, behaviour and impact
  • Ask what they would do differently
Don't
  • Use words like "always", "never" or personality labels

Recognition that actually means something

Praise works best when it is specific and sincere. "Great work, team!" in the WhatsApp group is pleasant but forgettable. "Aarti, the way you calmed down that customer and still collected the documents saved us a complaint. That's exactly the approach I want us to use" tells Aarti and everyone else what good looks like.

Use the same situation-behaviour-impact structure for praise. Recognise effort and good judgement, not only results; someone who followed the right process on a deal that fell through still deserves credit.

Ask people how they like to be recognised. Some enjoy a mention in the team meeting; others would rather get a quiet message or a note to your manager. Make sure recognition is shared fairly and not only to the most visible or vocal people.

Do
  • Make praise specific
  • Recognise good judgement as well as results
Don't
  • Praise only the loudest performers

Correcting in private, and in the right setting

Recognition can be public; correction should almost always be private. Pointing out someone's mistake in front of the team, or in a group chat, makes them defensive and damages trust for everyone watching.

Choose the moment. Do not give difficult feedback just before someone presents to a client or as they are leaving for the day. A quiet meeting room or a call with video on works far better than a long text message, where tone is easily misread.

Keep it short and focused on one or two points. A list of twelve complaints overwhelms people, and they will remember none of it.

Do
  • Correct in private, face to face or on a call
Don't
  • Criticise someone in a team meeting or group chat
  • Give feedback by long text message

Handling a difficult conversation

Some conversations are harder: repeated missed deadlines, behaviour that upsets colleagues, or performance that is putting a target at risk. Prepare for these. Write down the facts with dates, the impact, and what change you need to see.

Open directly and calmly: "I want to talk about the last three deadlines that were missed, because it is affecting the client." Then stop and listen. There may be a reason you do not know about, such as a health issue or a problem at home. Show care without dropping the expectation: "I'm sorry you're dealing with that. Let's work out what support helps, and what we can realistically commit to."

End with clear next steps and a follow-up date, and write a short summary afterwards. If the issue involves harassment, discrimination or anything that may breach company policy, involve HR rather than handling it alone.

Do
  • Prepare facts, impact and the change you need
  • Agree next steps and a follow-up date
Don't
  • Go in without preparing
  • Handle policy breaches like harassment alone

Key takeaways

  • Give small, frequent feedback close to the event.
  • Use situation, behaviour, impact — then ask what they'd do differently.
  • Make praise specific, sincere and fairly shared.
  • Praise in public; correct in private.
  • Prepare for difficult conversations and end with clear next steps.

Quick check

Which is the most useful corrective feedback?

  1. "You need to be more careful with your work, especially with the client reports."
  2. "In Monday's Mehta update the figures were last quarter's, and the client doubted our accuracy."
  3. "You're always making silly mistakes in reports and it reflects badly on us."
  4. "Please improve your attention to detail going forward, as this keeps happening with client work."
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. "In Monday's Mehta update the figures were last quarter's, and the client doubted our accuracy."

It names the situation, behaviour and impact, so the person knows exactly what to fix.
