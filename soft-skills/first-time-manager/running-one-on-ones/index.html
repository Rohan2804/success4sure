Running Useful One-on-Ones

Run regular one-on-ones that your team finds useful rather than a status check. You will learn how to set a rhythm, use good questions and follow through on what you hear.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

What a one-on-one is for

A one-on-one is a short, regular meeting between you and one team member. Its main purpose is not to collect status updates; you can get those from a tracker or a standup. It is the person's time to raise concerns, get help, discuss growth and build a working relationship with you.

Think of it as preventive maintenance. Small issues, such as confusion about priorities, friction with a colleague or a growing interest in another role, surface early here instead of turning into a resignation letter.

A useful rule of thumb: the team member should do most of the talking. If you speak for 25 minutes of a 30-minute slot, it has become your meeting, not theirs.

Do
  • Treat it as their meeting more than yours
Don't
  • Turn it into a project status update

Set a rhythm and protect it

Weekly or fortnightly, for 20 to 30 minutes, works for most teams. Larger teams may need fortnightly; new joiners often benefit from weekly in their first months. Pick a regular slot and put it in both calendars as a recurring meeting.

The most common mistake is cancelling. When a manager moves the one-on-one three times in a row for "something urgent", the message received is "you are not a priority." If you must reschedule, move it rather than cancel, and say why.

In hybrid or field teams, a video call works well. For sales staff on the road, a fixed call slot on the way back from client visits can work. Keep cameras on where possible, and avoid doing it while driving or checking email.

Do
  • Use a recurring slot in both calendars
  • Reschedule rather than cancel
Don't
  • Cancel repeatedly for "urgent" work
  • Check your phone during the meeting

A simple structure

A light structure stops one-on-ones from becoming either aimless chat or a lecture. A shared document with a running list of topics helps; either person can add points during the week.

One easy flow for 30 minutes: first, their topics (about 15 minutes). Then your topics, such as feedback or upcoming changes (about 10 minutes). Finally, actions: who will do what by when (about 5 minutes).

Every few weeks, set aside a session for longer-term topics: career goals, skills they want to build, or how they feel about their workload. These conversations rarely happen unless you plan for them.

Do
  • Keep a shared running agenda
  • End with clear actions and owners
Don't
  • Skip career conversations because "there's no time"

Questions that open people up

"Everything okay?" almost always gets "Yes, all good." Better questions are specific and open. Try a few of these, not all at once.

"What's taking up most of your energy this week?" "Is anything slowing you down that I could help remove?" "What's one thing I could do differently as your manager?" "Which part of your work do you enjoy most right now, and which least?" "Where would you like to be in a year, and what would help you get there?"

After asking, wait. Silence of a few seconds is fine; people often say the most important thing after a pause. When feedback about you comes, thank them and do not argue, even if you disagree. If you get defensive once, you may not hear honest feedback again.

Do
  • Ask open, specific questions
  • Pause and let them think
Don't
  • Ask only "Everything okay?"
  • Argue when they give you feedback

Follow through, and know your limits

What makes one-on-ones valuable is what happens afterwards. Note any action you agreed to, such as "I'll ask IT about your laptop" or "I'll speak to the regional manager about the training seat", and report back next time, even if the answer is no.

Keep what is shared confidential unless there is a safety, legal or policy concern, and be honest about that limit. If someone mentions serious stress, a health issue or a personal crisis, listen with care, but you are not a counsellor. Point them to the right support, such as HR or any employee assistance programme your company offers.

Do
  • Write down your actions and report back
  • Point people to HR or other support when needed
Don't
  • Promise total confidentiality you cannot keep

Key takeaways

  • A one-on-one is the team member's time, not a status update.
  • Hold it regularly and reschedule rather than cancel.
  • Use a light structure: their topics, your topics, actions.
  • Ask open, specific questions and then listen.
  • Follow through on every action you agree to.

Quick check

What is the main purpose of a one-on-one?

  1. To collect detailed status updates on every task and deadline the person has
  2. To check attendance and log-in times
  3. To give the team member time to raise concerns, get help and discuss growth
  4. To review the team's overall targets
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. To give the team member time to raise concerns, get help and discuss growth

Status updates belong in trackers; one-on-ones are for the person.
