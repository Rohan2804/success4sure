Posting on LinkedIn Without the Cringe

Write short, useful LinkedIn posts and comments that sound like you, not like a template. You will learn simple post formats, a four-line structure and the common habits that make posts feel fake.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Why post at all

Many people avoid posting because they fear sounding boastful, or because their feed is full of posts that make them wince. That is understandable. But a few thoughtful posts do something a profile cannot: they show how you think. A recruiter or client who has read your clear explanation of a topic already trusts you a little before you meet.

The goal is not to become an influencer or go viral. The goal is to be useful to a small group of people who care about your field. Ten relevant readers who remember you are worth more than a thousand strangers who scrolled past.

Start small. One post every week or two, plus a few thoughtful comments, is enough to be visible. You do not need to post daily to build a reputation.

Do
  • Aim to be useful to a small, relevant audience
  • Start with one post every week or two
Don't
  • Measure success only by likes
  • Post daily just to stay "active" if you have nothing to say

Three formats that are easy to write

Most good professional posts fit one of three simple formats. The first is "one thing I learned": a lesson from your work or study, with a concrete example. "This week I reconciled 400 entries in Excel. Using XLOOKUP instead of filtering manually cut the time from two hours to twenty minutes. Here's the formula I used."

The second is the plain-language explainer: take a concept from your field and explain it simply. "What is a CIBIL score, and why did my friend's loan get rejected even with a good salary?" The third is the honest recap: what you took away from an event, a book or a course, with one idea you will actually apply.

Each format puts the reader first. You are sharing something they can use, not asking them to admire you.

Do
  • Share a specific lesson with a real example
  • Explain one concept in plain language
Don't
  • Post only announcements about yourself
  • Share content without crediting the original source

A simple four-line structure

A post that is easy to read on a phone usually has four parts. A first line that tells the reader what they will get. Two or three short lines with the main point. One concrete example, number or story. And a closing line, which can be a genuine question or a takeaway.

Before: "Excited and humbled to share some thoughts on my journey of learning and growth in the dynamic world of finance... #blessed #grateful #motivation #success #mondaymotivation". After: "Three things my first month in a bank branch taught me about customer complaints: 1) Most complaints are about waiting, not money. 2) Saying what happens next calms people faster than apologising. 3) Writing it down shows you'll follow up. What would you add?"

Use line breaks generously, two or three relevant hashtags at most, and skip the emoji parade.

Do
  • Make the first line say what the reader gets
  • Use short lines and one concrete example
Don't
  • Open with "Excited and humbled to share"
  • Add a long string of hashtags

The habits that feel cringe

Some patterns make readers roll their eyes. The humblebrag: "I didn't think much of it, but my manager said my presentation was the best she's seen." Invented stories: the taxi driver or the chaiwala who taught you a grand business lesson that never happened. Engagement bait: "Comment YES if you agree" or "Agree?" at the end of an obvious statement.

Others include tagging twenty senior people who were not involved, so they get notified; reposting someone else's post as your own; and turning every personal event into a leadership lesson. Readers notice, and it costs you trust.

A simple test: would you be comfortable saying this out loud to a colleague you respect, in the same words? If not, rewrite it.

Do
  • Read your post aloud before publishing
  • Tag only people who were genuinely involved
Don't
  • Make up stories to illustrate a point
  • End with "Agree?" or "Comment YES"

Comments, confidentiality and criticism

Commenting is the easiest way to start. A useful comment adds an example, a question or a different angle: "We saw the same in our branch; moving KYC checks to the morning cut queues." "Great post!" adds nothing.

Before posting about work, check your employer's social media policy. Never share client names, internal numbers, screenshots of systems or anything said in confidence. If you work in financial services, avoid recommending specific products or promising outcomes in public posts.

If someone disagrees politely, thank them and respond to the point. If someone is rude, you do not owe them a debate; a short, calm reply or no reply is fine.

Do
  • Leave comments that add an example or a question
  • Check your employer's social media policy before posting about work
Don't
  • Share client names, internal data or system screenshots
  • Argue with rude commenters

Key takeaways

  • Post to be useful to a small, relevant audience, not to go viral.
  • Use simple formats: one lesson learned, a plain-language explainer or an honest recap.
  • Structure posts as a clear first line, the point, one example and a closing line.
  • Avoid humblebrags, invented stories, engagement bait and mass tagging.
  • Thoughtful comments count, and confidential work information never belongs in a post.

Quick check

Which opening line is most likely to make a reader keep going?

  1. "Excited and humbled to share my thoughts on this journey!"
  2. "Monday motivation for all my connections out there"
  3. "Three things my first month in a bank branch taught me about complaints:"
  4. "Not sure if anyone will read this, but here goes."
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. "Three things my first month in a bank branch taught me about complaints:"

It tells the reader exactly what they will get from the post.
