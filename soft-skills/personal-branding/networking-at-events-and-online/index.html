Networking at Events and Online

Start conversations at events, send connection requests people accept, and follow up so that a single meeting becomes a lasting professional relationship. You will practise openers, graceful exits and follow-up messages.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Networking is relationships, not collecting cards

Many people picture networking as handing out visiting cards and asking strangers for jobs. No wonder they dread it. A better way to think about it: networking is simply getting to know people in your field and staying in touch, so that you can help each other over the years.

The most useful mindset is to be curious and to give first. Ask people about their work because you are genuinely interested. Share an article, a contact or a tip that might help them. When you later need advice or an introduction, you are asking someone who already knows and likes you.

You do not need hundreds of contacts. Ten to twenty people you are in real touch with, across different companies and levels, can open more doors than a thousand silent connections.

Do
  • Go in curious about other people's work
  • Look for small ways to help first
Don't
  • Open a conversation by asking for a job
  • Judge an event by the number of cards you collected

Before and during an event

Preparation makes events far less awkward. Look at the agenda and the speaker list. Set a small, realistic goal, such as three good conversations. Prepare a one-line introduction: "I'm Ritika, I work in retail credit at an NBFC in Pune, and I'm interested in how small lenders use data."

Openers do not need to be clever. Context works best: "What brought you to this session?" "What did you think of the panel on UPI fraud?" "Is this your first time at this conference?" Then listen, and ask a follow-up question about their answer.

Groups standing in a circle with a gap are usually open; groups facing inward and talking intensely are not. If you join a group, listen for a minute before speaking. Standing near the tea counter is an easy, natural place to start conversations.

Do
  • Set a goal of two or three real conversations
  • Prepare a one-line introduction
Don't
  • Interrupt a closed, intense conversation
  • Spend the whole event with colleagues you already know

Exiting a conversation gracefully

Many people stay stuck in one conversation because they do not know how to leave politely. A clear, warm exit is respectful to both of you. Summarise, thank, and give a reason: "It was great hearing about your work on MSME lending. I'm going to catch the next session, but can I connect with you on LinkedIn?"

Other natural exits include introducing the person to someone else ("Have you met Faisal? He works on the same problem at another bank"), or simply saying you want to meet a few more people today. Nobody is offended by an honest, friendly exit.

Right after each conversation, write a two-line note on your phone: the person's name, what you discussed and anything you promised to send. You will not remember it by evening.

Do
  • Exit with a thank-you and a clear next step
  • Note names and promises immediately after
Don't
  • Disappear mid-sentence or keep checking your phone
  • Promise to send something and forget

Connection requests and follow-ups that work

Whether you met someone in person or found them online, send a short personal note with your connection request. Mention where you met or why you are reaching out. Before: no note, or "I'd like to add you to my professional network." After: "Hi Meera, enjoyed your point on co-lending at today's session in Mumbai. I'd love to stay in touch. – Karan".

For people you have not met, explain the link and keep any request small: "Hi Arvind, I read your post on credit underwriting for gig workers. I'm a final-year MBA student researching the same area. Would you be open to answering two short questions by message?"

Follow up within two days of an event, and send anything you promised. A follow-up that adds value, such as the article you mentioned, is remembered far longer than "Nice to meet you."

Do
  • Add a personal note to every connection request
  • Follow up within two days and send what you promised
Don't
  • Send a sales pitch right after someone accepts
  • Ask a stranger for a job in your first message

Staying in touch without being pushy

Relationships fade if the only time you appear is when you need something. Light, genuine contact every few months keeps them alive: congratulate someone on a new role, comment thoughtfully on their post, or send an article with one line on why it reminded you of them.

A simple spreadsheet or notes app can help. List the people you want to stay in touch with, when you last spoke and one thing you know about their work. Once a month, pick three names and reach out with something relevant.

Online communities count too. Alumni groups, professional association events and focused WhatsApp or Telegram groups for your field are good places to be helpful. Answer questions, share resources and be generous with credit.

Do
  • Reach out every few months with something relevant
  • Keep a simple list of who you want to stay in touch with
Don't
  • Only contact people when you need a favour
  • Forward spam or promotions into professional groups

Key takeaways

  • Networking means building real relationships by being curious and helping first.
  • Prepare a one-line introduction and aim for a few good conversations, not many cards.
  • Leave conversations warmly with a thank-you and a next step.
  • Send personal connection notes and follow up within two days.
  • Stay in light, genuine touch every few months, not only when you need something.
