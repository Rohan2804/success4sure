Asking for Referrals and Introductions

Ask for job referrals and introductions in a way that is specific, easy to say yes to and easy to decline. You will write a forwardable request and learn how to follow up and say thank you.

Last updated 19 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Referrals and introductions are borrowed trust

A referral is when someone inside a company recommends you for a specific role. An introduction is when someone connects you with a person they know, such as a hiring manager, a potential client or a mentor. In both cases, they are lending you their reputation. If you turn out to be unprepared or unprofessional, it reflects on them.

That is why the best requests make the other person's job easy and safe. They know exactly what you want, why you are a reasonable fit, and what to forward. They can also say no without awkwardness.

Remember that a referral usually gets your application looked at; it rarely guarantees an interview, and never guarantees an offer. Treat it as a door opened, not a job secured.

Do
  • Treat every referral as someone's reputation on the line
  • Prepare as seriously as you would for an interview
Don't
  • Assume a referral guarantees a job
  • Ask without understanding what you are asking for

Who to ask, and how well you need to know them

The strongest referrals come from people who have seen your work: a former manager, a project teammate, a professor who guided your dissertation. They can speak about you with specifics.

You can also ask people you know less well, such as alumni from your college or someone you met at an event, but adjust the request. Instead of asking them to vouch for you, ask for information first: "Could you tell me what the team looks for?" If the conversation goes well, they may offer to refer you, or you can ask, "Would you be comfortable referring me, or is there someone better to speak to?"

Avoid mass-messaging every employee of a company. It is easy to spot, and people rarely refer strangers who sent the same note to fifty others.

Do
  • Start with people who have seen your work
  • With weaker ties, ask for information before asking for a referral
Don't
  • Copy-paste the same request to dozens of employees
  • Ask someone to vouch for skills they have never seen

Make the request specific and forwardable

A good referral request includes four things: the exact role and job ID or link, two or three lines on why you fit, your resume as a sensibly named PDF, and a short paragraph they can forward as it is.

Before: "Hi bhaiya, any openings in your company? Please refer me. Resume attached." After: "Hi Rahul, I hope the new role is going well. I'm applying for the Credit Analyst position at your bank in Bengaluru (Job ID 48213). I have two years in MSME loan processing and have built cash-flow models for 150+ files. Would you be comfortable referring me? I've attached my resume and a short note below you can forward. Completely fine if it isn't possible."

That last line matters. Giving an easy way to say no makes people more comfortable, and protects the relationship either way.

Do
  • Include the role, job ID, why you fit and a forwardable note
  • Give an easy way to decline
Don't
  • Ask for "any openings" without a specific role
  • Pressure someone who hesitates

Asking for an introduction

Introductions work best as a "double opt-in": your contact checks with the other person first before connecting you. You can make this easy by writing a short note about yourself and your request that they can forward.

Example: "Hi Priya, could you introduce me to Anil from the treasury team? I'm a CA preparing to move into treasury operations and would value 15 minutes of his perspective on the skills that matter most. Here's a two-line note you can forward. No problem at all if it's not a good time for him."

Once introduced, move your contact to BCC with a thank-you, respond quickly, propose a few time options and keep the call to the time you asked for.

Do
  • Write a short forwardable note for the introducer
  • Respond quickly and keep to the time you requested
Don't
  • Ask for an introduction and then go silent
  • Turn a 15-minute advice call into a job demand

Follow up, report back and return the favour

If you have not heard back in about a week, one polite follow-up is fine: "Just checking whether you had a chance to see my note about the Credit Analyst role. No worries if not." If there is still no reply, let it go gracefully.

Whatever happens, close the loop. Tell the person whether you got an interview and how it went, and thank them even if it did not work out. People who refer you like to know the outcome, and it makes them more willing to help again.

Finally, be the person who helps others. When a junior asks you for a referral, apply the same standards: be honest about what you can do, ask for a clear request, and refer only people you can genuinely speak for.

Do
  • Follow up once, politely, after about a week
  • Tell your referrer the outcome and thank them
Don't
  • Send repeated reminders
  • Disappear once you get the job

Key takeaways

  • A referral lends you someone's reputation, so make it easy and safe for them.
  • Ask people who know your work first; with weaker ties, ask for information before a referral.
  • Give the exact role, job ID, why you fit, your resume and a forwardable note.
  • Always offer an easy way to say no.
  • Follow up once, report the outcome and thank them whatever happens.

Quick check

Which referral request is most likely to be acted on?

  1. "Any openings in your company? Please refer."
  2. A long message describing your entire life story
  3. Your resume with no message
  4. Role, job ID, two lines on fit, resume and a forwardable note
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Role, job ID, two lines on fit, resume and a forwardable note

Specific, ready-to-forward information makes it easy for the referrer to help.
