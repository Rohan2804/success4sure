Spotting Burnout Early

Recognise the early signs of burnout before they pile up, and take sensible first steps. You will learn a simple weekly self-check and what to do when the warning lights come on.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team

Practical everyday advice, not medical advice. If stress, low mood or anxiety lasts for weeks or affects daily life, please talk to a doctor or qualified counsellor.

Stress and burnout are not the same thing

Some stress at work is normal. A quarter-end deadline, a tough client meeting or a big presentation can leave you tense for a few days, and the feeling usually eases once the task is done. Short bursts of pressure can even help you focus.

Burnout is different. It builds slowly when heavy demands continue for months without enough rest, support or sense of control. The World Health Organization describes it as an occupational phenomenon linked to chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. It is not classed as a medical condition, and it is certainly not a personal weakness.

The key word is chronic. One bad week is not burnout. A pattern where weekends no longer refill you, and Monday already feels heavy by Saturday evening, is worth paying attention to.

Do
  • Notice how long a tough patch has lasted
  • Take a heavy feeling that lasts for weeks seriously
Don't
  • Assume every stressful week means burnout
  • Treat burnout as a sign that you are weak

The three warning lights

It helps to watch for three broad patterns. The first is exhaustion: feeling drained most days, even after a full night's sleep or a holiday. The second is distance: becoming cynical or switched off about work, snapping at colleagues, or feeling that nothing you do matters. The third is a drop in effectiveness: tasks that once took an hour now take three, and you make small mistakes you normally would not.

In everyday life these show up in ordinary ways. Priya, a credit analyst in Pune, noticed she was rereading the same email five times and dreading the sound of Teams notifications. Arjun, a fresher at a Bengaluru IT firm, stopped joining team lunches and kept saying "what's the point" about his projects.

None of these signs alone proves anything. Everyone has slow days. What matters is how many signs appear together and how long they last.

Do
  • Look for patterns over weeks, not single bad days
  • Listen when friends or family say you seem different
Don't
  • Dismiss constant exhaustion as laziness
  • Use the signs to label yourself or colleagues

A five-minute weekly self-check

Early signs are easy to miss when you are busy, so make noticing a habit. Every Sunday evening, or on your commute home on Friday, ask yourself four quick questions and rate each from 1 (fine) to 5 (very hard this week).

How drained did I feel on most days? How irritable or detached did I feel about work? How hard was it to concentrate or finish tasks? How little did I enjoy things outside work, such as family, friends, cricket, music or whatever you like?

Write the numbers in your phone's notes app. One high week after a product launch is expected. Scores that keep creeping up for three or four weeks are your signal to act, well before everything starts to feel like too much. The value is in the trend, not in any single number.

Do
  • Keep your scores somewhere private and easy to find
  • Compare this month with last month
Don't
  • Treat the self-check as a test you pass or fail

What to do when the lights come on

Start with what you can change this week. List the two or three things draining you most, perhaps late-night calls, an unclear project or skipping lunch every day, and pick one small fix. Protecting one evening a week or taking a real lunch break is a reasonable first step.

Then talk to someone. A trusted colleague, friend or family member can help you see things more clearly. If workload is the main cause, a calm conversation with your manager is usually more useful than silently pushing harder: "I've been stretched across four projects for two months and my quality is slipping. Can we look at priorities together?"

Burnout is usually about the situation, not about you, so it is fair to ask for changes. And if stress, low mood or anxiety lasts for weeks or starts affecting your sleep, appetite or daily life, it's important to talk to a doctor or a qualified counsellor. In India, the free Tele-MANAS helpline (14416) offers mental-health support in many languages.

Do
  • Pick one small change you can make this week
  • Raise workload with your manager early, with facts
Don't
  • Wait until you are completely exhausted to say anything
  • Try to fix everything on your own

Key takeaways

  • Stress after a deadline is normal; burnout builds from months of unrelieved pressure.
  • Watch for three signs: exhaustion, distance from work and slipping effectiveness.
  • Look at patterns over weeks, not a single bad day.
  • A short weekly self-check helps you spot the trend early.
  • Start with one small change and talk to someone you trust.
  • If low mood or anxiety lasts for weeks, talk to a doctor or qualified counsellor.

Quick check

Which situation sounds most like early burnout rather than ordinary work stress?

  1. Feeling tense the night before a big client presentation
  2. Feeling drained most days for two months, weekends included
  3. Being tired after one late night closing the quarterly accounts
  4. Feeling nervous on your first day in a new team
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Feeling drained most days for two months, weekends included

Burnout is about a long-running pattern; the other options are short, normal reactions to specific events.
