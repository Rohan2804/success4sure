Exchange Traded Interest Rate Futures: NISM Series V-D practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on Exchange Traded Interest Rate Futures, from the NISM Series V-D (Mutual Fund – Specialized Investment Fund (SIF) Distributors) syllabus.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter covers exchange-traded interest rate futures in India, mainly on government securities and Treasury bills. Revise contract design (underlying, quotation, cash settlement), pricing by cost of carry, mark-to-market calculation, hedge ratios using modified duration or PVBP, and the direction of hedges. These futures let debt long-short strategies manage duration and take short positions. Practise computing the number of contracts and daily MTM.

Exchange Traded Interest Rate Futures questions

Question 1

A bond portfolio has a PVBP of ₹30,000. An interest rate futures contract has a PVBP of ₹150. The number of contracts needed to hedge is:

  1. 200
  2. 150, matching the PVBP of one futures contract
  3. 300
  4. 450
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. 200

Hedge ratio = portfolio PVBP / futures PVBP = 30,000 / 150 = 200 contracts. Selling these would offset most of the portfolio's sensitivity to a 1 bp move.

Question 2

A ₹5 crore bond portfolio has a modified duration of 5. A futures contract is worth ₹2 lakh with a modified duration of 6.25. How many contracts hedge the portfolio?

  1. 250, ignoring the different durations of the portfolio and futures
  2. 160
  3. 200
  4. 125
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. 200

Contracts = (5 crore × 5) / (2 lakh × 6.25) = 25 crore / 12.5 lakh = 200. Ignoring durations gives 250.

Question 3

A debt fund expects yields to rise and wants to cut portfolio duration without selling bonds. It should:

  1. Buy IRF, which increases duration when rates move
  2. Buy longer bonds
  3. Do nothing
  4. Sell IRF
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Sell IRF

Short futures carry negative duration, so selling them lowers the portfolio's overall duration and reduces losses if yields rise.

Question 4

A trader buys 4 IRF contracts (face value ₹2 lakh, quoted per ₹100) at 97.80. The next day's settlement price is 97.55. The MTM is:

  1. Gain of ₹2,000, because lower prices mean higher yields
  2. Loss of ₹2,000
  3. Loss of ₹20,000
  4. Loss of ₹200
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Loss of ₹2,000

MTM = 4 × 2,00,000 × (97.55 − 97.80) / 100 = −₹2,000. A buyer loses when the futures price falls.

Question 5

IRF contracts on Indian exchanges are currently mostly settled:

  1. By physical delivery of the cheapest-to-deliver bond from a basket
  2. In gold
  3. In cash
  4. By rolling over automatically
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. In cash

Exchange-traded IRFs in India are cash-settled against a final settlement price derived from the underlying security's market prices.

Question 6

If a G-sec's coupon income exceeds the repo financing rate, its theoretical futures price will be:

  1. Above the spot price by the amount of coupon income received
  2. Equal to face value
  3. Zero
  4. Below spot
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Below spot

Futures = spot + financing cost − coupon income. When coupon income is larger, carry is positive and futures trade below spot.

Question 7

An insurer expects a large inflow in two months to invest in G-secs and fears yields will fall. It can lock in today's yields by:

  1. Selling IRF now and buying the bonds later with the inflow
  2. Buying IRF now
  3. Selling bonds short
  4. Buying a cap
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Buying IRF now

If yields fall, bond prices rise. Long futures gain in that case, offsetting the higher price the insurer will pay for bonds later.

Question 8

Why are IRF positions marked to market daily?

  1. To settle gains and losses each day
  2. To let traders avoid paying any losses until the contract finally expires
  3. To fix the interest rate
  4. To change the lot size
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. To settle gains and losses each day

Daily MTM settles each day's change in value, so losses are paid as they occur. This reduces credit risk for the clearing corporation and participants.
