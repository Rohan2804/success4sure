Strategies Using Interest Rate Futures and Options: NISM Series V-D practice questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter applies interest rate futures and options to hedging, duration management, yield-curve trades and arbitrage, as a debt long-short manager would. Revise when to buy or sell futures, how to change duration with futures, curve steepener and flattener trades, cash-and-carry and reverse arbitrage, and option-based protection. Practise profit and loss computations on futures positions and hedge sizing, and be clear about how each trade performs if yields rise or fall.

Strategies Using Interest Rate Futures and Options questions

Question 1

A trader expects the yield curve to steepen (long yields rising more than short yields). A futures trade that fits this view is to:

  1. Buy long-tenor futures and sell short-tenor futures in equal duration amounts
  2. Buy both tenors
  3. Sell long, buy short tenor futures
  4. Sell only short-tenor futures
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Sell long, buy short tenor futures

If long yields rise more, long-tenor futures fall more. Selling them and buying short-tenor futures profits from the steepening with limited exposure to parallel moves.

Question 2

A debt manager expecting a sharp RBI rate cut wants to increase portfolio duration quickly. He should:

  1. Buy IRF
  2. Sell IRF to reduce the impact of any rate change on the portfolio
  3. Buy floaters
  4. Shift to cash
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Buy IRF

Long futures add duration, increasing gains if yields fall after the rate cut. Selling futures or moving to cash and floaters would reduce duration.

Question 3

A trader sold 20 IRF contracts (₹2 lakh each, quoted per ₹100) at 98.60 and bought them back at 99.10. The result is:

  1. Gain of ₹20,000, as the trader closed the position at a higher price
  2. Loss of ₹20,000
  3. Loss of ₹2,000
  4. Loss of ₹2,00,000
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Loss of ₹20,000

Loss = 20 × 2,00,000 × (99.10 − 98.60) / 100 = ₹20,000. A short position loses when the futures price rises.

Question 4

IRF are trading above fair value based on cost of carry. The arbitrage is to:

  1. Sell the bond short and buy the overpriced futures contract
  2. Buy futures only
  3. Sell both
  4. Buy the bond, sell futures
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Buy the bond, sell futures

Cash-and-carry: buy the bond, fund it in the repo market, and sell the overpriced futures. Convergence at expiry locks in the mispricing.

Question 5

A bond fund hedged with short IRF sees yields fall sharply. The overall result is most likely:

  1. Bond gains offset by futures loss
  2. Gains on both the bonds and the short futures position, doubling the return
  3. Losses on both
  4. No effect on the portfolio
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Bond gains offset by futures loss

Falling yields raise bond prices but also raise futures prices, so the short futures lose. A hedge gives up upside in exchange for protection.

Question 6

A corporate treasurer will borrow in three months at a floating rate and wants protection from higher rates while keeping benefit from lower rates. The best instrument is:

  1. Selling an interest rate floor, which earns premium but offers no protection
  2. An interest rate cap
  3. Buying IRF
  4. A receive-fixed swap
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. An interest rate cap

A cap limits the borrowing rate if rates rise, while the borrower still benefits if rates fall. Buying futures or receiving fixed would gain only if rates fell.

Question 7

A trader buys the near-month IRF and sells the far-month IRF of the same underlying. This is a:

  1. Butterfly spread, involving three different maturities
  2. Basis trade
  3. Collar
  4. Calendar spread
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Calendar spread

A calendar spread uses two expiries of the same contract. Margins are usually lower because the legs offset most price risk.

Question 8

To hedge a bond portfolio with a modified duration of 4 using futures with a modified duration of 8 and equal values, the hedge ratio (futures value to portfolio value) is:

  1. 2.0, the futures duration divided by the portfolio duration
  2. 1.0
  3. 0.5
  4. 4.0
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. 0.5

Hedge ratio = portfolio duration / futures duration = 4 / 8 = 0.5. Selling futures worth half the portfolio's value offsets its rate sensitivity.
