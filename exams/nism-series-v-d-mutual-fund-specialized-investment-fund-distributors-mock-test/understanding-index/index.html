Understanding Index: NISM Series V-D practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on Understanding Index, from the NISM Series V-D (Mutual Fund – Specialized Investment Fund (SIF) Distributors) syllabus.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter explains stock market indices, which are the underlying for index derivatives and benchmarks for funds. Revise index construction methods (free-float market capitalisation, price-weighted, equal-weighted), base value and base date, index maintenance and rebalancing, impact cost and liquidity criteria for inclusion, and the difference between price return and total return indices. Understand why index derivatives are used for hedging portfolios and how index levels move when constituent prices change.

Understanding Index questions

Question 1

In a free-float market capitalisation index, a company's weight depends on:

  1. Total number of employees of the company and its subsidiaries
  2. Face value of its shares
  3. Market value of shares available to the public
  4. Its share price alone
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Market value of shares available to the public

Free-float methodology counts only shares freely available for trading, excluding promoter and locked-in holdings, and multiplies by price to set weights.

Question 2

In a price-weighted index, which stock has the greatest influence on the index?

  1. The highest-priced stock
  2. The company with the largest market capitalisation, regardless of price
  3. The smallest company
  4. The most traded stock
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. The highest-priced stock

Price-weighted indices give more weight to higher-priced shares, regardless of company size. This is why most modern indices use market capitalisation instead.

Question 3

A Total Return Index differs from a Price Return Index because it:

  1. Excludes all dividends and corporate actions from the index calculation
  2. Assumes dividends are reinvested
  3. Uses only bank stocks
  4. Is calculated once a year
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Assumes dividends are reinvested

TRI adds back dividends as if reinvested, so it rises more than the price index over time and is the fair benchmark for funds.

Question 4

Impact cost is used as a criterion for index inclusion because it indicates a stock's:

  1. Long-term profitability and its dividend payout history
  2. Credit rating
  3. Face value
  4. Liquidity
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Liquidity

Low impact cost means large orders can be executed with little price movement. Index providers prefer liquid stocks so the index can be tracked and traded.

Question 5

An index has a base value of 1,000. The current market capitalisation of its constituents is 25 times the base period capitalisation. The index level is:

  1. 25,000
  2. 1,025, adding 25 to the base value
  3. 40
  4. 2,500
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. 25,000

Index = base value × current market cap / base market cap = 1,000 × 25 = 25,000.

Question 6

Why do index providers periodically rebalance indices?

  1. To guarantee that the index rises every year
  2. To keep the index representative
  3. To raise brokerage income for exchanges
  4. To fix the prices of constituent stocks
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. To keep the index representative

Rebalancing replaces stocks that no longer meet criteria and adjusts weights, so the index continues to represent its intended market segment.

Question 7

An equal-weighted index of 50 stocks will, compared with its market-cap-weighted version, typically:

  1. Give exactly the same weight to each stock as the market-cap-weighted version
  2. Have no volatility
  3. Contain only large banks
  4. Give more weight to smaller companies
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Give more weight to smaller companies

Equal weighting gives every stock 2%, so smaller companies get relatively more weight than in a cap-weighted index, often increasing volatility.

Question 8

Index futures are widely used to hedge diversified equity portfolios because:

  1. Index futures eliminate both systematic and stock-specific risk completely
  2. Index futures need no margin
  3. Portfolios largely move with the index
  4. Index futures never expire
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Portfolios largely move with the index

A diversified portfolio's risk is mostly market risk, which index futures can offset. Stock-specific risk remains.
