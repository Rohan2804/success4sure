What this chapter covers

This chapter covers two ways in which ownership changes without a regular public issue. Disinvestment is the government's sale of its stake in public sector enterprises through methods such as IPOs, offer for sale through stock exchanges, strategic sale and ETFs, managed by DIPAM. Buyback is a company repurchasing its own shares under the Companies Act, 2013 and SEBI (Buy-back of Securities) Regulations, through permitted routes such as tender offer. Revise sources and limits of buyback, approvals needed, debt-equity conditions, gaps between buybacks and how shares bought back are treated.

Disinvestment & Buyback questions

Question 1

A company's paid-up capital is ₹200 crore and its free reserves are ₹800 crore. What is the maximum buyback it can make with only board approval?

  1. ₹250 crore
  2. ₹200 crore
  3. ₹100 crore
  4. ₹80 crore
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. ₹100 crore

With board approval alone, a buyback is limited to 10% of paid-up capital plus free reserves: 10% × (₹200 + ₹800) crore = ₹100 crore. A special resolution allows up to 25%, which would be ₹250 crore.

Question 2

What happens to shares bought back by a company?

  1. They are held as treasury shares and resold later
  2. They are transferred to the promoters free of cost
  3. They are given to employees as bonus shares
  4. They are extinguished within the prescribed time
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. They are extinguished within the prescribed time

Shares bought back must be extinguished within the time prescribed; Indian law does not allow companies to hold them as treasury stock for resale. They cannot be passed on to promoters or employees.

Question 3

In which method of disinvestment does the government sell a significant stake along with management control to a buyer?

  1. Strategic sale
  2. Offer for sale through stock exchange
  3. Exchange traded fund route
  4. Minority stake sale in an IPO
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Strategic sale

A strategic sale transfers a substantial stake and management control to a strategic buyer. OFS, ETFs and minority IPO sales reduce the government's holding without handing over control.

Question 4

A company can fund a buyback of its equity shares out of:

  1. Proceeds of an earlier issue of the same kind of equity shares
  2. Free reserves, securities premium or other-kind issue proceeds
  3. Borrowed funds that take its debt to five times equity
  4. Money received as share application money pending allotment
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Free reserves, securities premium or other-kind issue proceeds

The Companies Act permits buyback out of free reserves, securities premium or proceeds of any shares or specified securities other than those of the same kind. Using proceeds of the same kind of shares is prohibited.

Question 5

Under the current tax rules effective from October 2024, money received by a resident shareholder on a buyback of shares is:

  1. Fully exempt in the hands of the shareholder
  2. Treated as a dividend and taxed in the shareholder's hands
  3. Taxed only in the company's hands at a flat rate
  4. Taxed as long-term capital gain at 10%
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Treated as a dividend and taxed in the shareholder's hands

From 1 October 2024, buyback proceeds are treated as deemed dividend taxable in the shareholder's hands, with the cost of shares treated as a capital loss. Earlier, the company paid buyback tax and holders were exempt.

Question 6

The government sells a portion of its holding in a listed PSU through the stock exchange's dedicated window for promoters. This is called:

  1. Qualified institutions placement
  2. Rights issue
  3. Offer for sale through the exchange
  4. Preferential allotment
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Offer for sale through the exchange

The OFS mechanism lets promoters, including the government, sell shares of listed companies through a separate exchange window. QIPs, rights issues and preferential allotments involve the company issuing fresh shares.

Question 7

Which of the following is a reason a company may undertake a buyback?

  1. To return surplus cash to shareholders
  2. To increase the number of shares outstanding
  3. To raise fresh capital for expansion
  4. To reduce its free reserves to zero by law
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. To return surplus cash to shareholders

Buybacks return excess cash, reduce share count and can raise EPS and return on equity. They reduce, not increase, shares outstanding and do not raise new capital.

Question 8

Units of the CPSE ETF and the Bharat 22 ETF, sold to investors by the government, represent which disinvestment method?

  1. Strategic sale with transfer of control
  2. Buyback of shares by each CPSE
  3. Rights issue by the government
  4. Exchange traded fund route
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Exchange traded fund route

Through the ETF route, the government sells its holdings in a basket of CPSEs to an ETF, whose units investors buy. This divests stakes across many companies without transferring control, unlike a strategic sale.
