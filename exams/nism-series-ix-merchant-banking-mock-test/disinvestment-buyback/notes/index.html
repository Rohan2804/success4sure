Disinvestment & Buyback: NISM Series IX revision notes

This chapter covers two ways ownership changes without a regular public issue. Disinvestment is the government selling its stake in public sector companies; buyback is a company purchasing its own shares. Expect conceptual questions on disinvestment methods and numerical questions on buyback limits, approvals and conditions under the Companies Act and SEBI Buy-back Regulations.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Disinvestment and DIPAM

Disinvestment means the government reducing its shareholding in central public sector enterprises. The Department of Investment and Public Asset Management (DIPAM) under the Ministry of Finance manages it. Objectives include raising resources, improving efficiency, broadening ownership and promoting market discipline.

2. Methods of disinvestment

Methods include IPOs and FPOs of CPSEs, offer for sale through the stock exchange mechanism, strategic sale with transfer of management control to a buyer, exchange traded funds such as CPSE ETF and Bharat 22 ETF, buybacks by CPSEs, and minority stake sales to institutions. Strategic sales involve a bidding process and transfer of control.

3. OFS through stock exchanges

Promoters of eligible listed companies, including the government, can sell shares through a separate exchange window over one or two days. A minimum 10% of the offer is reserved for retail investors, who may get a discount, and a portion for mutual funds and insurers. The seller announces a floor price in advance.

4. Buyback routes

Under the SEBI Buy-back Regulations, a listed company can buy back shares through a tender offer to existing shareholders on a proportionate basis. The open market route through stock exchanges has been phased out since April 2025, so tender offers are now the main route (check current rules). Odd-lot buybacks are rare.

5. Sources of buyback

Buyback can be funded only from free reserves, the securities premium account or proceeds of an issue of other securities, but not from proceeds of an earlier issue of the same kind of shares. When funded from free reserves, an amount equal to the nominal value of shares bought back is transferred to the capital redemption reserve.

6. Limits and approvals

A buyback up to 10% of paid-up capital and free reserves needs only a board resolution; up to 25% needs a special resolution of shareholders. Equity shares bought back in a financial year cannot exceed 25% of paid-up equity capital. The company's post-buyback debt-equity ratio must not exceed 2:1 (with specified exceptions).

7. Gaps and restrictions

No fresh buyback offer can be made within one year of the previous one. After a buyback, the company cannot issue the same kind of shares for a specified period (except bonus or conversions). Promoters must disclose their intention to participate in tender offers.

8. Extinguishment

Shares bought back must be extinguished and physically destroyed (or the demat holding extinguished) within the prescribed period, generally seven days of completion. They cannot be reissued, which permanently reduces share capital.

9. Worked example

A company has paid-up capital ₹100 crore and free reserves ₹400 crore. Board approval alone permits a buyback up to ₹50 crore (10% of ₹500 crore); with a special resolution, up to ₹125 crore (25%). The 2:1 debt-equity test must also hold after the buyback reduces equity.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Board-approved buyback limit = 10% × (paid-up capital + free reserves); special-resolution limit = 25% × (paid-up capital + free reserves).

Common traps in the exam

  • Confusing OFS with an IPO. An OFS through exchanges is a sale of existing shares by promoters, bringing no fresh money to the company.
  • Funding a buyback from a fresh issue of the same kind of shares. That is not permitted.
  • Applying the 25% limit only to capital. It is 25% of paid-up capital plus free reserves, and separately 25% of equity shares in a year.
  • Forgetting the one-year gap between buybacks.

Quick recap

Disinvestment is managed by DIPAM via IPO/FPO, OFS through exchanges (10% retail), strategic sale and ETFs. Buyback: tender offer route, funded from free reserves, securities premium or other securities. Up to 10% with board approval, up to 25% with special resolution; post-buyback debt-equity ≤ 2:1; one-year gap; shares extinguished promptly.
