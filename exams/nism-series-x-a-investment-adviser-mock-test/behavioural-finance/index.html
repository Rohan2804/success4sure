Behavioural Finance: NISM Series X-A practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on Behavioural Finance, from the NISM Series X-A (Investment Adviser (Level 1)) syllabus.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter explains how psychology affects investment decisions. It covers heuristics and biases such as anchoring, mental accounting, overconfidence, herding, loss aversion, confirmation bias, framing, home bias and endowment effect, and the limits of the efficient-markets view. Advisers must recognise these biases in clients and in themselves and use process, education and goal-based planning to reduce costly mistakes. When revising, match each bias to a typical client statement or behaviour.

Behavioural Finance questions

Question 1

A client refuses to sell a stock until it returns to the price he paid, ₹500, although its prospects have changed. This shows:

  1. Herding
  2. Home bias
  3. Anchoring
  4. Overconfidence
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Anchoring

Anchoring is fixation on a reference point, here the purchase price, which is irrelevant to the stock's future value. Decisions should be based on current prospects.

Question 2

A client treats her bonus as 'fun money' and spends it on speculative trades while keeping her salary savings very conservative. This is:

  1. Loss aversion
  2. Recency bias
  3. Status quo bias
  4. Mental accounting
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Mental accounting

Mental accounting is treating money differently depending on its source or label, although all rupees are interchangeable. A unified portfolio view leads to better decisions.

Question 3

Investors rush to buy a theme because everyone around them is buying it. This behaviour is:

  1. Anchoring
  2. Herding
  3. Framing
  4. Endowment effect
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Herding

Herding is following the crowd instead of independent analysis, which can inflate bubbles. It often leads to buying near peaks and selling near troughs.

Question 4

A trader believes his stock picks are far better than average and trades very frequently. This reflects:

  1. Overconfidence
  2. Mental accounting
  3. Loss aversion
  4. Home bias
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Overconfidence

Overconfident investors overrate their knowledge and skill, leading to excessive trading and under-diversification, which tends to reduce net returns after costs.

Question 5

A client reads only news that supports his view that a stock will rise and ignores negative reports. This is:

  1. Recency bias
  2. Anchoring
  3. Herding
  4. Confirmation bias
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Confirmation bias

Confirmation bias is seeking information that confirms existing beliefs and ignoring contrary evidence. Advisers can counter it by presenting balanced analysis.

Question 6

A client prefers a plan described as having a '90% chance of meeting the goal' over one with a '10% chance of failing', though they are identical. This is:

  1. Mental accounting
  2. Overconfidence
  3. Framing
  4. Disposition effect
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Framing

Framing means decisions change with how identical information is presented. Advisers should present information neutrally and in several ways so the client's choice is not driven by wording.

Question 7

Loss aversion means that investors:

  1. Feel losses more than equal gains
  2. Prefer losses to gains of the same size
  3. Are indifferent between gains and losses
  4. Avoid all equity investments by law
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Feel losses more than equal gains

Prospect theory shows a loss hurts roughly twice as much as an equal gain pleases. This can make investors overly cautious or reluctant to realise losses.

Question 8

Which adviser action best helps clients avoid behavioural mistakes during a market crash?

  1. Switching the whole portfolio to cash immediately
  2. Revisiting the goal-based plan
  3. Recommending a new hot sector fund
  4. Avoiding any contact until markets recover
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Revisiting the goal-based plan

Reminding clients of their long-term goals, allocation and past recoveries reduces panic selling. Knee-jerk moves or silence tend to worsen outcomes.
