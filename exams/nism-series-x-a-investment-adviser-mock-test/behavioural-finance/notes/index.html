Behavioural Finance: NISM Series X-A revision notes

Clients are not perfectly rational, and their biases often cost more than poor fund selection. This chapter covers the main behavioural biases, how they appear in client behaviour, and how advisers can counter them. Questions describe a client's action and ask you to name the bias.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Why behaviour matters

Traditional finance assumes investors are rational and markets efficient. Behavioural finance shows that emotions and mental shortcuts lead to systematic errors - buying high, selling low and holding losers. An adviser's job includes protecting clients from their own reactions. Good process reduces such errors.

2. Disposition effect

Investors tend to sell winners quickly to lock in gains but hold losers hoping to break even. A client who sells a stock up 20% but keeps one down 40% 'until it recovers' shows this bias, which is linked to loss aversion and regret avoidance.

3. Loss aversion

Losses feel about twice as painful as equivalent gains feel good, according to prospect theory. This makes clients avoid equity altogether after one bad experience, or refuse to sell a loss-making investment even when the original reason for holding has disappeared.

4. Recency and extrapolation

Recency bias gives too much weight to recent events. After a year of very high equity returns, a client may insist on sharply raising equity allocation because 'markets always go up', ignoring long-term averages and the risk of a correction.

5. Herding

Herding means following the crowd - investing in a hot IPO, a popular theme or crypto because friends and social media are doing it. Herd behaviour feeds bubbles and crashes. Advisers should anchor decisions to the client's own plan. Many clients enter just before peaks.

6. Overconfidence and illusion of control

Overconfident investors overestimate their knowledge and trade too much, increasing costs and taxes. Those with an illusion of control believe active monitoring improves outcomes. Both are common among successful professionals managing their own money. Frequent trading in a demat account and concentrated bets on familiar companies are typical signs that an adviser should discuss.

7. Anchoring and mental accounting

Anchoring is fixating on a reference point, like a stock's past high or purchase price. Mental accounting treats money differently by source - spending a bonus freely while borrowing on a credit card at 36%. Both lead to inconsistent decisions.

8. Countering biases

Written investment policies, goal-based buckets, automatic SIPs, scheduled rebalancing and pre-agreed rules for selling help clients avoid emotional decisions. Advisers should explain likely drawdowns in advance so clients are not surprised when they happen. Reviewing past decisions with the client, including the mistakes, builds better habits and trust over the long term.

Common traps in the exam

  • Confusing the disposition effect (sell winners, hold losers) with herding (following others).
  • Labelling a client who raises equity after a great year as 'overconfident' when the question points to recency bias.
  • Treating loss aversion as mere risk aversion; it is the asymmetric pain of losses versus gains.
  • Thinking advisers are immune to biases; they need processes too.

Quick recap

Biases cost clients money. Disposition effect: sell winners, hold losers. Loss aversion: losses hurt about twice as much. Recency: extrapolating recent returns. Herding: following the crowd. Overconfidence: excessive trading. Anchoring and mental accounting distort decisions. Counter them with written plans, SIPs, rebalancing rules and expectation setting.
