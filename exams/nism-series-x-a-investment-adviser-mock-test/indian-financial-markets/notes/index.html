Indian Financial Markets: NISM Series X-A revision notes

This chapter maps India's financial system: the markets, the instruments in each and the regulators who oversee them. Questions ask which regulator governs a market or product and whether an instrument is a money market or capital market security. Clear categorisation is the key to scoring here.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Role of financial markets

Financial markets channel savings from households to businesses and governments that need capital. They provide price discovery, liquidity and risk transfer. Efficient markets lower the cost of capital and give savers access to a wide range of risk-return choices. Without them, capital would sit idle in households.

2. Money market

The money market deals in short-term funds with maturities up to one year: call money, treasury bills, commercial paper, certificates of deposit, repos and TREPS. Participants are mainly banks, primary dealers, mutual funds and large companies. RBI regulates it and uses it to transmit monetary policy.

3. Capital market

The capital market deals in long-term funds: equity shares, corporate bonds, government securities and their derivatives. It has a primary market for new issues and a secondary market for trading existing securities on stock exchanges. SEBI regulates most of it; RBI regulates the government securities market.

4. Call money and treasury bills

Call money is overnight interbank borrowing and lending, governed by RBI. Treasury bills are short-term government securities issued at a discount in 91, 182 and 364-day maturities. Their yields indicate short-term interest rates in the economy. T-bills are popular with liquid funds and corporate treasuries for parking short-term cash.

5. Regulators

RBI regulates banks, NBFCs, money and forex markets and payment systems. SEBI regulates securities markets, mutual funds, brokers and investment advisers. IRDAI regulates insurance; PFRDA regulates NPS and pension funds. IFSCA regulates financial services in GIFT City IFSC. A product can involve more than one regulator.

6. Financial intermediaries

Banks, NBFCs, mutual funds, insurers, brokers, depositories and investment advisers all act as intermediaries. Each operates under its regulator's licence. Advisers should know which regulator governs a product so they can guide clients on documents, disclosures and grievance routes. This also tells clients where to escalate complaints.

7. Monetary policy link

RBI's Monetary Policy Committee sets the repo rate. A cut usually lowers money market and bond yields, raising bond prices; a hike does the opposite. Advisers track policy to position clients' debt portfolios on duration. Long duration funds gain the most when yields fall, and lose the most when they rise.

8. Government securities market

G-secs are issued by RBI on behalf of the central government through auctions. Retail investors can buy them directly through RBI Retail Direct or through exchanges. They carry no credit risk but do carry interest rate risk. State development loans are issued by states.

Common traps in the exam

  • Classifying a five-year corporate bond as a money market instrument - money market covers maturities up to one year.
  • Naming SEBI as the regulator of the call money market; RBI governs it.
  • Assuming government securities are free of all risk; they carry interest rate risk.
  • Mixing up IRDAI (insurance) and PFRDA (pension) jurisdiction over NPS.

Quick recap

Money market = up to one year (call money, T-bills, CP, CD, repo), regulated by RBI. Capital market = long term (shares, bonds), mostly SEBI, with G-secs under RBI. Regulators: RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, PFRDA, IFSCA. Repo rate moves feed into bond yields. G-secs have no credit risk but do have rate risk.
