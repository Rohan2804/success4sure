Client Financial Position & Debt Management: NISM Series X-A revision notes

Before advising, an adviser must understand where the client stands today. This chapter covers building a personal balance sheet and cash flow statement, key ratios, and how to manage debt sensibly. Questions often give a client's numbers and ask for net worth, a ratio, or the better debt decision.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Personal balance sheet

Assets minus liabilities equals net worth. A client with a house worth ₹60 lakh, mutual funds ₹15 lakh, bank balances ₹5 lakh and a car ₹5 lakh has assets of ₹85 lakh. With a ₹35 lakh home loan, net worth is ₹50 lakh.

2. Types of assets

Advisers separate personal-use assets (home, car, jewellery worn) from investment assets (equity, debt, investment property) and liquid assets (cash, savings, liquid funds). Only investment and liquid assets can fund goals without lifestyle disruption. A client with ₹1 crore net worth may have only ₹10 lakh truly investable once the home and car are excluded.

3. Cash flow statement

Monthly or yearly income minus expenses shows the surplus available for investing. Separating fixed obligations (EMIs, rent, premiums) from discretionary spending shows where savings can be increased. A negative surplus signals a need to restructure before investing. Annual items like school fees need monthly provisioning.

4. Liquidity ratio

Liquid assets divided by monthly expenses shows how many months the client can survive without income. A ratio of three to six is a common rule of thumb; self-employed clients or single-income families may need more. Liquid funds and sweep FDs can hold this reserve.

5. Debt service ratio

Total EMIs divided by gross monthly income measures loan burden. A client earning ₹1,20,000 with EMIs of ₹30,000 and ₹6,000 has a ratio of 30%. Lenders and planners often treat 35% to 40% as an upper comfort zone, though it varies.

6. Savings ratio

Savings divided by income shows discipline. A household saving ₹25,000 of ₹1,00,000 monthly income has a 25% savings ratio. Rising income with a flat savings ratio usually means lifestyle inflation, a common issue for mid-career clients. Advisers often set target savings ratios by goal.

7. Good and bad debt

Debt that finances appreciating or income-producing assets at reasonable rates, like a home loan or education loan, can be productive. High-cost consumer debt - credit card revolving balances at around 36% a year or personal loans for consumption - erodes wealth.

8. Prioritising repayment

Repay the costliest debt first. A client with a credit card balance at 36% and an FD earning 7% should usually break the FD to clear the card: every rupee repaid 'earns' 36% risk-free. The debt avalanche method targets the highest rate first.

9. Prepayment decisions

Prepaying a home loan at 8.5% makes sense if expected post-tax investment returns are lower or the client values being debt-free. Tax benefits on home loan interest (where available under the chosen tax regime) lower the effective cost and should be factored in.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Net worth = Total assets - Total liabilities
  • Debt service ratio = Total EMIs / Gross monthly income
  • Liquidity ratio = Liquid assets / Monthly expenses

Common traps in the exam

  • Counting a car or self-occupied home as an investment asset available for goals.
  • Keeping a low-yield FD while paying high credit card interest - clearing the card usually wins.
  • Using net income in the debt service ratio when the question specifies gross income, or vice versa.
  • Assuming all debt is bad; productive debt at reasonable cost can build assets.

Quick recap

Net worth = assets minus liabilities; separate personal-use, investment and liquid assets. Cash flow shows investable surplus. Liquidity ratio of three to six months; debt service ratio ideally under about 40%. Repay costliest debt first, especially credit cards. Prepay loans when effective cost exceeds expected post-tax returns.
