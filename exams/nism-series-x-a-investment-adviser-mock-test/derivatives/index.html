Derivatives: NISM Series X-A practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on Derivatives, from the NISM Series X-A (Investment Adviser (Level 1)) syllabus.

What this chapter covers

This chapter introduces derivatives for an investment adviser: forwards, futures, options and swaps, their payoffs and their use in hedging and portfolio management. It covers margins, leverage, option terms such as strike, premium, intrinsic value and moneyness, and simple strategies like protective puts and covered calls. When revising, focus on how derivatives can reduce or increase risk, compute simple payoffs, and be able to judge whether a derivative strategy is suitable for a given client.

Derivatives questions

Question 1

A client holds shares at ₹800 and buys a put with strike ₹760 for ₹15. If the share falls to ₹650 at expiry, his net loss per share including premium is:

  1. ₹150
  2. ₹55
  3. ₹40
  4. ₹165
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. ₹55

The put limits the sale price to ₹760, so the stock loss is 800 − 760 = ₹40, plus the ₹15 premium = ₹55. Without the put, the loss would have been ₹150.

Question 2

Why is futures trading considered leveraged?

  1. Only a margin is paid for the full exposure
  2. The exchange lends shares to every trader free of cost
  3. Futures prices cannot move more than 1% a day
  4. The full contract value must be paid upfront in cash
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Only a margin is paid for the full exposure

With futures, a margin that is a fraction of contract value gives exposure to the full value, so small price moves cause large gains or losses relative to the money deposited.

Question 3

An interest rate swap typically involves:

  1. Buying shares in exchange for bonds
  2. Converting rupees into dollars at a fixed rate permanently
  3. Exchanging fixed-rate for floating-rate payments
  4. Swapping one mutual fund scheme for another
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Exchanging fixed-rate for floating-rate payments

In a plain interest rate swap, one party pays a fixed rate and the other a floating rate on a notional amount, with only net interest exchanged. It helps manage interest rate exposure.

Question 4

A call option with strike ₹1,000 when the stock is at ₹1,080 is:

  1. Out of the money
  2. At the money
  3. Worthless at any time
  4. In the money
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. In the money

A call is in the money when the spot price exceeds the strike, since exercise would buy below market. Here intrinsic value is ₹80.

Question 5

A client with no shares sells call options to earn premium. What is his risk?

  1. Loss limited to the premium received
  2. Potentially unlimited losses if the stock rises
  3. No risk, since he receives money upfront
  4. Loss limited to the strike price
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Potentially unlimited losses if the stock rises

A naked call writer must deliver at the strike if exercised, so losses grow without limit as the stock rises. The premium received is his maximum gain, not his maximum loss.

Question 6

For a retail client seeking capital protection, an adviser considering derivatives should first check:

  1. Which derivative offers the highest leverage
  2. The latest tips on social media
  3. Whether the client understands and can bear the risks
  4. Whether the broker offers free trades
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Whether the client understands and can bear the risks

Suitability requires that the client understands the product and can bear potential losses. Leverage, tips or brokerage offers are not appropriate grounds for recommending derivatives.

Question 7

An investor sells index futures against a diversified portfolio. If the market rises sharply, the futures position will:

  1. Lose money, offsetting portfolio gains
  2. Gain money, adding to portfolio gains
  3. Be unaffected by market movement
  4. Be automatically closed by SEBI
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Lose money, offsetting portfolio gains

A short futures hedge loses when the market rises, offsetting gains on the portfolio. Hedging reduces both downside and upside, locking in the current value.

Question 8

A forward contract differs from a futures contract in that a forward is:

  1. Traded on an exchange with daily margins
  2. Always settled in cash through a clearing corporation
  3. Available only on stock indices
  4. A private, customised OTC agreement
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. A private, customised OTC agreement

Forwards are bilateral and customised, with counterparty risk and no daily margining. Futures are standardised, exchange-traded and cleared by a clearing corporation.
