Derivatives: NISM Series X-A revision notes

X-A does not expect trading expertise, but advisers must explain derivatives, recognise when they help a client and when they add risk. This chapter covers futures and options basics, payoffs and common hedging uses. Expect a payoff calculation and scenario questions about protecting a portfolio.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. What derivatives are

Derivatives are contracts whose value depends on an underlying asset such as shares, an index, currencies, commodities or interest rates. In India, equity derivatives trade on NSE and BSE under SEBI regulation; currency and interest rate derivatives involve RBI as well.

2. Futures versus options buyer

A futures buyer is obliged to buy at the agreed price and faces unlimited gains or losses with daily mark-to-market. An option buyer pays a premium for the right, not the obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put); the maximum loss is the premium.

3. Call option payoff

An investor buys a call with a strike of ₹500 for a ₹20 premium. If the stock is at ₹540 at expiry, the payoff is 540 - 500 - 20 = ₹20 per share. If it ends at or below ₹500, the call expires worthless and the loss is ₹20.

4. Put option payoff

A put with strike ₹500 bought for ₹15 is profitable below the break-even of ₹485. If the stock falls to ₹450, profit is 500 - 450 - 15 = ₹35 per share. Puts act like insurance on holdings. Maximum profit is ₹485 if the price hits zero.

5. Protecting a client's portfolio

A client with a diversified equity portfolio who fears a sharp fall but wants to keep upside can buy index put options. Selling index futures also hedges, but it removes upside as well. The put costs a premium, much like an insurance policy.

6. Leverage and risk

Derivatives require only a margin, so small price moves create large gains or losses relative to capital. SEBI studies show most individual traders in equity F&O lose money. Advisers should be cautious about recommending derivatives for speculation to retail clients.

7. Forwards and swaps

Forwards are customised OTC contracts often used by exporters and importers through banks to fix exchange rates. Interest rate swaps exchange fixed and floating payments and are used mainly by companies and banks to manage rate exposure. Retail clients rarely need these directly.

8. Derivatives in products

Arbitrage funds use futures to lock in cash-futures spreads; some PMS and AIF strategies use options; market-linked debentures embed options. Advisers must understand the embedded derivative to explain how the product's payoff and risk really work. Market-linked debentures can be particularly complex to evaluate.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Call payoff = max(0, S - K) - premium
  • Put payoff = max(0, K - S) - premium

Common traps in the exam

  • Saying an option buyer must buy at expiry - the buyer has a right, the seller has the obligation.
  • Recommending futures selling when the client wants protection but also wants to keep upside - buying puts fits better.
  • Ignoring the premium when calculating option profit.
  • Treating derivative trading as a suitable income source for an inexperienced retail client.

Quick recap

Derivatives derive value from an underlying. Futures carry obligations and linear payoffs; option buyers have rights and lose at most the premium. Call profit = S - K - premium; put profit = K - S - premium. Index puts protect a portfolio while keeping upside. Leverage magnifies losses; be cautious with retail clients.
