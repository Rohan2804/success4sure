What this chapter covers

This chapter covers debt securities: features of bonds and debentures, coupon, face value, maturity, yield measures, the inverse price-yield relationship, duration, credit ratings and the main risks such as interest rate, credit, reinvestment and liquidity risk. It also covers government securities, corporate bonds, tax-free bonds and small savings. When revising, practise pricing a simple bond, compare yields, and match debt instruments to client needs based on horizon, risk and liquidity.

Debt Investment questions

Question 1

A 3-year bond with face value ₹100 pays a 7% annual coupon. If the market yield for such bonds is 8%, its price is closest to:

  1. ₹100.00
  2. ₹97.42
  3. ₹102.67
  4. ₹93.00
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. ₹97.42

Price = present value of coupons and principal at 8%: 7/1.08 + 7/1.08² + 107/1.08³ ≈ ₹97.42. When yield exceeds coupon, the bond trades at a discount; ₹102.67 is the price at a 6% yield.

Question 2

Which bond will be most sensitive to a change in interest rates, other things equal?

  1. A 20-year zero-coupon bond
  2. A 2-year bond with a high coupon
  3. A 91-day treasury bill
  4. A 5-year floating rate bond reset every quarter
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. A 20-year zero-coupon bond

Price sensitivity rises with maturity and falls with coupon size. A long zero-coupon bond has the highest duration, so its price moves most. Floating rate bonds reset coupons and have low interest rate sensitivity.

Question 3

The difference between the yield on a AA-rated corporate bond and a government security of similar maturity mainly reflects:

  1. The inflation rate in the economy
  2. The face value of the two bonds
  3. The tax deducted on government securities
  4. Credit and liquidity risk
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Credit and liquidity risk

The credit spread compensates investors for the corporate issuer's default risk and usually lower liquidity. Both bonds face the same inflation environment, so inflation does not explain the spread.

Question 4

A bondholder receives coupons and must reinvest them at lower rates because interest rates have fallen. This is:

  1. Credit risk
  2. Default risk
  3. Reinvestment risk
  4. Liquidity risk
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Reinvestment risk

Reinvestment risk is the risk that intermediate cash flows will be reinvested at lower rates than expected, reducing realised returns. It rises when rates fall, the opposite of price risk.

Question 5

A credit rating of 'AAA' on a bond indicates:

  1. A guarantee by the government of repayment
  2. That the bond will never fall in price
  3. The highest degree of safety on timely servicing
  4. That the issuer is a public sector company
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. The highest degree of safety on timely servicing

AAA denotes the highest safety regarding timely payment of interest and principal, in the rating agency's opinion. It is not a guarantee, and the bond's price can still change with interest rates.

Question 6

An upward-sloping yield curve means that:

  1. Longer maturities have higher yields
  2. Short-term yields are higher than long-term yields
  3. All maturities carry the same yield
  4. Bond prices always rise over time
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Longer maturities have higher yields

A normal or upward-sloping yield curve shows higher yields for longer maturities, reflecting term premium and expectations. An inverted curve has short-term yields above long-term yields.

Question 7

A conservative client wants regular income with minimal credit risk for five years. Which option best fits?

  1. Unrated debentures of a small company
  2. Perpetual bonds of a weak bank
  3. A sector equity fund
  4. Government securities of about five years
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Government securities of about five years

G-secs carry sovereign backing with negligible credit risk and pay regular coupons. Unrated or perpetual bonds of weak issuers carry significant credit risk, and equity funds do not offer stable income.

Question 8

If market yields fall after a client buys a fixed-coupon bond, the bond's market price will:

  1. Fall
  2. Rise
  3. Remain unchanged
  4. Become zero
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Rise

Bond prices move inversely to yields. When yields fall, existing bonds with higher coupons become more valuable, so their prices rise.
