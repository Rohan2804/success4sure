Debt Investment: NISM Series X-A revision notes

Debt is a core building block of client portfolios, and X-A tests it with calculations and concepts. You must understand the inverse price-yield relationship, compute current yield and duration effects, and know the risks of debt instruments. Questions also test government versus corporate bonds and ratings.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Bond basics

A bond pays periodic coupons and returns face value at maturity. An 8% bond with ₹100 face value pays ₹8 a year. The issuer may be the government, a PSU, a bank or a company. Secured bonds are backed by assets; debentures may be secured or unsecured.

2. Price and yield move inversely

When market interest rates rise, existing fixed-coupon bonds become less attractive and their prices fall; when rates fall, prices rise. A long-maturity bond's price moves more than a short-maturity bond's for the same change in yield. A gilt fund can therefore post a loss in a rising rate year.

3. Current yield and YTM

Current yield = annual coupon / price: an 8% coupon bond at ₹95 has a current yield of 8.42%. Yield to maturity also includes the gain or loss to maturity and assumes coupons are reinvested at the YTM. A bond below par has YTM above its coupon.

4. Duration

Macaulay duration is the weighted average time to receive a bond's cash flows. Modified duration = Macaulay duration / (1 + yield). A bond with Macaulay duration of 6 years and YTM of 9% has modified duration of about 5.5, so a 50 basis point rise in yield cuts its price by about 2.75%.

5. Convexity

Duration gives a straight-line estimate, but the real price-yield curve is convex. For normal bonds, convexity means prices rise more when yields fall than they drop when yields rise by the same amount - a benefit to the holder. Higher convexity is valuable when rates are volatile.

6. Credit risk and ratings

Credit rating agencies such as CRISIL, ICRA, CARE and India Ratings rate issuers' ability to pay. AAA is the highest; BBB- is the lowest investment grade. A downgrade raises the required yield and lowers the bond's price. Spreads over G-secs widen for lower ratings.

7. Zero-coupon bonds

Zero-coupon bonds are issued at a discount and redeemed at face value with no periodic coupons. Held to maturity, they eliminate reinvestment risk because there are no coupons to reinvest, but their duration equals maturity, so they are very sensitive to rate changes.

8. Other debt risks

Reinvestment risk arises when coupons must be reinvested at lower rates; liquidity risk when bonds trade thinly; call risk when issuers redeem early after rates fall; inflation risk erodes fixed coupons. Floating-rate bonds reduce interest rate risk because coupons reset.

9. Government and small savings

G-secs and state development loans carry sovereign backing. Small savings schemes - PPF, NSC, Senior Citizens Savings Scheme, Sukanya Samriddhi - have administered rates reset quarterly by the government. Check current rates on the official notifications. PPF also offers tax benefits under the old regime.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Current yield = Annual coupon / Market price
  • Modified duration = Macaulay duration / (1 + YTM)
  • % Price change ≈ - Modified duration x Change in yield

Common traps in the exam

  • Saying bond prices rise when rates rise - the relationship is inverse.
  • Using Macaulay duration directly to estimate price change; convert to modified duration first.
  • Thinking zero-coupon bonds have low interest rate risk; their duration equals maturity, so it is high.
  • Treating current yield and YTM as identical; YTM includes the pull to par.

Quick recap

Bond prices and yields move inversely; longer duration means bigger moves. Current yield = coupon / price; YTM adds pull to par. Modified duration = Macaulay / (1 + y); price change ≈ -MD x yield change. Convexity favours holders. Ratings drive credit spreads. Zero-coupon bonds remove reinvestment risk but carry high rate risk.
