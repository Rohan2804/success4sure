Equity Investment: NISM Series X-A revision notes

Advisers need to understand what drives equity returns and how shares are valued. This chapter covers valuation ratios, corporate actions, market-cap categories and analysis approaches. Expect calculation questions on P/E and dividend yield and conceptual questions on bonus issues, splits and buybacks.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Sources of equity return

Equity returns come from dividends and capital appreciation. Over long periods, earnings growth drives share prices, but in the short term valuation changes and sentiment dominate. Equity has no promised return and ranks last in claims on a company's assets.

2. Price-to-earnings ratio

P/E = market price / earnings per share. A share at ₹450 with EPS of ₹30 has a P/E of 15. A high P/E may reflect strong growth expectations or overvaluation; comparisons are meaningful only within the same industry and against the company's own history.

3. Price-to-book and dividend yield

P/B compares price with book value per share and is useful for banks and asset-heavy firms. Dividend yield = dividend per share / price: a ₹12 dividend on a ₹400 share is a 3% yield. High yields may signal value or a falling price.

4. Corporate actions

A 1:1 bonus issue doubles the number of shares and roughly halves the price, so the shareholder's total value is unchanged. A stock split does the same by reducing face value. Buybacks reduce share count and can raise EPS. Rights issues let existing shareholders buy new shares, usually at a discount.

5. Market cap categories

SEBI's categorisation used by mutual funds treats the top 100 listed companies by full market capitalisation as large caps, 101 to 250 as mid caps and the rest as small caps. Small caps typically offer higher growth potential with higher volatility and lower liquidity.

6. Fundamental analysis

Fundamental analysis evaluates the economy, industry and company - revenue growth, margins, return on equity, debt levels, management quality and competitive position - to estimate intrinsic value. Discounted cash flow and dividend discount models are common tools. The aim is to buy below intrinsic value.

7. Technical analysis

Technical analysis studies price and volume patterns, trends, moving averages and indicators to time entries and exits. It assumes prices reflect all information and trends persist. Advisers generally use it, if at all, as a supplement to fundamental work. Evidence of consistent profits is mixed.

8. Investment styles

Growth investors buy companies with fast-rising earnings, accepting high valuations. Value investors buy companies priced below intrinsic value. Quality, momentum and dividend-yield styles also exist. Styles go in and out of favour, which is why diversified portfolios mix them. No single style wins in every cycle.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • P/E = Market price / EPS
  • Dividend yield = Dividend per share / Market price
  • Dividend discount model (constant growth): Price = D1 / (k - g)

Common traps in the exam

  • Thinking a bonus issue increases a shareholder's wealth - total value is unchanged; only the share count rises.
  • Comparing P/E ratios across unrelated industries such as banks and software companies.
  • Assuming a high dividend yield always signals a good investment; it may reflect a collapsing price.
  • Defining large caps by a fixed rupee threshold instead of rank (top 100).

Quick recap

Equity returns = dividends + price change; earnings drive the long run. P/E = price / EPS; dividend yield = DPS / price; P/B suits banks. Bonus and split change share count, not value. Large cap = top 100, mid = 101-250. Fundamental analysis finds value; technical analysis times trades.
