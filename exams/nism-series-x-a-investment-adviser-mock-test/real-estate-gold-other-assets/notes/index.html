Real Estate, Gold & Other Assets: NISM Series X-A revision notes

Indian households hold a large share of wealth in property and gold, so advisers must evaluate these assets objectively. This chapter covers direct real estate, REITs and InvITs, the forms of gold investment and other alternatives. Questions compare features such as liquidity, cost, income and regulation.

Last updated 19 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Direct real estate

Property can provide rental income and appreciation, but it needs large lump sums, carries high transaction costs (stamp duty, registration, brokerage), is illiquid and concentrated in one location. Rental yields on Indian residential property are often modest compared with loan interest rates.

2. Evaluating property returns

Total return includes rent net of maintenance, property tax and vacancies, plus price appreciation, minus transaction costs on purchase and sale. A flat bought for ₹80 lakh earning ₹20,000 a month in rent has a gross rental yield of 3%.

3. REITs

Real Estate Investment Trusts, regulated by SEBI, own income-producing commercial property such as office parks and malls. They must distribute at least 90% of net distributable cash flows to unit holders and invest mostly in completed, rent-generating assets. Units are listed and can be traded like shares.

4. InvITs

Infrastructure Investment Trusts hold infrastructure assets like toll roads, power transmission lines and pipelines. Like REITs, they distribute most of their cash flows. Returns depend on traffic, tariffs and concession periods, so they carry asset-specific risks. Some InvITs are privately placed, not listed for retail.

5. Forms of gold

Jewellery carries making charges and purity concerns. Gold ETFs and gold fund of funds track gold prices without storage issues. Sovereign Gold Bonds paid 2.5% annual interest and were tax-free on redemption at maturity for individuals, though fresh tranches depend on government issuance. Electronic Gold Receipts trade on exchanges.

6. Role of gold

Gold tends to hold value during crises and when the rupee weakens, so it diversifies equity-heavy portfolios. It generates no income (except SGB interest), and returns can be flat for long stretches. Many advisers keep gold to around 5% to 15% of a portfolio.

7. Other alternatives

Silver ETFs, international equity funds, commodities, art, collectibles, unlisted shares and peer-to-peer lending are other options. Many are illiquid, hard to value or lightly regulated. Advisers should assess them only for clients who understand and can bear the risks. Valuation and exit can be very difficult.

8. Concentration in property

Many clients have most of their net worth in one or two properties. Advisers should point out the concentration and liquidity risk and avoid treating the self-occupied home as an asset available for retirement income unless the client plans to sell or rent it out.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Gross rental yield = Annual rent / Property value x 100

Common traps in the exam

  • Assuming REITs can invest mainly in under-construction projects - most assets must be completed and income-generating.
  • Forgetting the 90% minimum distribution requirement for REITs and InvITs.
  • Treating gold jewellery as an efficient investment despite making charges and purity issues.
  • Counting the client's self-occupied home as a liquid retirement asset.

Quick recap

Direct property: big tickets, high costs, illiquid, modest rental yields. REITs (commercial property) and InvITs (infrastructure) are SEBI-regulated, listed and distribute at least 90% of cash flows. Gold diversifies and hedges crises; prefer ETFs, gold funds or SGBs over jewellery. Alternatives suit only informed clients.
