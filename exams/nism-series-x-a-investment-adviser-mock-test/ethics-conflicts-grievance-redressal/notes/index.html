Ethics, Conflicts & Grievance Redressal: NISM Series X-A revision notes

The last X-A chapter is about professional behaviour: putting the client first, managing conflicts of interest, and handling complaints properly. Questions present ethical dilemmas and ask for the correct course of action, or test the grievance escalation path.

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Fiduciary duty

Investment advisers act in a fiduciary capacity: they must place the client's interests above their own, act honestly and fairly, and exercise due skill and care. This is a higher standard than the suitability obligation of a product seller. The duty applies throughout the relationship.

2. Code of conduct

SEBI's code for advisers requires honesty, fairness, diligence, capability, adequate disclosure, confidentiality and compliance. Advisers must not make exaggerated claims, promise returns or misrepresent their qualifications or registration. They must also maintain adequate infrastructure, keep their knowledge up to date and ensure that any employee or representative follows the same standards when dealing with clients.

3. Identifying conflicts

Conflicts arise whenever the adviser or a related person could benefit from a recommendation - for example, recommending a scheme managed by a spouse, a company in which the adviser holds shares, or a product from a group company. Such links must be disclosed upfront.

4. Managing conflicts

When an adviser is about to recommend a scheme whose fund manager is her spouse, she must disclose the relationship to the client before advising and ensure the advice is still in the client's interest; if the conflict cannot be managed, she should avoid the recommendation.

5. Personal trading

Advisers should not trade ahead of client recommendations (front-running) or against the advice they give. Many firms maintain restricted lists and pre-clearance processes. Personal holdings in recommended securities must be disclosed. Advisers should also avoid recommending securities they plan to sell from their own holdings, since the client's purchase could support the price at which they exit.

6. Confidentiality

Client information must be kept confidential and used only for the advisory relationship, except where disclosure is required by law. Sharing client data with distributors or product providers without consent breaches the code. Data security also covers digital records, so advisers must protect client files, emails and passwords against leaks or unauthorised access by staff.

7. Complaint redressal

An adviser must have a grievance mechanism and resolve complaints promptly, within SEBI's timelines. If the investor is not satisfied, the complaint can be lodged with SEBI on SCORES. Unresolved disputes can then go to the SMART ODR platform for online conciliation and arbitration.

8. Disclosure of complaints

Advisers must display the status of complaints received and resolved on their websites in the format SEBI prescribes. This transparency helps prospective clients judge an adviser's service record. The data includes complaints pending at the start of the month, received, resolved and pending at month end, along with average resolution time.

Common traps in the exam

  • Thinking disclosure alone always cures a conflict - the advice must still be in the client's best interest.
  • Escalating directly to SMART ODR without first approaching the adviser and SCORES.
  • Assuming advisers can share client data with group companies freely; consent is required.
  • Treating the fiduciary standard as the same as product suitability; it is higher.

Quick recap

Advisers are fiduciaries: client first, honesty, care and confidentiality. Identify conflicts such as family links or personal holdings, disclose them, and avoid advice that does not serve the client. No front-running. Complaints: adviser first, then SEBI SCORES, then SMART ODR. Publish complaint status as SEBI requires.
