PMS, AIF & Other Managed Portfolios: NISM Series X-A revision notes

For wealthier clients, advisers must understand managed portfolios beyond mutual funds. This chapter covers Portfolio Management Services, Alternative Investment Funds and similar products: minimum ticket sizes, structures, fees and risks. Questions test the minimum investments, PMS types and AIF categories.

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. PMS basics

A Portfolio Management Service manages a client's money in a separate account in the client's own name, regulated by SEBI's Portfolio Managers Regulations. SEBI requires a minimum investment of ₹50 lakh per client (check current limits). The client directly owns the securities in the portfolio.

2. Types of PMS

In discretionary PMS, the manager decides what to buy and sell without seeking approval for each trade. In non-discretionary PMS, the manager advises and executes only with the client's consent. Advisory PMS only recommends; the client executes. Most retail-facing PMS offerings are discretionary.

3. PMS fees and reporting

Fees can be fixed, performance-linked or both, often with a hurdle rate and high-water mark. Portfolio managers must disclose fees in the disclosure document and send periodic reports. Performance must be reported in the standardised format SEBI prescribes for comparability.

4. AIF basics

Alternative Investment Funds pool money from sophisticated investors under SEBI's AIF Regulations. The general minimum investment is ₹1 crore per investor (₹25 lakh for employees or directors of the manager), with exemptions for accredited investors in certain schemes. Accredited investor status depends on income or net worth criteria.

5. AIF categories

Category I invests in start-ups, SMEs, social ventures and infrastructure, which the government sees as beneficial. Category II includes private equity and private debt funds without leverage beyond operational needs. Category III uses complex strategies such as long-short and can use leverage.

6. AIF structure and liquidity

AIFs are usually trusts with a sponsor and manager who must have skin in the game. Category I and II funds are closed-ended with tenures of several years; Category III can be open or closed-ended. Exit before maturity is limited.

7. Comparing with mutual funds

PMS offers customisation and direct ownership but higher minimums and costs and less diversification. AIFs offer access to private markets but are illiquid. Mutual funds remain more liquid, cheaper and more tightly regulated for retail investors. Taxation also differs: PMS gains are taxed in the client's hands on every trade.

8. Suitability checks

Only clients with adequate wealth, long horizons and a clear understanding of risks should consider PMS or AIFs. Advisers should check concentration, liquidity needs, fee drag and the manager's track record, and avoid recommending them just because a client is wealthy.

Common traps in the exam

  • Mixing up PMS (₹50 lakh minimum) and AIF (₹1 crore minimum) thresholds.
  • Describing non-discretionary PMS as one where the manager trades freely - that is discretionary.
  • Placing hedge-fund style leveraged strategies in Category I or II; they are Category III.
  • Assuming PMS securities are pooled like mutual fund units; they are held in the client's own name.

Quick recap

PMS: separate account in client's name, ₹50 lakh minimum, discretionary or non-discretionary. AIF: pooled vehicle, ₹1 crore general minimum, Category I (socially beneficial), II (PE and debt), III (complex, leverage allowed). Both are less liquid and costlier than mutual funds; recommend only to suitable, informed clients.
