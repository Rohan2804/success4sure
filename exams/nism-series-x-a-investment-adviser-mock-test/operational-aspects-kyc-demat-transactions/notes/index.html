Operational Aspects (KYC, Demat, Transactions): NISM Series X-A revision notes

Advisers must know the operational side of investing so clients can implement advice smoothly. This chapter covers KYC and the KRA system, demat accounts and depositories, nomination and the mechanics of buying and selling securities and funds. Questions are factual and procedural.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. KYC and KRAs

KYC verifies a client's identity and address and is mandatory under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act. SEBI-registered KYC Registration Agencies (KRAs) store KYC records centrally, so a client verified once by one intermediary need not repeat the full process with another.

2. Central KYC

The CKYC Registry, managed by CERSAI, holds KYC records across sectors - banking, insurance, securities and pensions. Clients receive a 14-digit KYC Identification Number. Intermediaries upload and download records, reducing duplication. A client who completed KYC for a bank account can often use the same KIN when opening a demat account, saving time and paperwork.

3. Documents and in-person verification

PAN is mandatory for securities market transactions. Proof of address and identity can be Aadhaar, passport, voter ID or driving licence. In-person verification can be done physically or through video. Aadhaar-based eKYC is widely used for online onboarding. KYC must be updated when details change.

4. Depositories

India has two depositories, NSDL and CDSL, which hold securities in electronic form. Investors access them through depository participants such as banks and brokers. Dematerialisation removes risks of physical certificates such as forgery, loss and delays in transfer. Both are regulated by SEBI.

5. Demat and trading accounts

A trading account with a broker is used to place orders; the demat account holds the securities. A linked bank account handles funds. Shares bought are credited to the demat account after settlement; sold shares are debited through a delivery instruction or eDIS authorisation.

6. Nomination

Investors are encouraged to nominate in demat accounts and mutual fund folios or explicitly opt out. Nomination ensures quick transmission to the nominee on death. SEBI has expanded nomination facilities - check the current maximum number of nominees. Without it, legal heirs face lengthy paperwork.

7. Power of attorney limits

SEBI has restricted the use of broad powers of attorney. Brokers now use the Demat Debit and Pledge Instruction (DDPI) for specific purposes such as settling sales and margin pledges, rather than open-ended control over a client's account. This protects client securities from misuse.

8. Mutual fund transactions

Mutual fund units can be held in statement form or in demat. Transactions happen through AMC websites, RTAs, MF Central, exchange platforms or advisers' execution tools. Advisers should implement through direct plans and keep records of client consent. Clients should transact only in their own name.

Common traps in the exam

  • Naming CDSL and NSE as India's depositories - NSDL and CDSL are the depositories; NSE is an exchange.
  • Confusing a KRA (securities market KYC) with the CKYC Registry (cross-sector, run by CERSAI).
  • Assuming the trading account holds shares; the demat account does.
  • Thinking brokers may still take an unrestricted power of attorney; DDPI limits use to specific purposes.

Quick recap

KYC is mandatory under PMLA; KRAs store securities KYC, CKYC (CERSAI) works across sectors. PAN is mandatory. NSDL and CDSL are the depositories, accessed via DPs. Trading account places orders, demat account holds securities. Nominate or opt out. DDPI replaced broad powers of attorney. Implement advice through direct plans with documented consent.
