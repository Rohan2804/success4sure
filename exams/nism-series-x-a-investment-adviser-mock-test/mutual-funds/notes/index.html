Mutual Funds: NISM Series X-A revision notes

Mutual funds are the product advisers recommend most often, so X-A tests them in practical terms: NAV, categories, plans, costs, taxation and suitability. Questions are short scenarios - a client's need is described and you choose the category, or a number is given and you compute NAV.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. NAV calculation

NAV = (investments + other assets - liabilities) / units outstanding. A scheme with investments worth ₹120 crore, liabilities of ₹2 crore and 8 crore units has an NAV of ₹14.75. NAV is declared daily for open-ended schemes and already reflects expenses.

2. Categories that matter

SEBI defines equity categories by market cap and style, debt categories by duration or credit quality, and hybrid categories by equity-debt mix. Advisers use categories to match risk and horizon rather than chasing individual scheme returns. Two funds in one category may still differ sharply in style and risk.

3. Short-term parking

A client parking surplus cash for about two months with low risk and quick access is best served by a liquid or overnight fund, or a money market fund for slightly longer. Equity or long duration funds are unsuitable for such short horizons.

4. Direct versus regular plans

A direct plan has no distributor commission, so its expense ratio is lower and NAV grows faster than the regular plan's over time. SEBI-registered investment advisers generally implement advice through direct plans, since they cannot receive commission from the same client.

5. Tax classification

A scheme is 'equity-oriented' for tax purposes if it invests at least 65% in equity shares of domestic listed companies. Such schemes enjoy concessional long-term gains after 12 months. Funds with over 65% in debt bought after 1 April 2023 are taxed at slab rates, as of 2026.

6. Passive options

Index funds and ETFs offer market returns at low cost. For large cap exposure, many advisers prefer index funds because consistently beating the index after costs is difficult. Tracking error and expense ratio are the main comparison points. Large passive funds also have low turnover.

7. Evaluating schemes

Compare a scheme with its benchmark TRI and category peers on rolling returns, risk-adjusted ratios, portfolio quality, expense ratio and fund manager consistency. A single year's top ranking says little about future results. Rolling three- and five-year returns reveal how consistently a fund has delivered.

8. Systematic plans

SIPs build discipline and average purchase costs; STPs move a lump sum gradually from a liquid fund to equity; SWPs generate regular cash flow in retirement while the rest stays invested. Each SWP withdrawal is a redemption with tax on the gain portion only.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • NAV = (Assets - Liabilities) / Units outstanding

Common traps in the exam

  • Recommending an equity fund for money needed in two months because recent returns were high.
  • Assuming the direct plan has a different portfolio from the regular plan; only expenses differ.
  • Using 50% instead of 65% domestic equity as the equity-oriented threshold.
  • Thinking an investment adviser can take commission on regular plans from the same advisory client.

Quick recap

NAV = net assets / units. Pick categories by horizon and risk: liquid or overnight for short-term cash, equity for long goals. Direct plans cost less and suit advisory clients. Equity-oriented = 65%+ domestic equity. Prefer low-cost index funds where active managers struggle. Use SIP, STP and SWP to match cash flows.
