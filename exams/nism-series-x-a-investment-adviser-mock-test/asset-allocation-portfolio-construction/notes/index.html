Asset Allocation & Portfolio Construction: NISM Series X-A revision notes

Asset allocation is the adviser's most powerful tool. This chapter covers how to set a client's mix of asset classes, how diversification works through correlation, how to compute portfolio expected return and how to rebalance. Expect calculations and comparisons of strategic, tactical and dynamic approaches.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Why allocation matters

The mix of equity, debt, gold and cash explains most of the variation in a portfolio's long-term returns and risk. Choosing between two good large cap funds matters far less than deciding whether the client holds 30% or 70% in equity.

2. Strategic asset allocation

Strategic allocation is the long-term target mix based on the client's goals, horizon and risk profile - for example 60% equity, 30% debt and 10% gold. It changes only when the client's circumstances change, such as approaching retirement. It is recorded in the investment policy statement.

3. Tactical asset allocation

Tactical allocation makes temporary deviations from the strategic mix to exploit perceived market opportunities, such as raising equity by 5% after a sharp fall. It requires skill and discipline; mistimed moves can hurt returns. Limits on tactical shifts, such as plus or minus 10% from the strategic weight, keep the portfolio anchored to the client's risk profile.

4. Correlation and diversification

Diversification reduces risk most when assets have low or negative correlation. Equity and gold have historically shown low correlation, so combining them smooths returns. Two highly correlated assets, such as two large cap funds, offer little diversification benefit. Correlations tend to rise during market crises.

5. Expected portfolio return

Expected return is the weighted average of asset returns. With 60% equity at 12%, 30% debt at 7% and 10% gold at 8%, expected return = 7.2 + 2.1 + 0.8 = 10.1%. Portfolio risk, however, is lower than the weighted average of individual risks unless correlations are perfect.

6. Rebalancing

If a 60:40 equity-debt portfolio drifts to 70:30 after a rally, rebalancing sells equity and buys debt to restore 60:40. This locks in gains and controls risk. Rebalancing can be calendar-based (yearly) or threshold-based (when drift exceeds, say, 5%). Tax and exit loads affect the choice of method.

7. Life-stage and goal-based allocation

Young investors with long horizons can hold more equity; allocation shifts towards debt as goals approach. Goal-based allocation assigns separate buckets to each goal, so a house purchase in two years is in debt while retirement savings stay in equity.

8. Core and satellite

A core-satellite approach puts most of the portfolio in low-cost diversified holdings such as index funds (the core) and a smaller part in active or thematic ideas (satellites). It balances cost control with the chance to add value. Satellites are usually capped at 20-30% of the portfolio.

Common traps in the exam

  • Calling a short-term shift to exploit market conditions 'strategic' - that is tactical allocation.
  • Assuming diversification works best with highly correlated assets; low or negative correlation helps most.
  • Thinking portfolio risk is the simple weighted average of individual risks.
  • Letting a portfolio drift indefinitely after a rally instead of rebalancing to target.

Quick recap

Allocation drives most outcomes. Strategic = long-term target; tactical = temporary tilts. Low correlation gives the most diversification. Expected return is a weighted average; risk is lower unless correlation is 1. Rebalance by calendar or threshold. Use goal buckets and a low-cost core with smaller satellites.
