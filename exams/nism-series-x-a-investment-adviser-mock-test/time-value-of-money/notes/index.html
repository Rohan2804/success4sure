Time Value of Money: NISM Series X-A revision notes

Time value of money underpins almost every calculation in X-A: goal planning, loan EMIs, bond prices and retirement corpus. The exam expects you to compute FV, PV, annuity values and effective rates on a financial calculator or spreadsheet. Practise the worked examples below until you can do them quickly.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Why money has time value

A rupee today is worth more than a rupee tomorrow because it can be invested to earn a return, and because inflation reduces future purchasing power. Every comparison of cash flows at different dates must bring them to a common point in time.

2. Future value of a lump sum

₹1,00,000 invested for 5 years at 8% compounded annually grows to 1,00,000 x 1.08^5 = ₹1,46,933. Compounding more frequently increases the result slightly, since interest starts earning interest sooner. The same amount at 12% for 5 years would grow to about ₹1,76,234, showing how a few percentage points of extra return add up over time.

3. Present value of a lump sum

To have ₹10,00,000 in 10 years at 7% a year, the client must invest 10,00,000 / 1.07^10 = about ₹5,08,349 today. The higher the discount rate or the longer the period, the lower the present value. At 10%, only about ₹3,85,543 would be needed today.

4. Effective annual rate

A nominal 12% compounded monthly gives an effective annual rate of (1 + 0.12/12)^12 - 1 = 12.68%. When comparing loans or deposits with different compounding frequencies, compare effective rates, not nominal ones. Quarterly compounding at 12% gives 12.55%, so the more frequent the compounding, the higher the effective rate.

5. Annuities

An annuity is a series of equal payments at equal intervals. In an ordinary annuity, payments come at the end of each period (most SIPs and EMIs are modelled this way); in an annuity due, at the beginning (rent paid in advance). An annuity due is worth more because each payment compounds one period longer.

6. Future value of a SIP

₹10,000 at the end of each month for 10 years at 12% a year (1% a month) grows to 10,000 x [(1.01^120 - 1) / 0.01] = about ₹23 lakh, against ₹12 lakh invested. The extra ₹11 lakh is the effect of compounding.

7. Loan EMIs

EMI = P x r x (1+r)^n / [(1+r)^n - 1], where r is the monthly rate. A ₹30 lakh home loan at 9% for 20 years has an EMI of about ₹26,992. Early EMIs are mostly interest; the principal share grows over time.

8. Rule of 72

Dividing 72 by the annual return gives approximate doubling time: at 8%, money doubles in about 9 years; at 12%, in about 6. Clients find it an intuitive way to grasp the power of returns and the cost of high-interest debt.

9. Real rate of return

Real rate = (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) - 1. With 10% nominal and 6% inflation, real return is 3.77%. Retirement planning often discounts expenses at the real rate to find the corpus needed for inflation-adjusted withdrawals. Simple subtraction gives 4%, which overstates it.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • FV = PV x (1 + r)^n; PV = FV / (1 + r)^n
  • FV of ordinary annuity = P x [(1 + r)^n - 1] / r
  • EAR = (1 + i/m)^m - 1, where m is compounding periods per year
  • Real rate = (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) - 1

Common traps in the exam

  • Using the annual rate with monthly periods - convert to a monthly rate and monthly number of periods.
  • Confusing annuity due (start of period) with ordinary annuity (end of period).
  • Comparing nominal rates with different compounding frequencies instead of effective rates.
  • Subtracting inflation from nominal return when the question asks for the exact real rate.

Quick recap

Bring all cash flows to one date. FV = PV(1+r)^n; PV = FV/(1+r)^n. EAR adjusts for compounding frequency. Annuity due beats ordinary annuity by one period of growth. Match rate and period units. Rule of 72 gives doubling time. Use the exact real rate formula when precision is asked.
