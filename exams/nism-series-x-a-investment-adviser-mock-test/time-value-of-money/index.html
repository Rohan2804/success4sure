Time Value of Money: NISM Series X-A practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on Time Value of Money, from the NISM Series X-A (Investment Adviser (Level 1)) syllabus.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter covers the time value of money: compounding and discounting, future and present value of lump sums and annuities, ordinary annuity versus annuity due, effective annual rates, real versus nominal returns, loan EMIs and rules of thumb such as the rule of 72. These tools underpin goal planning and loan analysis. When revising, practise calculations with a financial calculator or spreadsheet, be careful about compounding frequency and payment timing, and interpret results in client terms.

Time Value of Money questions

Question 1

Using the rule of 72, roughly how many years will an investment take to double at 9% per annum?

  1. 9 years
  2. 6 years
  3. 12 years
  4. 8 years
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. 8 years

The rule of 72 estimates doubling time as 72 ÷ interest rate = 72 ÷ 9 = 8 years. The exact figure at 9% annual compounding is about 8.04 years, so the approximation is close.

Question 2

A deposit earns 10% per annum while inflation is 6%. What is the real rate of return, using the exact formula?

  1. Exactly 4.00%
  2. About 16.6%
  3. About 3.77%
  4. About 1.67%
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. About 3.77%

Real return = (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1 = 1.10 ÷ 1.06 − 1 ≈ 3.77%. Simply subtracting 6% from 10% gives 4%, which slightly overstates the real return.

Question 3

A home loan of ₹20 lakh is taken at 9% per annum for 15 years with monthly EMIs. The EMI is closest to:

  1. ₹15,000
  2. ₹20,285
  3. ₹26,111
  4. ₹11,111
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. ₹20,285

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)^n ÷ ((1 + r)^n − 1) with r = 0.75% and n = 180, giving about ₹20,285. ₹11,111 ignores interest and ₹15,000 is only the first month's interest.

Question 4

An investment grew from ₹1 lakh to ₹2 lakh in 6 years. What is its compound annual growth rate?

  1. About 12.2%
  2. About 16.7%
  3. About 10.0%
  4. About 14.5%
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. About 12.2%

CAGR = (2 ÷ 1)^(1/6) − 1 ≈ 12.25%. 16.7% is the simple average (100% ÷ 6), which ignores compounding and overstates the annual growth rate.

Question 5

What is the present value of ₹50,000 received at the end of each year for 10 years, discounted at 8% per annum?

  1. ₹5,00,000
  2. About ₹3,35,504
  3. About ₹3,62,344
  4. About ₹2,50,000
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. About ₹3,35,504

PV of an ordinary annuity = 50,000 × (1 − 1.08^−10) ÷ 0.08 ≈ ₹3,35,504. ₹3,62,344 is the annuity-due value with payments at the start of each year, and ₹5,00,000 ignores discounting.

Question 6

₹5 lakh is invested for 3 years at 8% per annum compounded quarterly. Its maturity value is closest to:

  1. ₹6,29,856
  2. ₹6,20,000
  3. ₹6,34,121
  4. ₹6,40,000
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. ₹6,34,121

Quarterly rate 2% for 12 quarters: 5,00,000 × 1.02^12 ≈ ₹6,34,121. Annual compounding gives ₹6,29,856 and simple interest gives ₹6,20,000, both lower because interest compounds less often.

Question 7

Compared with an ordinary annuity of the same amount, rate and term, the future value of an annuity due is:

  1. Lower, because payments are made later
  2. Exactly the same in all cases
  3. Higher only when the interest rate is negative
  4. Higher, as each payment earns one extra period
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Higher, as each payment earns one extra period

An annuity due has payments at the start of each period, so every payment compounds for one more period. Its value equals the ordinary annuity value multiplied by (1 + r).

Question 8

If the discount rate used to value a future goal increases, the present value of that goal will:

  1. Fall
  2. Rise
  3. Stay the same
  4. Double
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Fall

Present value = future value ÷ (1 + r)^n. A higher discount rate increases the denominator, so the present value falls. This is why the assumed return strongly affects required savings.
