Securities Market Segments: NISM Series X-A revision notes

This chapter explains how securities are first issued and then traded. It covers public issue types, the book-building process, ASBA and UPI, secondary market trading, settlement cycles and the role of exchanges, clearing corporations and depositories. Questions are factual and often about which market a transaction belongs to.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Primary market

The primary market is where securities are issued for the first time and money flows to the issuer. An IPO is a company's first public offer; a follow-on public offer (FPO) is a later one. Rights issues offer shares to existing shareholders in proportion to their holdings.

2. Offer for sale vs fresh issue

In a fresh issue, new shares are created and the company receives the money. In an offer for sale, existing shareholders such as promoters or PE funds sell their shares, and the money goes to them, not the company. Many IPOs combine both.

3. Book building

In book building, investors bid within a price band and the final price is discovered from demand. In a fixed price issue, the price is set in advance. Retail individual investors receive a reserved portion of the issue in book-built IPOs.

4. ASBA and UPI

Applications Supported by Blocked Amount (ASBA) keep the application money in the investor's bank account, only blocked until allotment. Retail investors can use UPI mandates. Listing happens within a few working days after the issue closes under SEBI's shortened timeline.

5. Private placements and QIPs

Companies can also raise money through private placements to select investors and qualified institutions placements (QIPs) for listed companies selling to institutional buyers. These are faster and cheaper than public issues but not open to retail investors. Preferential allotments are another such route.

6. Secondary market

The secondary market is where existing securities are traded between investors on stock exchanges such as NSE and BSE. The company receives no money from these trades. Liquidity in the secondary market makes primary issues attractive. Prices are set by demand and supply among investors.

7. Settlement cycle

Indian equity cash market trades settle on a T+1 rolling basis, with an optional T+0 cycle introduced for select stocks. Shares are credited to the buyer's demat account and funds to the seller after settlement through the clearing corporation. Shorter cycles reduce counterparty risk.

8. Market infrastructure

Stock exchanges provide trading platforms; clearing corporations guarantee and settle trades; depositories (NSDL and CDSL) hold securities electronically. Together they are called market infrastructure institutions and are closely regulated by SEBI. Their ownership and governance norms are stricter than for ordinary companies, because the whole market depends on them working without interruption.

Common traps in the exam

  • Calling an IPO a secondary market transaction - shares are issued fresh, so it is primary.
  • Assuming the company receives money from an offer for sale; the selling shareholders do.
  • Quoting T+2 as the current equity settlement cycle; India moved to T+1.
  • Believing ASBA debits the money at application; it only blocks it until allotment.

Quick recap

Primary market = new issues, money to issuer (IPO, FPO, rights, QIP). Offer for sale money goes to selling shareholders. Book building discovers price within a band; ASBA blocks funds. Secondary market = trading between investors on exchanges. Equity settles T+1. Exchanges, clearing corporations and depositories form the market infrastructure.
