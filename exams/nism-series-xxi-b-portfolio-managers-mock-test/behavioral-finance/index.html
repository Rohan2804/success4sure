Behavioral Finance: NISM Series XXI-B practice questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter explains how psychological biases affect investor and manager decisions. It covers cognitive biases (anchoring, confirmation, availability, representativeness, mental accounting, overconfidence) and emotional biases (loss aversion, regret aversion, endowment effect, herding), prospect theory, and the impact of biases on markets, such as bubbles and under- or over-reaction. For portfolio managers, the focus is recognising biases in clients and in themselves, and using process and communication to limit their impact.

Behavioral Finance questions

Question 1

Prospect theory suggests that investors:

  1. Always make decisions that maximise their expected wealth
  2. Feel gains and losses of the same size with equal intensity
  3. Prefer certain losses to uncertain losses in all cases
  4. Feel losses more strongly than equal gains
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Feel losses more strongly than equal gains

Kahneman and Tversky showed that losses hurt roughly twice as much as equal gains please, and people evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point. This explains many investment errors.

Question 2

A client keeps 'safe money' in a fixed deposit earning 6% while carrying a credit card balance at 36%. This reflects:

  1. Herding behaviour driven by the actions of other investors
  2. Overconfidence in his own stock-picking ability
  3. Mental accounting
  4. Hindsight bias about past market events
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Mental accounting

Mental accounting treats money in separate 'buckets' instead of as a whole. Paying off the expensive loan with the deposit would clearly improve his overall position.

Question 3

A fund manager who credits gains to his skill but blames losses on bad luck is showing:

  1. Anchoring to a historical purchase price
  2. Self-attribution bias
  3. Loss aversion causing him to hold losers
  4. The disposition effect in his tax planning
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Self-attribution bias

Self-attribution bias leads people to take credit for successes and deflect failures. Over time it feeds overconfidence and can lead to excessive risk-taking.

Question 4

Investors judge a company to be a great investment simply because it has a great product they love. This is an example of:

  1. Mental accounting of different money pots
  2. Representativeness bias
  3. Regret aversion about missed opportunities
  4. Status quo bias in portfolio allocation
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Representativeness bias

Representativeness leads people to judge by stereotypes or similarity: a good product feels like a good stock. But a stock's return depends on price paid relative to value.

Question 5

The 'endowment effect' leads investors to:

  1. Sell inherited shares immediately to diversify
  2. Prefer to buy assets that others are selling
  3. Value assets they own more than identical ones
  4. Ignore how much they paid for an asset
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Value assets they own more than identical ones

People demand more to give up something they own than they would pay to acquire it. Clients may refuse to sell inherited or long-held shares even when diversification is needed.

Question 6

Which practice best helps a portfolio manager reduce the impact of behavioural biases on decisions?

  1. A rules-based process with written criteria
  2. Making decisions quickly based on gut feeling during market hours
  3. Following the most popular stock ideas on social media
  4. Reviewing only information that supports existing holdings
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. A rules-based process with written criteria

Checklists, documented investment cases and independent review make decisions more consistent and expose biases like confirmation and overconfidence. Gut feeling and herding amplify them.

Question 7

After a strong two-year rally, a client wants to put all savings into equities because 'markets always go up'. The bias most evident is:

  1. Recency bias
  2. Loss aversion leading to excessive caution
  3. Endowment effect over inherited property
  4. Conservatism in updating beliefs
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Recency bias

Recency bias overweights recent experience and extrapolates it into the future. The manager should remind the client about cycles and stick to the agreed allocation.

Question 8

Regret aversion can cause investors to:

  1. Trade much more frequently to capture every opportunity
  2. Diversify widely across many asset classes and countries
  3. Rebalance strictly every quarter as per the IPS
  4. Avoid action to escape the pain of a wrong decision
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Avoid action to escape the pain of a wrong decision

Fear of regret may lead investors to delay decisions, stick to familiar choices or follow the crowd. This can leave portfolios unbalanced or in unsuitable assets.
