Investing in Fixed Income Securities: NISM Series XXI-B practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on Investing in Fixed Income Securities, from the NISM Series XXI-B (Portfolio Managers) syllabus.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter covers bond investing for portfolio managers: bond features and types, pricing and yield measures (current yield, YTM, yield to call), the term structure and yield curve shapes, duration, modified duration and convexity, credit risk, ratings and spreads, and the Indian debt market segments such as G-secs, SDLs, corporate bonds, CPs and CDs. Practise bond pricing and duration-based price change calculations and understand how yield curve changes affect different bonds.

Investing in Fixed Income Securities questions

Question 1

A bond with face value ₹1,000 and coupon 8% is trading at ₹1,050. Which statement is correct?

  1. Its YTM is below 8%
  2. Its YTM is above 8% because the price is above face value
  3. Its current yield equals its coupon rate of exactly 8%
  4. It is trading at a discount to its face value
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Its YTM is below 8%

A bond priced above face value is at a premium, so the buyer gets less than the coupon rate as yield. Current yield = 80 ÷ 1,050 ≈ 7.6%, also below 8%.

Question 2

An upward-sloping (normal) yield curve indicates that:

  1. Short-term yields are higher than long-term yields for all maturities
  2. All maturities offer exactly the same yield to investors
  3. Long-term yields are higher than short-term yields
  4. Bond prices are guaranteed to rise over the next year
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Long-term yields are higher than short-term yields

A normal yield curve slopes upward because investors usually demand extra yield for longer maturities. An inverted curve, with short yields above long yields, often signals slowing growth.

Question 3

Convexity is valuable to a bond investor because, for large yield changes, it means:

  1. Prices fall more and rise less than duration alone would suggest
  2. The bond's coupon automatically resets to market rates
  3. The bond becomes free of all credit and liquidity risk
  4. Prices rise more and fall less than duration predicts
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Prices rise more and fall less than duration predicts

Duration is a linear estimate. Positive convexity curves the price–yield line so actual gains exceed and losses fall short of the duration estimate for big moves.

Question 4

A bond has modified duration 6 and convexity effects can be ignored. If its yield rises from 7.0% to 7.5%, its price changes by approximately:

  1. +3%
  2. −6%
  3. −3%
  4. −0.5%
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. −3%

Price change ≈ −modified duration × change in yield = −6 × 0.5% = −3%. Prices move opposite to yields, scaled by duration.

Question 5

A callable bond is less attractive to investors than a similar non-callable bond because:

  1. The investor must hold it until maturity without any coupon payments
  2. It always carries a lower credit rating than non-callable bonds
  3. Its coupons are taxed at a higher rate than other bonds
  4. The issuer may redeem it early when rates fall
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. The issuer may redeem it early when rates fall

Issuers call bonds when rates fall, forcing investors to reinvest at lower yields and capping price gains. Investors therefore demand a higher yield for callable bonds.

Question 6

State Development Loans (SDLs) are bonds issued by:

  1. State governments
  2. Public sector banks for funding home loans to individuals
  3. The Central Government to fund the Union budget deficit
  4. Municipal corporations to fund local water projects only
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. State governments

SDLs are market borrowings of state governments, auctioned through the RBI. They usually yield slightly more than Central Government securities of similar maturity.

Question 7

The credit spread on a corporate bond is the:

  1. Difference between the bond's coupon and the RBI repo rate
  2. Brokerage paid when buying the bond in the secondary market
  3. Gap between the bond's face value and its issue price
  4. Extra yield over a comparable government bond
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Extra yield over a comparable government bond

Credit spread compensates for default and liquidity risk relative to a risk-free G-sec of similar maturity. Spreads widen when credit risk is perceived to rise.

Question 8

A zero-coupon bond's Macaulay duration is:

  1. Equal to its time to maturity
  2. Always zero, because it does not pay any coupon at all
  3. Half of its time to maturity in all cases
  4. Longer than its time to maturity
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Equal to its time to maturity

A zero-coupon bond pays a single cash flow at maturity, so its weighted average time to cash flow equals its maturity. Coupon bonds have durations shorter than maturity.
