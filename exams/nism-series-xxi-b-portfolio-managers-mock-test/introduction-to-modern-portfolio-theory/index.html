What this chapter covers

This chapter covers Harry Markowitz's mean-variance framework. It explains expected return and variance of a portfolio, covariance and correlation, the diversification effect, the efficient frontier, the minimum variance portfolio, the optimal portfolio given investor utility, and the limitations of MPT (estimation error, normality assumption, unstable correlations). Practise two-asset portfolio return and risk calculations and interpret how correlation changes the risk of combinations.

Introduction to Modern Portfolio Theory questions

Question 1

A portfolio holds 40% in asset A (expected return 12%) and 60% in asset B (expected return 8%). Its expected return is:

  1. 10.0%
  2. 9.6%
  3. 10.4%
  4. 20.0%
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. 9.6%

Expected portfolio return is the weighted average: 0.4 × 12% + 0.6 × 8% = 4.8% + 4.8% = 9.6%. Unlike risk, expected return does not depend on correlation.

Question 2

Two assets each have standard deviation 10%, and the correlation between them is +1. A 50:50 portfolio has standard deviation:

  1. 5%
  2. 7.07%
  3. 0%
  4. 10%
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. 10%

With perfect positive correlation there is no diversification benefit, so portfolio risk is the weighted average: 10%. With zero correlation it would be about 7.07%.

Question 3

Covariance between two assets' returns measures:

  1. How their returns move together
  2. The average return of the two assets combined over time
  3. The difference between their highest and lowest prices
  4. The dividend yield of the combined portfolio
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. How their returns move together

Covariance shows whether returns tend to move in the same direction. Correlation standardises covariance by dividing by the product of standard deviations, giving a value between −1 and +1.

Question 4

The optimal portfolio for a particular investor in MPT is found where:

  1. The efficient frontier meets the horizontal axis at zero risk
  2. The portfolio has the highest return regardless of its risk
  3. The portfolio contains the largest number of assets possible
  4. Her indifference curve touches the efficient frontier
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Her indifference curve touches the efficient frontier

Each investor picks the efficient portfolio that maximises her utility, at the point where her highest attainable indifference curve is tangent to the frontier.

Question 5

A key practical limitation of mean-variance optimisation is that:

  1. It cannot handle portfolios with more than two assets
  2. Outputs are very sensitive to estimated inputs
  3. It ignores expected returns entirely and uses only prices
  4. It requires all assets to have exactly the same risk
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Outputs are very sensitive to estimated inputs

Small changes in expected returns or correlations can produce very different 'optimal' weights, often extreme. Practitioners use constraints or robust methods to handle estimation error.

Question 6

Two assets have standard deviations of 15% and 25% and a correlation of zero. The standard deviation of a 50:50 portfolio is approximately:

  1. 14.6%
  2. 20.0%
  3. 10.0%
  4. 17.5%
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. 14.6%

σ = √(0.5² × 15² + 0.5² × 25²) = √(56.25 + 156.25) = √212.5 ≈ 14.6%. Low correlation gives risk below the 20% weighted average.

Question 7

As more randomly chosen stocks are added to a portfolio, total portfolio risk:

  1. Falls to zero once the portfolio holds about 30 stocks
  2. Rises steadily because each stock adds its own risk
  3. Falls towards the level of market risk
  4. Stays exactly the same regardless of the number of stocks
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Falls towards the level of market risk

Diversification removes stock-specific risk, but systematic market risk remains. Risk therefore declines and then levels off at the market risk floor.

Question 8

An asset with a lower expected return than others may still earn a place in a mean-variance optimal portfolio because:

  1. Its low correlation can reduce total risk
  2. Mean-variance optimisation always gives equal weights to every asset available
  3. Assets with low expected returns are guaranteed to rise in the next year
  4. SEBI requires every portfolio to hold at least one low-return asset
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Its low correlation can reduce total risk

In MPT what matters is an asset's contribution to portfolio risk and return. A low- or negatively correlated asset can lower overall volatility enough to improve the portfolio's risk-return trade-off.
