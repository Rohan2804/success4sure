NPS – Exit, Withdrawal & Annuity: NISM Series XVII revision notes

Exit rules decide how much of an NPS corpus becomes cash and how much must buy a pension. This chapter is frequently tested because the rules differ for normal exit, premature exit and death, and PFRDA has amended them several times, including changes for non-government subscribers in 2025. Learn the logic and the traditional numbers, and confirm current limits with PFRDA before advising.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Normal exit

Normal exit happens on superannuation or at 60. Traditionally, at least 40% of the corpus had to buy an annuity and up to 60% could be withdrawn as lump sum. PFRDA's 2025 amendments allow non-government subscribers a larger lump sum share; check the current split before advising.

2. Small corpus

If the total corpus at normal exit is at or below a threshold set by PFRDA (raised over time), the subscriber can withdraw the entire amount without buying an annuity. This avoids forcing tiny annuities that would pay negligible monthly income.

3. Premature exit

Exit before 60 (after a minimum period in NPS) is treated as premature. Traditionally at least 80% of the corpus had to be annuitised and at most 20% taken as lump sum, again with full withdrawal allowed for small corpuses. The rule discourages using NPS as a short-term saving.

4. Death of subscriber

On death, the entire corpus is paid to the nominee or legal heirs, who may also choose to buy an annuity. For government employees covered by additional relief rules, family pension options may apply instead.

5. Partial withdrawal

After a minimum period of subscription, Tier I subscribers can withdraw up to 25% of their own contributions (not employer contributions or returns) for specified purposes: children's higher education or marriage, buying or building a first house, treatment of specified illnesses, starting a business or skill development. The number of withdrawals is limited.

6. Deferment and systematic withdrawal

Subscribers can defer the lump sum and annuity purchase to a later age, staying invested. They can also take the lump sum in instalments through systematic lump sum withdrawal, giving a mutual-fund-SWP-like income while the balance stays invested.

7. Annuity purchase

The annuity must be bought from an empanelled annuity service provider, which are life insurance companies. The subscriber chooses the annuity type (life, with return of purchase price, joint life and so on). The annuity rate is fixed at purchase and annuity income is taxable as income.

8. Tax at exit

The lump sum withdrawn at normal exit, up to 60% of the corpus, is tax exempt. Partial withdrawals within the rules are exempt. The amount used to buy an annuity is not taxed at purchase, but the annuity received later is taxable in the year of receipt.

Common traps in the exam

  • Partial withdrawal is up to 25% of the subscriber's own contributions, not 25% of the total corpus.
  • Annuities must be bought from empanelled life insurers (ASPs), not from pension fund managers.
  • Annuity income is taxable even though the commuted lump sum is tax free.
  • Premature exit forces a much larger annuity share than normal exit.

Quick recap

Normal exit at 60: traditionally 60% lump sum (tax free) and 40% annuity; 2025 changes give non-government subscribers more flexibility. Small corpuses can be fully withdrawn. Premature exit: traditionally 80% annuity. Death: full corpus to nominee. Partial withdrawal up to 25% of own contributions for listed needs. Annuity from empanelled insurers; annuity income is taxable.
