Pension Systems & Regulatory Framework (PFRDA): NISM Series XVII revision notes

This chapter explains the types of pension systems, how India's pension landscape evolved and how the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority regulates NPS and related schemes. Questions test the difference between defined benefit and defined contribution, key dates in NPS history, PFRDA's role and the functions of intermediaries such as Points of Presence.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Defined benefit versus defined contribution

In a defined benefit plan the pension is fixed by a formula, such as 50% of last drawn pay, and the employer bears investment and longevity risk. In a defined contribution plan, contributions are fixed and the pension depends on accumulated corpus and returns, so the member bears the risk. NPS is defined contribution.

2. Funded and unfunded

An unfunded (pay-as-you-go) scheme pays current pensions from current revenues, as with the old government pension scheme. A funded scheme invests contributions to pay future pensions. Rising pension bills of governments were a key reason for moving to funded schemes.

3. Pillars of pension

The World Bank multi-pillar model is often used: a non-contributory social pension (pillar zero), mandatory contributory schemes, voluntary savings, and informal family support. In India, examples include old-age social assistance, EPF and EPS, NPS, APY and personal savings.

4. Evolution in India

Central government employees joining from 1 January 2004 (except armed forces) moved to the National Pension System; most states followed. NPS was opened to all citizens in 2009. From April 2025, central government NPS employees can opt for the Unified Pension Scheme, which assures a pension linked to average basic pay for qualifying service.

5. PFRDA

PFRDA was set up in 2003 by executive order and became statutory under the PFRDA Act, 2013 (effective 2014). It regulates NPS, Atal Pension Yojana administration and pension funds, registers intermediaries, protects subscribers and promotes old-age income security.

6. NPS intermediaries

Points of Presence (banks and others) are the first point of contact for subscribers, handling registration and contributions. Central Recordkeeping Agencies maintain accounts and issue the PRAN. Pension funds invest the money. The NPS Trust oversees assets in subscribers' interest. A trustee bank handles fund flows, a custodian holds securities, and annuity service providers (life insurers) pay annuities.

7. Retirement advisers under PFRDA

PFRDA's Retirement Adviser Regulations allow registered advisers to guide subscribers on NPS and retirement planning. They must hold the NISM XVII certification and follow a code of conduct covering suitability, disclosure and fair dealing.

8. Subscriber protection

PFRDA prescribes caps on charges, disclosure of scheme returns and portfolios, grievance redressal through the CRA's central grievance system and escalation to the NPS Trust and an ombudsman. Investment guidelines limit concentration and credit risk.

Common traps in the exam

  • NPS is defined contribution; the old government pension was defined benefit and unfunded.
  • NPS began for central government employees on 1 January 2004 and for all citizens in 2009.
  • Points of Presence collect contributions and register subscribers; CRAs keep records; pension funds invest.
  • PFRDA became statutory under the 2013 Act, though the authority existed from 2003.

Quick recap

Defined benefit fixes the pension; defined contribution fixes contributions and shifts risk to the member. India moved government employees from an unfunded DB pension to NPS from 2004, opened NPS to all in 2009 and added UPS in 2025. PFRDA (statutory from 2014) regulates NPS and APY. Intermediaries: POP, CRA, pension funds, NPS Trust, trustee bank, custodian, ASPs.
