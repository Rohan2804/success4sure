National Pension System – Architecture: NISM Series XVII revision notes

This chapter explains how NPS is structured for different subscriber groups and how an account works day to day. You need to know the account tiers, subscriber models, eligibility, minimum contributions and the tax benefits linked to contributions. Several limits have changed in recent years, so wherever a number appears, treat it as current as of 2026 and verify with PFRDA.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. PRAN

Each subscriber receives a unique Permanent Retirement Account Number from the CRA. It stays the same across jobs, cities and POPs, so a subscriber moving from a corporate job to self-employment keeps the same NPS account. Portability is a key advantage over older schemes.

2. Tier I and Tier II

Tier I is the pension account with restrictions on withdrawal and tax benefits on contributions. Tier II is a voluntary savings account that can be opened only with an active Tier I, allows free withdrawals and generally carries no tax benefit (except a special Tier II tax-saver for government employees).

3. Subscriber models

NPS works through several models: central government, state government, corporate (employers enrol staff, often with employer contributions), all-citizen (individuals through POPs or eNPS online), and NPS Lite/Swavalamban (now largely replaced by APY). Each model has slightly different default choices.

4. Eligibility

Indian citizens, including NRIs, aged 18 to 70 years can open an all-citizen NPS account. OCI cardholders are also eligible. Contributions can continue till exit, and PFRDA has allowed deferment of exit and continuation beyond 60 subject to its rules.

5. NPS Vatsalya

Launched in 2024, NPS Vatsalya lets parents or guardians open an NPS account for a minor. On attaining 18, the account converts into a regular all-citizen Tier I account after fresh KYC of the subscriber. It helps start compounding very early.

6. Contributions

There is a small minimum contribution per year for Tier I to keep the account active, and no maximum. Contributions can be made online through eNPS, through POPs or by employers. Employer contributions in the corporate model go directly to the employee's PRAN.

7. Tax on contributions

Own contributions qualify under Section 80CCD(1) within the overall ₹1.5 lakh limit with 80C, plus an extra ₹50,000 under Section 80CCD(1B) in the old tax regime. Employer contributions qualify under 80CCD(2) up to a percentage of salary, also available in the new regime. Check current limits for 2026.

8. Charges

NPS has among the lowest charges of any retirement product: pension fund management fees, CRA charges and POP charges are capped by PFRDA. Low costs make a big difference over 30-year horizons.

Common traps in the exam

  • Tier II requires an active Tier I account; it cannot exist alone.
  • PRAN does not change when a subscriber changes job or POP.
  • 80CCD(1B) gives ₹50,000 over and above the ₹1.5 lakh 80C limit, only in the old regime.
  • NPS Vatsalya converts into a normal Tier I account at 18; it is not closed and paid out.

Quick recap

Every subscriber gets a portable PRAN. Tier I is the locked-in pension account with tax benefits; Tier II is optional, liquid and needs Tier I. Models: government, corporate, all-citizen, Lite. Citizens and NRIs aged 18 to 70 can join; OCIs too. NPS Vatsalya for minors converts at 18. Tax: 80CCD(1), extra ₹50,000 under 80CCD(1B), employer 80CCD(2). Charges are capped.
