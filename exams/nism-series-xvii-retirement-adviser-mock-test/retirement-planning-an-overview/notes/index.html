Retirement Planning: An Overview: NISM Series XVII revision notes

NISM Series XVII (Retirement Adviser) opens with why individuals in India must plan for retirement themselves and what makes it different from other goals. The chapter covers demographic and social trends, the main risks retirees face and the accumulation and distribution phases. Expect conceptual questions plus a simple future-value calculation of expenses under inflation.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Why planning matters now

Indians are living longer, joint families are giving way to nuclear households, and most private-sector and self-employed workers have no defined-benefit pension. Rising healthcare costs add pressure. The result is that an individual's own savings must fund 20 to 30 years of retired life.

2. Longevity risk

Longevity risk is the risk of outliving one's savings. Planning to age 85 when a client lives to 95 can leave ten unfunded years. Advisers usually plan for a life expectancy well above the average, especially for the younger spouse in a couple.

3. Inflation risk

Inflation erodes purchasing power over long retirements. At 6% inflation, prices roughly double every 12 years. Monthly expenses of ₹40,000 today will be about ₹40,000 x (1.06)^20 = ₹1.28 lakh in 20 years. Fixed-income-only portfolios struggle against this.

4. Other risks

Retirees also face interest rate risk (falling FD rates reduce income), market risk, sequence-of-returns risk (poor returns early in retirement hurt more), health risk, and the risk of financial dependence or fraud. A plan must address each, not just accumulate a large number.

5. Phases of retirement planning

Accumulation is the working-life phase of saving and growing the corpus. Transition is the few years before retirement when risk is reduced and plans are finalised. Distribution is the phase of drawing income. Different products and asset mixes suit each phase.

6. Start early: power of compounding

A 25-year-old investing ₹5,000 a month at 10% a year accumulates far more by 60 than a 40-year-old investing ₹15,000 a month at the same rate, despite contributing less in total. Delay is the costliest mistake in retirement planning.

7. Expenses after retirement

Some expenses fall (commuting, children's education, EMIs), while others rise (healthcare, travel, help at home). A replacement ratio of 70% to 80% of pre-retirement expenses is a rough starting point, but a line-by-line budget is better.

8. Role of the retirement adviser

The adviser assesses needs, estimates the corpus, recommends suitable products such as NPS, EPF, PPF, mutual funds and annuities, monitors progress and helps convert savings into income. PFRDA registers retirement advisers who must pass NISM XVII.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Future expense = current expense x (1 + inflation)^years.
  • Rule of 72: years to double = 72 / rate (at 6% inflation, about 12 years).

Common traps in the exam

  • Longevity risk is outliving savings, not dying early.
  • Sequence-of-returns risk matters most just before and just after retirement, not early in the career.
  • Expenses do not simply fall to zero after retirement; healthcare usually rises.
  • Starting early beats contributing more later because of compounding.

Quick recap

Longer lives, nuclear families and lack of pensions make self-funded retirement essential. Main risks: longevity, inflation, interest rate, market, sequence of returns, health. Phases: accumulation, transition, distribution. Inflate expenses: E x (1+i)^n; ₹40,000 at 6% for 20 years is about ₹1.28 lakh. Start early. Advisers register with PFRDA after NISM XVII.
