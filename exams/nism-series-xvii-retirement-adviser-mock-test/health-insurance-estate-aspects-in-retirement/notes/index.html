Health, Insurance & Estate Aspects in Retirement: NISM Series XVII revision notes

A retirement plan can fail because of one hospital bill or a messy inheritance. This chapter covers health insurance for retirees, the gap left by employer group cover, life insurance needs after retirement, and estate planning tools such as wills and nominations. Questions are mostly scenario-based: choose the most suitable advice for a client approaching retirement.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Medical inflation

Healthcare costs in India have historically risen faster than general inflation, and older people need care more often. A retirement budget must include health insurance premiums, out-of-pocket costs and long-term care, not just routine expenses.

2. Group cover gap

Employer group health cover usually ends at retirement. A 55-year-old relying only on group cover should buy an individual or family floater policy well before retirement, so that waiting periods for pre-existing diseases are served while still employed and premiums are lower.

3. Senior citizen policies

Policies for seniors often have co-payment (the insured pays a share of each claim), room rent limits and disease-wise sub-limits. Read these carefully. IRDAI removed the upper age limit for buying health insurance in 2024 and capped waiting periods for pre-existing conditions.

4. Top-up and super top-up

A top-up pays above a deductible for each claim; a super top-up pays when aggregate claims in a year exceed the deductible. A base cover of ₹5 lakh plus a super top-up of ₹20 lakh is often cheaper than a single ₹25 lakh base policy.

5. Government schemes

PM-JAY (Ayushman Bharat) was extended in 2024 to all citizens aged 70 and above regardless of income, with cover per family per year. CGHS covers central government pensioners. These complement, not replace, private cover for most middle-class retirees.

6. Life insurance after retirement

Once children are independent and loans repaid, the need for life cover usually falls. Term cover may still be useful if a spouse depends on the retiree's pension that will stop on death, or to cover outstanding liabilities.

7. Wills and succession

A will directs how assets pass after death and can appoint an executor. Without a will, assets pass under succession laws such as the Hindu Succession Act or the Indian Succession Act, which may not match the person's wishes. A will can be changed any time; the latest valid will prevails.

8. Nomination and joint holding

Nomination makes transfer easier for bank accounts, demat accounts, mutual funds, insurance and NPS. Courts have generally held that a nominee may receive assets as a trustee for legal heirs, so nomination does not replace a will. Joint holding with 'either or survivor' helps the spouse access funds quickly.

9. Power of attorney and other tools

A power of attorney helps someone manage finances if the retiree becomes unable to; it ends on death. Private trusts can manage assets for dependants with special needs. The Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007 allows seniors to claim maintenance from children.

Common traps in the exam

  • Nomination is not a substitute for a will; a nominee may hold assets for legal heirs.
  • Group health cover usually ends at retirement; buy individual cover before, not after.
  • A super top-up looks at aggregate yearly claims; a top-up looks at each claim separately.
  • A power of attorney ends on the death of the person who granted it.

Quick recap

Plan for medical inflation. Buy individual health cover before group cover ends, mind co-pays and sub-limits, use super top-ups for cheap extra cover. PM-JAY covers all 70+. Life cover need falls but may protect a dependent spouse. Write a will; nomination eases transfer but nominees may be trustees for heirs. Use joint holdings, POA and trusts sensibly.
