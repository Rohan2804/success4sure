Risk Management & Insurance Concepts: NISM Series X-B revision notes

The Investment Adviser Level 2 exam starts its insurance block by asking how an adviser should think about risk before choosing any product. This chapter covers risk identification, the four ways of handling risk, how insurance pools risk, and the legal principles that decide claims. Expect scenario questions that describe a claim and ask which principle applies.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Identifying personal risks

Households face risks to life (premature death), health (illness, disability), property (fire, theft, accidents), liability (legal claims by others) and income (job loss). The adviser lists these risks, estimates their likely frequency and severity, and checks which ones could derail the client's goals.

2. Four ways to handle risk

Avoidance means not taking the activity at all, such as not riding a motorbike. Reduction lowers frequency or severity, like installing smoke detectors. Retention means bearing small losses yourself through an emergency fund. Transfer moves the financial impact to someone else, mainly through insurance.

3. Frequency-severity matrix

Low-frequency, high-severity risks (early death, major illness, house fire) should be transferred through insurance. High-frequency, low-severity risks (minor repairs, small medical bills) are cheaper to retain. High-frequency, high-severity risks should be avoided. This matrix explains why buying insurance for small, predictable losses is inefficient.

4. Pooling of risk

Insurance works because many people facing similar risks pay premiums into a common pool from which the few who suffer losses are paid. The law of large numbers makes losses predictable for the insurer even though each individual loss is uncertain.

5. Utmost good faith

Both parties must disclose all material facts honestly. If a proposer hides a known heart condition, the insurer may repudiate the claim. Under the Insurance Act, a life policy cannot be called in question on any ground after three years from issue, which protects policyholders from old disputes.

6. Insurable interest

The insured must suffer a financial loss if the event occurs. A person has insurable interest in their own life, spouse and dependants, and in property they own. For life insurance it must exist at the time of the contract; for property insurance, at the time of loss.

7. Indemnity, subrogation and contribution

Indemnity means the insured is restored to the same financial position as before the loss, not better; it applies to general insurance, not life. Subrogation lets the insurer, after paying, step into the insured's shoes to recover from a third party at fault. Contribution shares the loss among multiple insurers covering the same property.

8. Proximate cause

A claim is payable only if the loss is caused by an insured peril as the dominant, effective cause. If a fire (insured) causes a wall to collapse, the collapse damage is covered; if an uninsured peril is the dominant cause, the claim may fail.

9. Insurance in the financial plan

Insurance protects goals from being derailed by shocks. An adviser addresses protection before building investment portfolios: an emergency fund, adequate term life cover, health cover and key general insurance come first, and investments are then planned around them.

Common traps in the exam

  • Applying indemnity to life insurance. Life policies pay the sum assured; they are not indemnity contracts.
  • Insuring small, frequent losses. Retention is cheaper; insurance suits low-frequency, high-severity risks.
  • Getting the timing of insurable interest wrong. For life, at inception; for property, at the time of loss.
  • Confusing subrogation with contribution. Subrogation recovers from a third party; contribution shares between insurers.

Quick recap

Identify risks, then avoid, reduce, retain or transfer them using the frequency-severity matrix. Insurance pools risk. Principles: utmost good faith, insurable interest, indemnity (not life), subrogation, contribution, proximate cause. Protection comes before investment in a financial plan.
