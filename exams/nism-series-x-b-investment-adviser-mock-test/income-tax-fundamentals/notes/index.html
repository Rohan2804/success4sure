Income Tax Fundamentals: NISM Series X-B revision notes

Advisers must understand how individuals are taxed in order to make sensible recommendations. This chapter covers residential status, heads of income, the two tax regimes, deductions, clubbing, loss set-off and advance tax. The Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act from 1 April 2026 with largely similar concepts and a single 'tax year', so focus on principles rather than section numbers.

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Residential status

An individual is resident in India for a year if present for 182 days or more, or 60 days in the year plus 365 days in the preceding four years (with modified rules for certain Indian citizens and visitors). Residents are taxed on global income; non-residents only on income arising or received in India.

2. Heads of income

Income is computed under five heads: salaries, income from house property, profits and gains of business or profession, capital gains, and income from other sources. Each head has its own deductions and rules, and the totals are combined to compute gross total income.

3. New vs old regime

The new regime is the default, with lower slab rates and few deductions; the old regime allows deductions such as provident fund, life insurance and ELSS, health insurance premiums and home loan interest. Salaried taxpayers can choose each year; those with business income have limited switching options.

4. Rebate and standard deduction

Under the new regime, a rebate makes total income up to ₹12 lakh effectively tax-free for residents, and salaried individuals get a standard deduction of ₹75,000 (as of 2026; check the current Finance Act). Rebate does not apply to special-rate income such as certain capital gains.

5. Surcharge and cess

Surcharge is an additional tax on high incomes, applied on tax at rates that increase with income bands. Health and education cess of 4% applies on tax plus surcharge. Marginal relief prevents the surcharge from exceeding the extra income above a threshold.

6. Clubbing of income

Income from assets transferred to a spouse without adequate consideration is taxed in the transferor's hands. A minor child's income is generally clubbed with the parent having higher income, with a small exemption per child, except income from the minor's own skill or manual work.

7. Set-off and carry forward

Losses can be set off against income in the same year subject to rules. A house property loss can be set off against other heads up to a limit. Short-term capital loss can be set off against short or long-term gains; long-term capital loss only against long-term gains. Most unabsorbed losses carry forward for eight years if the return is filed on time.

8. Advance tax

Taxpayers whose liability exceeds ₹10,000 must pay advance tax in instalments: 15% by 15 June, 45% by 15 September, 75% by 15 December and 100% by 15 March. Resident senior citizens without business income are exempt. Shortfalls attract interest.

9. Filing returns

Returns must be filed by the due date, typically 31 July for non-audit individual cases. Late filing attracts fees and loss of the right to carry forward certain losses. Updated returns allow correcting omissions within a time limit, with additional tax.

Common traps in the exam

  • Assuming non-residents are taxed on global income. Only residents are taxed on worldwide income.
  • Setting off long-term capital loss against short-term gains. It can only be set off against long-term gains.
  • Applying the rebate to special-rate capital gains. The rebate generally does not cover them.
  • Clubbing a minor's income from their own skill. That income is not clubbed.

Quick recap

Residential status decides scope of taxation. Five heads of income. New regime is the default with rebate up to ₹12 lakh and ₹75,000 standard deduction (check current year); old regime keeps deductions. Cess 4% on tax plus surcharge. Clubbing for spouse transfers and minors. LTCL only against LTCG. Advance tax 15/45/75/100.
