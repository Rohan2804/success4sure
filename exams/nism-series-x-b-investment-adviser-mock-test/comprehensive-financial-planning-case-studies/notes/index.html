Comprehensive Financial Planning & Case Studies: NISM Series X-B revision notes

The final chapter of the Investment Adviser Level 2 exam ties all modules into one client plan. Case-study questions present a family's data and ask what to address first, how to resolve conflicting goals and what to change when circumstances shift. Marks depend on logical, client-centred judgement as much as on correct calculations.

Last updated 19 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Planning process

The process is: establish the relationship and scope, gather data (quantitative and qualitative), analyse the current position, develop recommendations, present and agree on them, implement, and monitor and review. SEBI's investment adviser rules also require risk profiling and suitability assessment before advice.

2. Data gathering

Collect income, expenses, assets, liabilities, insurance, investments, tax details, goals with time frames, risk tolerance and family circumstances. Qualitative data such as values, attitudes to debt and family responsibilities often explains why a plan succeeds or fails. Incomplete data leads to flawed plans.

3. Analysing the current position

Build a personal balance sheet and cash flow statement. Key ratios include savings ratio (savings ÷ income), liquidity ratio (liquid assets ÷ monthly expenses), debt-to-income ratio (EMIs ÷ income) and solvency (net worth ÷ assets). These quickly reveal weaknesses.

4. Order of priorities

A common sequence: build an emergency fund of several months' expenses, secure adequate term life and health cover, repay high-cost debt such as credit cards, and then invest for goals. Retirement should not be sacrificed for children's goals because children can borrow for education, but retirees cannot borrow for retirement.

5. Resolving conflicting goals

When resources fall short, options include extending goal timelines, reducing goal amounts, increasing savings, improving asset allocation within risk tolerance or prioritising goals by importance. The adviser presents trade-offs clearly and lets the client decide with informed consent.

6. Integration across areas

Recommendations must work together: insurance needs reflect liabilities and goals; investment allocation reflects goal horizons; tax planning should not drive poor product choices; estate planning aligns nominations with the will. A tax-saving product that fails the suitability test is not a good recommendation.

7. Case example

A 38-year-old with two children, ₹20 lakh income, a ₹50 lakh home loan, only employer health cover and an endowment policy with ₹10 lakh cover. Priority: buy adequate term cover and family health cover, build an emergency fund, then restructure investments towards education and retirement goals.

8. Presenting recommendations

A good plan states assumptions, explains the reasoning for each recommendation, shows the impact on goals, discloses costs and conflicts, and gives action steps with responsibilities and dates. Clear documentation protects both client and adviser.

9. Monitoring and review

Plans need regular reviews, at least annually, and after life events such as job loss, inheritance, birth, divorce or a market crash. Reviews compare progress to targets, rebalance portfolios and update assumptions. A plan is a living document, not a one-time report.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Savings ratio = annual savings ÷ annual income; liquidity ratio = liquid assets ÷ monthly expenses; debt-service ratio = total EMIs ÷ monthly income.

Common traps in the exam

  • Investing before securing protection. Emergency fund and insurance come first.
  • Prioritising children's education over retirement without discussion. Education can be funded by loans; retirement cannot.
  • Recommending a product mainly for tax savings. Suitability must come first.
  • Treating the plan as complete after delivery. Monitoring and reviews are part of the process.

Quick recap

Process: engage, gather data, analyse, recommend, implement, monitor. Use ratios to diagnose. Priority: emergency fund, insurance, costly debt, then goals. Do not sacrifice retirement for children's goals. Integrate insurance, investments, tax and estate. Present assumptions and trade-offs clearly and review at least yearly and after life events.
