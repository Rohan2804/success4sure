Wealth Management & Estate Planning Concepts: NISM Series X-B revision notes

Estate planning ensures that wealth reaches the intended people smoothly when someone dies or becomes unable to manage their affairs. This chapter introduces wealth management for affluent clients and the tools for transferring wealth. Questions focus on roles: who is a nominee, legal heir, executor or trustee, and which tool suits which family situation.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Wealth management scope

Wealth management for high-net-worth clients integrates investment management, tax planning, risk management, succession and philanthropy. It goes beyond portfolio returns to protecting and transferring wealth across generations, often coordinating with lawyers and chartered accountants.

2. Nominee vs legal heir

A nominee receives assets from the institution, giving it a valid discharge, but courts have held that nomination generally does not override succession law. In 2023, the Supreme Court confirmed that nominees for shares do not become absolute owners; legal heirs under a will or succession law ultimately inherit. Nomination should therefore match the will.

3. Joint holding

Joint holding with 'either or survivor' mode allows the survivor to operate the account after one holder's death. It avoids freezing of accounts but does not decide ultimate ownership, which still follows the will or succession law. Joint holding is a convenience tool, not a substitute for a will.

4. Gifts

Gifts during one's lifetime transfer assets immediately. In India, gifts from specified relatives are tax-free in the recipient's hands, while gifts from non-relatives above ₹50,000 in a year are taxable. Gifts of immovable property need a registered gift deed. Clubbing rules may apply to gifts to a spouse or minor child.

5. Power of attorney

A power of attorney authorises another person to act on one's behalf, for example to manage bank accounts or property. A general POA covers broad acts; a specific POA covers defined acts. A POA ends on the death or, in most cases, the mental incapacity of the principal, so it cannot be used for estate transfer.

6. Wills

A will states how a person's assets will be distributed after death and names an executor to carry it out. It can be changed any number of times until death; the latest valid will prevails. Without a will, assets pass under the applicable succession law, which may not match the person's wishes.

7. Trusts

A trust transfers assets to trustees to be held for beneficiaries. Private trusts help with protecting assets for minors or dependants with special needs, managing family business holdings and avoiding fragmentation. They can operate during the settlor's lifetime and continue after death.

8. Roles to distinguish

Executor: carries out the will. Trustee: manages trust assets for beneficiaries. Nominee: receives assets from an institution. Legal heir: entitled under succession law. Beneficiary: receives benefit under a will or trust. Exam questions often swap these roles.

9. Records and documentation

Estate planning fails when families cannot find assets. A record of accounts, policies, demat holdings, property documents, loans and digital assets, kept with key contacts, saves months of effort. Updating nominations, wills and records after life events is essential for everyone, not only the very wealthy.

Common traps in the exam

  • Treating a nominee as the ultimate owner. Legal heirs or will beneficiaries usually prevail.
  • Assuming a power of attorney survives death. It ends on the principal's death.
  • Believing joint holding settles ownership. It allows operation by the survivor, but ownership follows the will or law.
  • Thinking estate planning is only for the rich. Every family benefits from nominations, a will and organised records.

Quick recap

Wealth management integrates investment, tax, risk and succession. Nominees receive; legal heirs own. Joint holding gives access, not ownership. Gifts from relatives are tax-free; from others above ₹50,000 taxable. POA ends at death. Wills direct distribution through an executor; trusts manage assets for beneficiaries. Keep records updated.
