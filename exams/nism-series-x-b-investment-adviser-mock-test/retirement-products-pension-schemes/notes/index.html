Retirement Products & Pension Schemes: NISM Series X-B revision notes

Indian retirement savers have many products, each with its own rules on contributions, lock-in, withdrawals and taxes. This chapter compares them so that an adviser can match products to clients. Limits and exit rules change, so focus on structure and suitability, and use exact figures only with the year in mind.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. EPF and EPS

Under the Employees' Provident Fund scheme, employee and employer each contribute 12% of basic wages plus DA. Of the employer's share, 8.33% (on wages up to ₹15,000 a month) goes to the Employees' Pension Scheme, which pays a monthly pension after ten years of service. EPF interest is declared annually by EPFO.

2. Voluntary Provident Fund

Employees can contribute more than 12% to their PF account as VPF, earning the same interest rate. Interest on employee contributions above a threshold is taxable, which reduces VPF's appeal for high earners.

3. Public Provident Fund

PPF is a government-backed account with a 15-year term, extendable in blocks of five years, with annual contributions between ₹500 and ₹1.5 lakh. Partial withdrawals and loans are allowed after specified years. Interest is set quarterly by the government and is tax-free.

4. NPS Tier I and Tier II

The National Pension System, regulated by PFRDA, has a Tier I pension account with restrictions on withdrawal and a Tier II voluntary account with free withdrawals. Subscribers choose active or auto choice across equity, corporate bonds and government securities, with lifecycle funds reducing equity exposure with age.

5. NPS exit rules

At normal exit, a part of the corpus must be used to buy an annuity and the rest can be withdrawn. PFRDA relaxed these rules in 2025, reducing the minimum annuity requirement for many non-government subscribers (check current PFRDA rules). Premature exit requires a larger annuity share.

6. Atal Pension Yojana

APY targets unorganised-sector workers aged 18–40, offering a guaranteed monthly pension of ₹1,000 to ₹5,000 from age 60 depending on contribution and joining age. On the subscriber's death, the spouse gets the pension and then the nominee gets the corpus.

7. Senior citizens' schemes

The Senior Citizens' Savings Scheme offers a five-year deposit for those aged 60 and above, with quarterly interest and an investment ceiling. The Pradhan Mantri Vaya Vandana Yojana has closed for new investment. Bank senior citizen FDs offer additional interest.

8. Annuity products

Annuities from life insurers convert a corpus into lifelong income. Options include life annuity, joint life with last survivor, and return of purchase price. Annuity rates are fixed at purchase, so buying when interest rates are low locks in lower income.

9. Retirement-oriented mutual funds

Retirement solution-oriented mutual fund schemes have a lock-in of five years or until retirement, whichever is earlier. They are flexible in asset allocation but offer no guaranteed income. They suit clients who want market-linked growth with built-in discipline.

Common traps in the exam

  • Assuming the full employer contribution goes to EPF. A part goes to EPS, calculated on capped wages.
  • Treating NPS Tier II like Tier I. Tier II is voluntary, flexible and has no retirement lock-in.
  • Thinking APY is open to anyone at any age. It is for ages 18–40 joining.
  • Believing annuity income is tax-free. It is taxable as income in the year received.

Quick recap

EPF: 12% + 12%, with part of employer share to EPS. PPF: 15 years, ₹500–₹1.5 lakh a year, tax-free. NPS: Tier I locked to retirement with partial annuity at exit; Tier II flexible. APY: guaranteed pension, entry 18–40. SCSS for 60+. Annuities lock in rates. Check current limits before advising.
