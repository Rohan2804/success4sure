Retirement Planning Concepts: NISM Series X-B revision notes

Retirement is the longest and most expensive goal most clients have. This chapter explains how to think about retirement before choosing products: the phases of retirement, estimating expenses, the effects of inflation and longevity, sequence-of-returns risk and the power of starting early. Expect conceptual questions and simple projections.

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Phases of retirement

Retirement planning has an accumulation phase (building the corpus during working years), a transition phase around retirement, and a distribution phase in which the corpus is drawn down. Within retirement, active early years often cost more on travel, while later years cost more on health and care.

2. Estimating retirement expenses

Start with current expenses, remove items that will end (children's education, EMIs, commuting), add new ones (health, travel, domestic help), and project to retirement using inflation. A family spending ₹50,000 a month today on items that continue may need about ₹1.6 lakh a month in 20 years at 6% inflation.

3. Income replacement ratio

Some planners express retirement need as a percentage of pre-retirement income, often 70–80%. This is a shortcut; a detailed expense-based estimate is more reliable because it accounts for the client's actual lifestyle and obligations.

4. Inflation risk

Inflation erodes purchasing power: at 6% inflation, prices double in about 12 years (rule of 72). A fixed pension that looks adequate at 60 may cover only half the same expenses at 72. Retirement income must therefore include growth assets or inflation-linked components.

5. Longevity risk

Longevity risk is the risk of outliving savings. Life expectancy at 60 in India is rising, and many retirees live into their late 80s or 90s. Planning to age 85 or 90, rather than average life expectancy, reduces the chance of running out of money.

6. Sequence-of-returns risk

Poor returns early in retirement, while withdrawals are happening, damage a corpus far more than the same poor returns later. Two retirees with identical average returns can end up very differently depending on the order of returns. Keeping a few years of expenses in stable assets helps manage this.

7. Real rate of return

What matters is return after inflation. If a portfolio earns 8% and inflation is 6%, the real return is about 1.9% using (1.08 ÷ 1.06) − 1. Corpus calculations use real returns to capture the effect of rising expenses.

8. Starting early

Compounding rewards early savers. Saving ₹10,000 a month from age 25 to 60 at 10% annual return builds roughly ₹3.8 crore, while starting at 35 produces only about ₹1.3 crore. Delaying ten years cuts the corpus by about two-thirds for the same monthly saving.

9. Levers available

If the projected corpus falls short, clients can save more, invest in higher-return assets within their risk tolerance, work longer, retire later, reduce planned expenses or plan part-time income. Each lever has trade-offs that the adviser must discuss.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Real return = (1 + nominal return) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1.
  • Future expense = current expense × (1 + inflation)^years; e.g. ₹50,000 × 1.06^20 ≈ ₹1.6 lakh a month.

Common traps in the exam

  • Using nominal returns in corpus calculations without adjusting for inflation. Use the real rate.
  • Planning only to average life expectancy. Plan to a conservative age such as 85–90.
  • Ignoring the order of returns. Losses early in retirement hurt more than the same losses later.
  • Assuming expenses fall sharply at retirement. Health and leisure costs often offset savings on work-related items.

Quick recap

Retirement: accumulation, transition, distribution. Estimate continuing expenses and inflate them. Manage inflation, longevity and sequence risk. Use real returns: (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. Starting early is the biggest lever; otherwise save more, work longer or spend less.
