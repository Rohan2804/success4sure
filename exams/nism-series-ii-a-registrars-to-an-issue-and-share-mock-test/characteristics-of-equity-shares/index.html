Characteristics of Equity Shares: NISM Series II-A practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on Characteristics of Equity Shares, from the NISM Series II-A (Registrars to an Issue and Share Transfer Agents – Corporate) syllabus.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter explains the nature of equity shares: face value, issue price, securities premium, market capitalisation, dividends and voting rights, along with special types such as partly paid shares, sweat equity and shares with differential voting rights. It also covers corporate actions that change the number or face value of shares, like bonus, split and consolidation, and the reserves a company may use for a bonus issue. Practise simple calculations of dividend, premium, market capitalisation and post-consolidation holdings, since RTAs apply these to shareholder records.

Characteristics of Equity Shares questions

Question 1

A company has 4.5 crore equity shares outstanding and its share price is ₹320. What is its market capitalisation?

  1. ₹1,440 crore
  2. ₹144 crore
  3. ₹14,400 crore
  4. ₹1,280 crore
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. ₹1,440 crore

Market capitalisation equals shares outstanding multiplied by market price: 4.5 crore × ₹320 = ₹1,440 crore. Face value is not used in this calculation.

Question 2

A company issues equity shares of face value ₹10 at ₹150 per share. How much per share is credited to the securities premium account?

  1. ₹150
  2. ₹140
  3. ₹10
  4. ₹160
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. ₹140

Only the face value of ₹10 goes to share capital. The excess of the issue price over face value, ₹150 − ₹10 = ₹140, is credited to the securities premium account.

Question 3

A company with shares of face value ₹2 declares a dividend of 150%. How much will a shareholder holding 600 shares receive (ignoring tax)?

  1. ₹180
  2. ₹1,200
  3. ₹18,000
  4. ₹1,800
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. ₹1,800

Dividend percentages are applied to face value. 150% of ₹2 is ₹3 per share, and ₹3 × 600 shares = ₹1,800. Applying the rate to market price would be a common mistake.

Question 4

A company consolidates its shares of face value ₹2 each into shares of face value ₹10 each. An investor holding 1,250 shares will now hold:

  1. 6,250 shares
  2. 125 shares
  3. 2,500 shares
  4. 250 shares
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. 250 shares

Consolidation is the reverse of a split. Five shares of ₹2 become one share of ₹10, so 1,250 ÷ 5 = 250 shares. The total face value of ₹2,500 remains unchanged.

Question 5

Equity shares that carry voting rights different from those of ordinary equity shares are known as:

  1. Shares with differential voting rights
  2. Sweat equity shares
  3. Bonus shares
  4. Partly paid-up shares with unpaid calls outstanding
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Shares with differential voting rights

DVR shares give more or fewer votes per share than ordinary shares. Sweat equity is issued for know-how or services, bonus shares are issued free from reserves, and partly paid shares relate to unpaid calls.

Question 6

Sweat equity shares are issued by a company to:

  1. Employees or directors for providing know-how, intellectual property or value additions
  2. Existing shareholders free of cost by capitalising the company's accumulated free reserves
  3. Qualified institutional buyers through a placement document
  4. Holders of convertible bonds on the date of conversion
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Employees or directors for providing know-how, intellectual property or value additions

Sweat equity rewards employees or directors for know-how, IPR or value addition, at a discount or for non-cash consideration. Free shares to existing holders are bonus shares, and QIB placements are QIPs.

Question 7

Ravi holds partly paid shares of face value ₹10 on which ₹4 has been paid up. If the company is wound up, Ravi:

  1. Can be required to pay the unpaid ₹6 per share
  2. Has no further liability on these shares once wound up
  3. Must pay the full ₹10 per share again
  4. Becomes a creditor for ₹6 per share
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Can be required to pay the unpaid ₹6 per share

A shareholder's liability is limited to the unpaid amount on the shares held. Ravi has paid ₹4, so he can be called upon for the remaining ₹6 per share, but nothing beyond that.

Question 8

Under the Companies Act, 2013, which of the following can NOT be used to issue fully paid bonus shares?

  1. Free reserves
  2. Securities premium account
  3. Revaluation reserve
  4. Capital redemption reserve account
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Revaluation reserve

Section 63 allows bonus shares out of free reserves, securities premium or capital redemption reserve, but specifically bars reserves created by revaluation of assets, since these are not realised gains.
