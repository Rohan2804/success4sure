Introduction to Securities: NISM Series II-A practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on Introduction to Securities, from the NISM Series II-A (Registrars to an Issue and Share Transfer Agents – Corporate) syllabus.

What this chapter covers

This chapter introduces what securities are, the laws that define and govern them, and how the primary and secondary markets differ. Learners should be clear on the main types of instruments (equity, debt, hybrids, money market instruments), the role of the key regulators and laws such as the SCRA 1956, the Depositories Act 1996 and the Companies Act 2013, and where an RTA fits among market intermediaries. Focus on distinguishing capital market from money market instruments and on who receives money when securities change hands.

Introduction to Securities questions

Question 1

In India, the term 'securities' for the purposes of the securities market is defined in which law?

  1. Indian Contract Act, 1872, read with the Companies Act, 2013
  2. Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956
  3. Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881
  4. Sale of Goods Act, 1930
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956

Section 2(h) of the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956 defines securities to include shares, debentures, units of collective schemes, government securities and other instruments. The other laws deal with contracts, cheques and goods, not securities.

Question 2

Meena buys 100 shares of a listed company on the stock exchange from another investor. Which statement about this trade is correct?

  1. The company receives no money; the price is paid to the selling investor
  2. The company receives the price as fresh capital for its business plans
  3. The RTA collects the price and credits it to the company's bank account
  4. The exchange keeps the price until the company's next annual general meeting
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. The company receives no money; the price is paid to the selling investor

A secondary market trade is between investors. The selling investor receives the price; the issuer raises no new money. Companies receive money only when they issue fresh securities in the primary market.

Question 3

Which of the following is a money market instrument rather than a capital market instrument?

  1. A 10-year government bond
  2. An equity share of a listed company
  3. A five-year non-convertible debenture
  4. A 91-day treasury bill
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. A 91-day treasury bill

Money market instruments have an original maturity of up to one year and are used for short-term funding. A 91-day T-bill qualifies; long-dated bonds, debentures and equity shares are capital market instruments.

Question 4

Capital market instruments are generally those whose original maturity is:

  1. Up to 14 days only
  2. Exactly one year and not beyond
  3. More than one year, or with no fixed maturity
  4. Between 91 days and 364 days only, as fixed by the RBI
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. More than one year, or with no fixed maturity

The capital market deals in long-term funds: bonds and debentures with maturities above one year, and equity shares that have no maturity at all. Short-term paper of up to one year belongs to the money market.

Question 5

Government of India dated securities and treasury bills are issued, on behalf of the Government, by:

  1. The Reserve Bank of India
  2. The Securities and Exchange Board of India
  3. The National Stock Exchange of India
  4. The Registrar of Companies
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. The Reserve Bank of India

The RBI acts as the debt manager of the Government and conducts auctions of G-secs and T-bills. SEBI regulates the securities market, while stock exchanges only provide trading platforms for these instruments.

Question 6

Which security combines features of both debt and equity?

  1. A treasury bill
  2. A commercial paper
  3. A convertible debenture
  4. An ordinary equity share
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. A convertible debenture

A convertible debenture pays interest like debt until it converts into equity shares, so it is a hybrid. Treasury bills and commercial paper are pure short-term debt, while equity shares carry only ownership features.

Question 7

A listed company's continuing disclosure obligations, such as intimating record dates and filing shareholding patterns, mainly arise under:

  1. SEBI (Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2018
  2. SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015
  3. SEBI (Substantial Acquisition of Shares and Takeovers) Regulations, 2011
  4. Foreign Exchange Management (Non-debt Instruments) Rules, 2019
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015

LODR Regulations set the ongoing obligations of listed entities after listing. ICDR governs the issue of capital, the Takeover Regulations deal with acquisitions, and the FEMA rules cover foreign investment.

Question 8

The legal framework that allows securities to be held and transferred in electronic book-entry form in India is provided mainly by:

  1. The Information Technology Act, 2000
  2. The Banking Regulation Act, 1949
  3. The Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007
  4. The Depositories Act, 1996
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. The Depositories Act, 1996

The Depositories Act, 1996 enabled depositories to hold securities in dematerialised form and made the depository the registered owner, with investors as beneficial owners. The other Acts cover electronic records, banks and payment systems.
