Risk and Return Fundamentals: NISM Series XXV-A practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on Risk and Return Fundamentals, from the NISM Series XXV-A (Persons Associated with Research Services (Sales and Other Non-Core Services)) syllabus.

Last updated 16 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter explains the basic concepts of risk and return that underpin research recommendations. It covers measures of return (holding period, annualised, CAGR, total return), measures of risk (standard deviation, beta, drawdown), systematic and unsystematic risk, diversification, risk-adjusted return (Sharpe ratio), the time value of money and the relationship between risk and expected return. It also covers communicating risk honestly to clients. Practise basic return calculations and the interpretation of risk measures.

Risk and Return Fundamentals questions

Question 1

A share bought at ₹400 is worth ₹576 after 2 years with no dividends. Its CAGR is:

  1. 22%
  2. 20%
  3. 44%
  4. 18%
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. 20%

CAGR = (576 / 400)^(1/2) − 1 = (1.44)^0.5 − 1 = 1.2 − 1 = 20%. Dividing the 44% total gain by two gives 22%, which ignores compounding.

Question 2

A stock with beta of 0.6 is best described as:

  1. Twice as volatile as the market at all times
  2. Less volatile than the market, on average
  3. Completely free of market risk
  4. Guaranteed to rise when the market falls
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Less volatile than the market, on average

A beta below 1 means the stock tends to move less than the market. It still carries market risk and does not necessarily move opposite to the market.

Question 3

A client sees that a research report's model portfolio fell 30% from its peak before recovering. This peak-to-trough fall is called:

  1. Maximum drawdown
  2. Tracking error relative to the benchmark
  3. Dividend yield on the portfolio
  4. Expense ratio of the portfolio
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Maximum drawdown

Maximum drawdown measures the largest fall from a peak to a subsequent trough. It helps clients understand how bad losses have been in the past.

Question 4

Which statement about diversification is correct?

  1. It reduces company-specific risk but not market risk
  2. It removes all types of investment risk completely
  3. It increases risk because more stocks are held
  4. It works only for government bonds and not for equities
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. It reduces company-specific risk but not market risk

Holding many different securities offsets company-specific shocks, but market-wide events such as recessions or rate changes affect all stocks. That systematic risk remains.

Question 5

A portfolio returned 14% with standard deviation 16%; the risk-free rate is 6%. Its Sharpe ratio is:

  1. 0.875
  2. 1.25
  3. 0.50
  4. 0.375
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. 0.50

Sharpe ratio = (14 − 6) ÷ 16 = 0.50. It measures excess return per unit of total risk, helping compare portfolios with different volatility.

Question 6

A client says, 'This stock has never fallen, so it's safe.' The most appropriate response is that:

  1. The stock is risk-free because it has not fallen before
  2. Past performance does not guarantee future results
  3. SEBI guarantees stocks that have never fallen
  4. Only bonds can fall in value
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Past performance does not guarantee future results

Any equity can fall in future, whatever its history. Research communications must make clear that past performance is not indicative of future returns.

Question 7

A dividend of ₹8 on a share bought at ₹200, which is now ₹210, gives a total return of:

  1. 5%
  2. 9%
  3. 4%
  4. 4.2%
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. 9%

Total return = (price gain + dividend) ÷ cost = (10 + 8) ÷ 200 = 9%. Price return alone is 5%, and dividend yield on cost is 4%.

Question 8

Standard deviation as a risk measure treats:

  1. Only losses below zero as risk
  2. Upside and downside deviations equally
  3. Only the single worst day as risk
  4. Only dividends as a source of risk
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Upside and downside deviations equally

Standard deviation measures dispersion around the average in both directions. Measures like downside deviation focus only on returns below a target.
