Commodity Futures: NISM Series XVI revision notes

Commodity futures are the core product in NISM XVI and carry the most questions. The chapter covers contract specifications, settlement types, pricing through the cost of carry including storage and insurance, convenience yield, basis and the behaviour of futures prices near expiry. Practise a few fair value calculations and memorise the meaning of each specification field.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Contract specifications

Each contract specifies the commodity and its quality, trading unit, delivery unit, price quote basis (for example ₹ per 10 grams for gold, ₹ per quintal for many agri goods), tick size, daily price limits, delivery centres and expiry date. A gold mini contract and a gold contract differ in lot size, not quality.

2. Settlement types

Contracts can be compulsory delivery (all open positions at expiry must deliver or take delivery), both-option delivery (delivery only when both buyer and seller choose it) or cash settled. Many agricultural contracts on NCDEX are compulsory delivery; some metals and energy contracts on MCX are cash settled against international benchmarks.

3. Cost of carry

Fair futures price = spot + interest cost + storage + insurance - convenience yield. If chana spot is ₹5,500 per quintal, interest is 9% a year, and storage and insurance cost ₹40 for three months: fair futures is about 5,500 + 124 + 40 = ₹5,664, ignoring convenience yield.

4. Convenience yield

Holding the physical commodity gives benefits, such as keeping a factory running during shortages. This convenience yield lowers the futures price relative to spot. When stocks are tight, convenience yield can exceed carry costs and push futures below spot (backwardation).

5. Basis

Basis is spot minus futures. It reflects carry costs, local supply and demand, and quality and location differences. A strong local harvest can widen negative basis at a mandi far from the delivery centre. At expiry, basis for delivery-centre spot converges toward zero.

6. Seasonality

Agricultural prices are influenced by sowing, monsoon and harvest. Futures for months after harvest often trade lower than those before harvest. Traders and hedgers watch crop estimates, MSP announcements and import duty changes.

7. Tender and delivery period

Before expiry, compulsory-delivery contracts enter a tender or staggered delivery period when sellers can give intention to deliver. Additional delivery period margins apply. Buyers receive goods through accredited warehouses with quality certified by assayers.

8. Price limits

Daily price limits (DPL) cap how far prices can move in a day, with relaxation after a cooling-off period if the limit is hit. International-linked contracts like gold and crude have wider or flexible bands that follow global prices.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Fair futures price = spot x (1 + r x t) + storage + insurance - convenience yield.
  • Basis = spot price - futures price.

Common traps in the exam

  • Convenience yield reduces the fair futures price; storage and insurance increase it.
  • Both-option delivery requires both buyer and seller to opt for delivery; otherwise it cash settles.
  • Mini contracts differ in size, not in the grade of the commodity.
  • Backwardation in commodities is common during shortages because of high convenience yield.

Quick recap

Commodity futures specify quality, unit, quote, tick, DPL, delivery centre and expiry. Settlement: compulsory delivery, both-option or cash. Fair value = spot + interest + storage + insurance - convenience yield. Basis = spot minus futures and converges at expiry. Seasonality drives agri prices. Delivery via accredited warehouses and assayers, with tender period and delivery margins.
