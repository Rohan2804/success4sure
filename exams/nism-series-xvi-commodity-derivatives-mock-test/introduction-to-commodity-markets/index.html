What this chapter covers

This chapter introduces commodities as an asset class: how physical (spot) markets work, the difference between forward and futures contracts, the evolution of commodity derivatives trading in India and the roles of exchanges, hedgers, speculators and arbitrageurs. It also covers the ecosystem that supports physical delivery, such as warehouses, assayers and electronic warehouse receipts. When revising, focus on why commodity derivatives exist (price discovery and price-risk management), how forwards differ from exchange-traded futures, and the classification of commodities into agricultural, bullion, base metals and energy.

Introduction to Commodity Markets questions

Question 1

Compared with an over-the-counter forward contract, an exchange-traded commodity futures contract mainly removes which risk for the two parties?

  1. Price risk, because futures prices cannot move against either party
  2. Basis risk, because futures always converge to the local mandi price at expiry
  3. Liquidity risk in the underlying physical commodity market
  4. Counterparty default risk, as the clearing corporation guarantees settlement
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Counterparty default risk, as the clearing corporation guarantees settlement

The clearing corporation becomes the counterparty to every trade and collects margins, so default risk is largely removed. Price risk remains with the position holder, and basis risk still exists between futures and local spot prices.

Question 2

A trader buys silver futures purely because he expects prices to rise over the next week and has no exposure to physical silver. He is best described as a:

  1. Hedger
  2. Speculator
  3. Arbitrageur
  4. Market maker
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Speculator

A speculator takes a position to profit from expected price moves without an underlying physical exposure. A hedger offsets an existing risk, while an arbitrageur exploits price differences between markets.

Question 3

Which of the following is a key economic function of commodity futures markets?

  1. Fixing minimum support prices for agricultural crops for each crop season
  2. Providing working-capital loans to farmers against stored crops
  3. Setting import duties on bullion and edible oils
  4. Price discovery, giving producers and users a forward reference price
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Price discovery, giving producers and users a forward reference price

Futures markets aggregate the views of many participants and so discover expected future prices. MSP is fixed by the government, loans come from banks and duties are a fiscal decision.

Question 4

Copper, zinc, aluminium and nickel are grouped in commodity markets as:

  1. Bullion
  2. Energy commodities
  3. Base metals
  4. Soft commodities
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Base metals

Industrial non-ferrous metals such as copper, zinc, aluminium and nickel are called base metals. Bullion refers to precious metals like gold and silver, and soft commodities are agricultural products.

Question 5

An electronic negotiable warehouse receipt (e-NWR) is useful to a farmer mainly because it:

  1. Guarantees that the produce will be sold at the minimum support price announced by government
  2. Can be pledged for finance or transferred without moving the produce
  3. Exempts the stored produce from quality testing by the warehouse
  4. Converts the produce automatically into an open futures position
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Can be pledged for finance or transferred without moving the produce

An e-NWR is a transferable electronic title to goods stored in a registered warehouse, so it can be used as collateral or traded. It does not guarantee any price and stored goods are still graded.

Question 6

Why are commodity markets typically more exposed to seasonality than equity markets?

  1. Commodity exchanges are open only during the harvest months
  2. Commodity prices are fixed by the government outside the harvest season
  3. Supply of many agricultural commodities depends on sowing and harvest cycles
  4. Equity prices are set annually while commodity prices change monthly
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Supply of many agricultural commodities depends on sowing and harvest cycles

Agricultural output arrives in harvest seasons, so supply, stocks and prices follow seasonal patterns. Exchanges trade all year and prices are market-determined, not fixed by government.

Question 7

A jeweller in Jaipur buys gold in the spot market and simultaneously sells gold futures when futures trade well above the full cost of carry. The jeweller is acting as a/an:

  1. Speculator, betting that gold prices will rise sharply over the coming months
  2. Arbitrageur, locking in the gap over carrying cost
  3. Hedger of a future purchase requirement
  4. Market maker earning only the bid-ask spread
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Arbitrageur, locking in the gap over carrying cost

Buying spot and selling overpriced futures (cash-and-carry) locks in a near riskless gain if futures exceed spot plus carrying costs. It is an arbitrage, not a directional bet or a purchase hedge.

Question 8

Which statement about participants in Indian commodity derivatives markets is correct as per the current framework?

  1. Eligible FPIs may trade non-agricultural commodity derivatives, subject to SEBI conditions
  2. Only farmers and farmer producer organisations may trade agricultural futures on exchanges
  3. Mutual funds are prohibited from taking any exposure at all to commodity derivatives
  4. Retail individuals may trade only options and not futures
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Eligible FPIs may trade non-agricultural commodity derivatives, subject to SEBI conditions

SEBI has opened commodity derivatives to institutional participants such as eligible FPIs (in non-agricultural contracts), mutual funds and PMS within limits. Retail investors may trade both futures and options.
