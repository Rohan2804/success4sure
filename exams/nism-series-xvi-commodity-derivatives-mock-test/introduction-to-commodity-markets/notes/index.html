Introduction to Commodity Markets: NISM Series XVI revision notes

The first chapter of NISM Series XVI introduces commodities as an asset class and traces how Indian commodity markets evolved. It covers spot and derivatives markets, the shift of regulation from the Forward Markets Commission to SEBI, the national exchanges, and the role of warehousing. Questions are mostly factual: dates, institutions, classifications and the reasons commodity derivatives exist.

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. What makes a commodity

A commodity is a basic good that is broadly interchangeable with other units of the same grade, such as wheat of a defined quality, gold of 995 purity or crude oil of a stated grade. Standardisation of grade and quantity is what allows commodities to be traded on exchanges.

2. Classification

Commodities are usually grouped as agricultural (cereals, pulses, oilseeds, spices, cotton, guar), metals (precious such as gold and silver; base metals such as copper, zinc, aluminium) and energy (crude oil, natural gas). Agricultural commodities are seasonal and perishable, so storage and quality matter more.

3. Spot markets

Physical commodities trade in mandis regulated under state APMC laws, through traders and commission agents, and increasingly through electronic platforms like e-NAM. Spot prices vary by location and quality. Fragmented spot markets are one reason futures prices provide a useful national reference.

4. Evolution of regulation

Commodity futures were regulated under the Forward Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1952 by the Forward Markets Commission. In 2003 national multi-commodity exchanges were set up. In September 2015 the FMC merged with SEBI and the FCRA was repealed; commodity derivatives became securities under SCRA.

5. Exchanges

MCX is the largest exchange for metals and energy; NCDEX focuses on agricultural commodities. NSE and BSE also offer commodity derivatives after SEBI allowed universal exchanges in 2018. Each exchange works with a clearing corporation, such as MCXCCL for MCX.

6. Why commodity derivatives

They let farmers, traders, processors, jewellers and importers hedge price risk, support price discovery across the country, and give investors exposure to commodities. A dal mill can lock in the price of tur months ahead, and a jeweller can hedge gold inventory.

7. Participants

Hedgers (producers, processors, exporters, importers), speculators, arbitrageurs, and institutional participants such as mutual funds, AIFs, portfolio managers and eligible foreign entities with exposure to Indian commodities. Farmer producer organisations (FPOs) aggregate small farmers to use exchanges.

8. Warehousing

Delivery-based commodity contracts need accredited warehouses, assayers to certify quality and electronic warehouse receipts. The Warehousing Development and Regulatory Authority (WDRA), under the 2007 Act, regulates warehouses issuing negotiable warehouse receipts, which can be used as collateral for loans.

Common traps in the exam

  • FMC merged into SEBI in September 2015; FCRA was repealed then, and SCRA now governs commodity derivatives.
  • NCDEX is agri-focused; MCX dominates metals and energy.
  • WDRA regulates warehouses and receipts; it does not regulate commodity exchanges.
  • Standardised grade and quantity are essential for exchange trading; unique goods cannot be commoditised.

Quick recap

Commodities are standardised, interchangeable goods: agri, metals, energy. Spot trade happens in APMC mandis and e-NAM. FCRA 1952 and FMC regulated futures until the 2015 merger with SEBI; SCRA now applies. MCX leads metals and energy, NCDEX agri; NSE and BSE also trade. Participants include hedgers, speculators, arbitrageurs, FPOs and institutions. WDRA regulates warehouse receipts.
