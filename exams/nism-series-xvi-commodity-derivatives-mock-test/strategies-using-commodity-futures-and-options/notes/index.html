Strategies using Commodity Futures and Options: NISM Series XVI revision notes

This chapter applies futures and options to real business problems: a farmer protecting harvest prices, a mill fixing raw material cost, a jeweller managing inventory and traders exploiting spreads. Questions are scenario-based and often include a small calculation of the effective price after hedging. Think through who is exposed to rising or falling prices before choosing a position.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Short hedge for producers

A soybean farmer or a stockist holding inventory fears falling prices and sells futures. If spot falls from ₹4,800 to ₹4,500 per quintal and futures fall by a similar amount, the futures gain offsets the lower spot sale, giving an effective price close to the original level.

2. Long hedge for consumers

A refinery, mill or jeweller that must buy later fears rising prices and buys futures. A cotton mill buying cotton futures in March for October requirements locks in an input cost; if prices rise, futures gains offset the higher purchase price.

3. Basis risk

Hedges are imperfect when the local spot price moves differently from the futures price because of location, quality or timing. A farmer in a region with a bumper crop may see local prices fall more than futures. The effective price = futures price at hedge + basis at close.

4. Hedge ratio

The hedge ratio is the number of futures units per unit of exposure. A simple hedge uses a ratio of one. When the hedged item differs from the contract commodity, the optimal ratio is based on correlation and relative volatility of price changes.

5. Calendar spreads

Buy one month and sell another month of the same commodity to profit from changes in the price difference. If the June-August gold spread is unusually wide relative to carry cost, a trader can sell August and buy June. Spreads have lower margins because the legs offset.

6. Inter-commodity spreads

Trade related commodities, such as the soybean crush spread (soybean versus soy oil and soymeal) or the crack spread (crude versus refined products). A processor's margin depends on these spreads, so spreads can hedge processing margins.

7. Arbitrage

Cash and carry arbitrage: buy spot and sell futures when futures exceed fair value including storage and insurance, then deliver at expiry. Inter-exchange arbitrage exploits price differences of the same commodity on two exchanges or between Indian and international prices adjusted for duty and currency.

8. Option strategies

Farmers can buy puts to set a minimum price while keeping upside. Processors can buy calls to cap costs. A collar (buy put, sell call) reduces premium cost but limits upside. Covered call writing by stockists earns premium on inventory.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Effective price for a short hedger = initial futures price + basis at the time of closing (basis = spot - futures).
  • Minimum variance hedge ratio = correlation x (standard deviation of spot changes / standard deviation of futures changes).

Common traps in the exam

  • Producers and stockists hedge by selling futures; consumers and processors hedge by buying.
  • A perfect hedge exists only when basis does not change; basis risk is the residual risk.
  • Spread positions usually have lower margins than outright positions because the legs offset.
  • Buying puts gives a floor price without margin calls, but costs premium upfront.

Quick recap

Producers and stockists sell futures (short hedge); consumers and processors buy (long hedge). Basis risk remains: effective price = initial futures + closing basis. Hedge ratio may differ from one when commodities differ. Calendar and inter-commodity spreads (crush, crack) trade price differences. Arbitrage compares futures with full carry cost. Puts give floors, calls give caps, collars reduce cost.
