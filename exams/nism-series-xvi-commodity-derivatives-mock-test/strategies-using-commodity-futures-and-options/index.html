What this chapter covers

This chapter applies futures and options to real needs: hedging by producers, processors, traders and consumers, speculation, arbitrage and spread trading. Topics include short and long hedges, basis risk, hedge ratios and the number of contracts, calendar and inter-commodity spreads (such as crack and crush spreads), and option strategies like protective puts, covered calls, bull and bear spreads, straddles and strangles. When revising, practise identifying the right hedge for a business exposure, computing effective prices after a hedge and finding the maximum profit, maximum loss and break-even of common option strategies.

Strategies using Commodity Futures and Options questions

Question 1

A trader buys a ₹6,000 strike call at ₹180 and sells a ₹6,200 strike call at ₹90 on the same commodity and expiry. His maximum profit per unit is:

  1. ₹110
  2. ₹200
  3. ₹90
  4. ₹290
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. ₹110

This bull call spread costs a net ₹90 (180 − 90). The maximum pay-off is the strike difference of ₹200, so maximum profit = 200 − 90 = ₹110. The maximum loss is the ₹90 net premium.

Question 2

A trader buys a gold call and a gold put, both with strike ₹72,000, paying ₹1,100 and ₹900 respectively. His break-even prices at expiry are:

  1. ₹71,100 and ₹72,900
  2. ₹70,000 and ₹74,000
  3. ₹70,900 and ₹73,100
  4. ₹72,000 only
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. ₹70,000 and ₹74,000

A long straddle costs 1,100 + 900 = ₹2,000. It breaks even when the price moves ₹2,000 either side of the strike: ₹70,000 and ₹74,000.

Question 3

A refiner fears that the margin between crude oil prices and product prices will narrow. A position in crude oil futures against offsetting positions in petroleum product futures is known as a:

  1. Crush spread
  2. Calendar spread
  3. Crack spread
  4. Butterfly spread
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Crack spread

The crack spread captures the refining margin between crude and its products. The crush spread relates soybeans to meal and oil, while a calendar spread uses different expiries of the same commodity.

Question 4

A copper importer must buy 50,000 kg in two months. The optimal hedge ratio is 0.8 and one futures lot is 5,000 kg. The number of lots to buy is:

  1. 10
  2. 12
  3. 8
  4. 4
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. 8

Number of lots = hedge ratio × exposure ÷ lot size = 0.8 × 50,000 ÷ 5,000 = 8. Ten lots would be a one-to-one hedge ignoring the hedge ratio.

Question 5

A cotton mill buys cotton futures at ₹5,200 to hedge a future purchase. When it buys cotton in the spot market at ₹5,600, it closes the futures at ₹5,550. Its effective purchase cost is:

  1. ₹5,250
  2. ₹5,600
  3. ₹5,200
  4. ₹5,950
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. ₹5,250

Futures gain = 5,550 − 5,200 = ₹350. Effective cost = spot price − futures gain = 5,600 − 350 = ₹5,250. The difference from ₹5,200 reflects the change in basis.

Question 6

A farmer holding a short futures hedge will be better off than expected when the basis (spot minus futures):

  1. Weakens, i.e. becomes more negative
  2. Strengthens, i.e. becomes more positive
  3. Remains exactly unchanged
  4. Becomes equal to the cost of carry
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Strengthens, i.e. becomes more positive

A short hedger is long the physical and short futures, so he gains if spot rises relative to futures, which is a strengthening basis. A weakening basis hurts the short hedger.

Question 7

A stockist holding chana inventory writes call options on chana futures against it. This strategy, which earns premium but caps upside, is a:

  1. Covered call
  2. Protective put
  3. Long strangle
  4. Bear put spread
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Covered call

Writing calls against an existing long position is a covered call: the premium cushions small falls but gains above the strike are given up. A protective put would buy a put instead.

Question 8

A trader buys near-month cardamom futures and sells far-month cardamom futures, expecting the price gap to narrow. This is an example of a/an:

  1. Inter-commodity spread
  2. Inter-exchange arbitrage
  3. Outright directional position
  4. Calendar (intra-commodity) spread
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Calendar (intra-commodity) spread

Positions in the same commodity across different expiries form a calendar or intra-commodity spread, which profits from changes in the price difference rather than the overall price level.
