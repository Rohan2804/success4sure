What this chapter covers

This chapter explains how commodity indices are built and used. It covers spot-based versus futures-based indices, the choice of constituents and weights (for example production- or liquidity-based weighting), the roll mechanism for futures indices, and the sources of return in a futures index: spot return, roll yield and collateral return. It also introduces Indian commodity indices and index futures. Revise the purpose of an index, how contango and backwardation affect roll yield, and why rebalancing and weighting rules matter for an index that is used as a tradeable benchmark.

Commodity Indices questions

Question 1

In a commodity futures index, the return earned from replacing an expiring contract with a cheaper later contract when the market is in backwardation is called:

  1. Collateral yield
  2. Spot return
  3. Positive roll yield
  4. Convenience premium
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Positive roll yield

In backwardation, later contracts trade below the expiring one, so rolling means selling high and buying lower, producing a positive roll yield. Spot return comes from changes in spot prices and collateral yield from interest on margin money.

Question 2

A commodity futures index has consistently underperformed the spot price of its main constituent over several years. The most likely reason is that the futures curve has been in:

  1. Backwardation, causing positive roll yield
  2. Perfect convergence with zero basis
  3. Inverted spread with a high convenience yield
  4. Contango, causing negative roll yield
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Contango, causing negative roll yield

When futures are in contango, each roll buys a dearer contract, so index returns lag the spot price. Backwardation would have the opposite effect and add to returns.

Question 3

The three components of the total return of a fully collateralised commodity futures index are:

  1. Dividend yield, roll yield and currency return
  2. Spot return, storage cost and insurance cost
  3. Coupon return, roll yield and inflation return
  4. Spot return, roll yield and interest on collateral
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Spot return, roll yield and interest on collateral

A fully collateralised index earns from spot price changes, from rolling contracts along the curve, and from interest on the cash backing the futures. Commodities pay no dividends or coupons.

Question 4

Why might an index provider cap the weight of a single commodity in a diversified commodity index?

  1. To ensure that the index always rises when inflation rises
  2. Because exchanges prohibit indices containing more than one energy product
  3. To eliminate the need for rolling futures contracts
  4. To prevent one commodity's price moves from dominating index behaviour
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. To prevent one commodity's price moves from dominating index behaviour

Weight caps keep the index diversified so that a single commodity, such as crude oil, does not drive most of the index's risk. They do not remove rolling or guarantee any inflation link.

Question 5

An index that weights constituents in proportion to the value of their world production is said to use:

  1. Equal weighting
  2. Liquidity-based weighting
  3. Price-weighting by contract size
  4. Production-based weighting
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Production-based weighting

Production-value weighting reflects each commodity's economic importance. Liquidity-based weighting uses trading volumes instead, and equal weighting gives each constituent the same share.

Question 6

A spot-price commodity index differs from a futures-based index in that a spot index:

  1. Includes roll yield and interest on collateral in its reported returns
  2. Is the only type of index permitted to have index futures in India
  3. Is always calculated only once at the end of each month
  4. Tracks physical prices and is hard for an investor to replicate
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Tracks physical prices and is hard for an investor to replicate

A spot index tracks current physical prices, which an investor cannot easily hold because of storage and delivery issues. Futures-based indices are designed to be investable and include roll and collateral returns.

Question 7

An Indian exchange launches futures on a bullion index. Compared with buying separate gold and silver futures, a trader using the index future mainly gains:

  1. Physical delivery of gold and silver in fixed proportions
  2. Protection from all price falls in gold and silver
  3. Exemption from margin requirements
  4. A single position giving diversified exposure to the bullion basket
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. A single position giving diversified exposure to the bullion basket

Index futures offer exposure to a basket through one contract. They are cash-settled, so no physical delivery takes place, and they carry normal price risk and margin requirements.

Question 8

A commodity index is rebalanced annually to its target weights. After a year in which crude oil rose sharply and grains fell, rebalancing will typically:

  1. Add further to crude oil and reduce grains
  2. Leave weights unchanged, as rebalancing affects only roll dates
  3. Reduce the weight of crude oil and add to grains
  4. Remove grains from the index permanently
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Reduce the weight of crude oil and add to grains

Rebalancing restores target weights, so outperformers like crude, now overweight, are trimmed and underperformers, now underweight, are topped up. Constituents are not removed merely because of one year's fall.
