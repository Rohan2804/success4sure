Accounting and Taxation: NISM Series XVI practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on Accounting and Taxation, from the NISM Series XVI (Commodity Derivatives) syllabus.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter explains how commodity derivative transactions are accounted for and taxed. It covers the accounting treatment of margin deposits, daily mark-to-market settlements, open positions at year-end and hedging under applicable accounting guidance, as well as Commodity Transaction Tax, the treatment of profits as business income, the distinction between speculative and non-speculative transactions, GST implications on physical delivery and the treatment of exchange charges and brokerage. Focus on concepts rather than rates that change frequently, and on how hedging transactions are distinguished from trading for accounting purposes.

Accounting and Taxation questions

Question 1

Initial margin paid to a broker for commodity futures is generally shown in the trader's books as:

  1. An expense in the profit and loss account
  2. A current asset, as a deposit recoverable on closure of positions
  3. Income received in advance
  4. Part of the cost of the commodity purchased
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. A current asset, as a deposit recoverable on closure of positions

Initial margin is a deposit, not an expense, so it appears as a current asset until positions are closed. Only realised and MTM gains or losses affect profit.

Question 2

On which of the following commodity derivative transactions is Commodity Transaction Tax generally NOT payable?

  1. Derivatives on agricultural commodities notified as exempt
  2. Futures on gold traded on a recognised stock exchange in India
  3. Options on crude oil futures
  4. Futures on base metals such as copper
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Derivatives on agricultural commodities notified as exempt

CTT applies to non-agricultural commodity derivatives; derivatives on specified agricultural commodities are exempt. Gold, crude oil and base metal contracts attract CTT.

Question 3

Why is trading in exchange-traded commodity derivatives, on which CTT is paid, treated as non-speculative for income-tax purposes?

  1. Tax law excludes eligible exchange trades from speculative transactions
  2. Because all commodity contracts on Indian exchanges are settled by compulsory physical delivery
  3. Because commodity profits are fully exempt from tax
  4. Because CTT replaces income tax on such profits
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Tax law excludes eligible exchange trades from speculative transactions

The tax law carves out eligible commodity derivative trades on recognised exchanges from speculative transactions, so they are non-speculative business income. CTT is an additional transaction tax, not a substitute for income tax.

Question 4

A trader has a non-speculative business loss from commodity futures this year. Under the income-tax rules, this loss can generally be:

  1. Set off only against speculative income of the same year and of later assessment years
  2. Set off against salary income in the same year
  3. Set off against other business income and carried forward, subject to conditions
  4. Neither set off nor carried forward under any circumstances
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Set off against other business income and carried forward, subject to conditions

Non-speculative business losses can be set off against other heads except salary in the same year, and unabsorbed losses carried forward against business income, subject to timely filing of returns.

Question 5

A manufacturer takes delivery of copper through a commodity exchange contract. On the physical delivery, the manufacturer will typically pay:

  1. Commodity Transaction Tax only, without GST
  2. Goods and Services Tax on the value of the goods delivered
  3. Securities Transaction Tax on the delivery value
  4. No tax, as exchange deliveries are tax exempt
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Goods and Services Tax on the value of the goods delivered

Physical delivery is a supply of goods, so GST applies on its value. CTT relates to the derivative transaction itself, and STT applies only to securities like shares.

Question 6

An exporter uses commodity futures solely to hedge the price of goods it will sell. For accounting purposes, it should:

  1. Treat the futures as long-term investments carried at historical cost until they mature
  2. Document the hedge relationship and apply hedge accounting if eligible
  3. Ignore the futures until the export sale is completed
  4. Record the full contract value as a liability
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Document the hedge relationship and apply hedge accounting if eligible

Hedge accounting requires documentation of the hedged item, the hedging instrument and effectiveness. Without it, futures are measured at fair value through profit or loss. Contract value is not recorded as a liability.

Question 7

Brokerage and exchange transaction charges paid by a business trader in commodity derivatives are generally:

  1. Added to Commodity Transaction Tax payable
  2. Not deductible because they relate to derivatives
  3. Deductible only against capital gains
  4. Deductible as business expenses in computing business income
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Deductible as business expenses in computing business income

For a trader whose derivatives income is business income, brokerage and exchange charges are ordinary deductible expenses. There is no capital gains head involved in such business income.

Question 8

At the year-end, a company holds open commodity futures positions that have been marked to market daily. The cumulative MTM losses already paid are:

  1. Recognised in the profit and loss account for the year
  2. Shown as a recoverable deposit until expiry
  3. Ignored until the contracts are closed
  4. Added to the cost of inventory automatically
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Recognised in the profit and loss account for the year

MTM losses paid daily reflect the fall in fair value of open futures and are recognised in profit or loss, unless hedge accounting rules require deferral. They are not a recoverable deposit.
