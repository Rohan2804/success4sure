Accounting and Taxation: NISM Series XVI revision notes

This short chapter explains how commodity derivative transactions are recorded and taxed. Key points are the commodity transaction tax, the treatment of exchange-traded commodity derivatives as non-speculative business income, accounting entries for margin and mark-to-market, and the GST implications when contracts end in physical delivery. Rates change, so focus on principles.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Commodity transaction tax

CTT, introduced through the Finance Act 2013, is levied on the sale of non-agricultural commodity derivatives traded on recognised exchanges, such as gold, crude oil and copper futures. Specified agricultural commodities are exempt. Check current rates on the income tax department website.

2. Non-speculative business income

Section 43(5) treats eligible transactions in commodity derivatives on recognised exchanges, on which CTT is chargeable, as non-speculative. Agri commodity derivatives exempt from CTT were also brought within non-speculative treatment by later amendments. Income or loss is taxed as business income.

3. Set-off and carry forward

Non-speculative business losses from commodity derivatives can be set off against other business income (and, in the same year, against other heads except salary) and carried forward for eight years against business income. CTT paid is deductible when computing business income.

4. Accounting for margins

Initial and additional margins paid are recorded as current assets in a margin deposit account. Margins in the form of securities or bank guarantees are disclosed but not booked as cash.

5. Accounting for MTM

Daily MTM is passed through a mark-to-market account. At the reporting date, net debit balances on open positions are provided for as losses, while net credit balances are usually ignored under prudence. On final settlement, the balance is taken to profit and loss.

6. Accounting for options

Premium paid is carried as an asset and premium received as a liability until expiry, exercise or squaring off. On exercise of options on futures, the premium is adjusted against the resulting futures position as per applicable guidance.

7. GST on delivery

When a contract results in delivery of goods, GST applies on the supply at the rate applicable to that commodity. The seller must be GST-registered where required, and the exchange and clearing corporation handle invoicing through prescribed procedures. Cash-settled contracts involve no supply of goods.

8. Hedging accounting

Companies that hedge raw material or inventory may use hedge accounting under Ind AS 109 if documentation and effectiveness tests are met, so that gains and losses on the hedge are matched with the hedged item.

Common traps in the exam

  • CTT applies to non-agricultural commodity derivatives; specified agri commodities are exempt.
  • Exchange-traded commodity derivative income is non-speculative business income, not capital gains.
  • Net unrealised MTM losses are provided for; unrealised gains are generally not recognised under prudence.
  • GST arises only when there is physical delivery, not on cash-settled positions.

Quick recap

CTT is charged on sale of non-agri commodity derivatives; specified agri ones are exempt. Exchange commodity derivatives are non-speculative business income under Section 43(5); losses carry forward eight years and CTT is deductible. Margins are assets; MTM via separate account with provision for net losses. Premiums are assets or liabilities till settlement. GST applies on delivery.
