Trading Mechanism: NISM Series XVI revision notes

This chapter describes how commodity derivatives are actually traded: the membership structure, trading hours, order types, client codes, price limits and position limits. Questions are practical and procedural, asking which order type fits a need, what happens when a daily price limit is hit, or why agricultural and international commodities have different trading hours.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Membership

Trading members execute trades for themselves and clients; clearing members clear and settle trades. Members are registered with SEBI as stock brokers in the commodity derivatives segment. Clients deal through members who must complete KYC and provide rights and obligations and risk disclosure documents.

2. Trading hours

Agricultural commodities usually trade during Indian daytime hours, close to physical market activity. Metals and energy, which are linked to global prices, trade till late night to overlap with international markets such as London and New York. Exact timings are set by exchanges and change with daylight saving in the US.

3. Order-driven matching

Exchanges run anonymous order-driven electronic platforms matched on price-time priority. The best buy and best sell orders are shown in the order book. Each order carries a Unique Client Code so trades can be traced to the client.

4. Order types

Limit orders specify the maximum buy or minimum sell price; market orders execute at the best available price; stop-loss orders trigger when price reaches a set level; immediate-or-cancel orders execute what is possible immediately and cancel the rest. Good-till-cancelled or good-till-date orders stay until executed or expiry where offered.

5. Daily price limits

Each contract has a DPL, for example a few percent around the previous close. When a limit is hit, trading may pause for a cooling-off period and the limit may then be relaxed by a further amount. Internationally linked contracts may have dynamic limits that track global prices.

6. Position limits

Exchanges and SEBI set client-level and member-level position limits, often as a percentage of market-wide open interest or a fixed quantity, whichever is higher. Agri commodities have tighter limits to prevent hoarding-type speculation. Hedgers can seek higher limits with proof of exposure.

7. Contract launch and expiry

Exchanges publish a calendar of contracts with launch and expiry dates. Several months are available at once; the near month is usually most liquid. Before expiry, positions in delivery contracts enter the tender or staggered delivery period.

8. Surveillance

Exchanges monitor price and volume patterns, concentrated positions and synchronised trades. They can impose special margins, restrict new positions or suspend contracts in extreme cases, such as sharp unexplained price spikes in a thinly traded agri commodity.

Common traps in the exam

  • International-linked commodities trade late into the night; agri contracts mostly trade during the day.
  • An IOC order cancels any unexecuted portion immediately; it does not wait in the book.
  • Hitting the daily price limit triggers a cooling-off and possible relaxation, not automatic closure for the day in every case.
  • Hedgers can get higher position limits only with documented underlying exposure.

Quick recap

Members registered with SEBI trade and clear for clients with KYC and UCC. Agri contracts trade daytime; metals and energy till late night. Price-time priority matching; limit, market, stop-loss and IOC orders. Daily price limits with cooling-off and relaxation. Client and member position limits, tighter for agri, with hedger exemptions. Exchanges publish contract calendars and run surveillance.
