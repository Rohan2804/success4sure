Commodity Options: NISM Series XVI practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on Commodity Options, from the NISM Series XVI (Commodity Derivatives) syllabus.

What this chapter covers

This chapter covers options on commodity futures and options on goods as traded in India. Learners should know call and put pay-offs, intrinsic and time value, moneyness, break-even points, the factors affecting option premiums (underlying price, strike, time, volatility and interest rates) and the option Greeks. It also explains Indian contract features such as European-style exercise, devolvement of exercised options on futures into futures positions, and the exercise of in-the-money options at expiry. Practise pay-off calculations and the impact of volatility and time decay on long and short option positions.

Commodity Options questions

Question 1

A trader buys a put option on a commodity with a strike of ₹900 at a premium of ₹25. The lot is 100 units. If the underlying settles at ₹850 at expiry, his net profit is:

  1. ₹5,000
  2. ₹7,500
  3. ₹2,500
  4. ₹2,000
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. ₹2,500

Intrinsic value at expiry = 900 − 850 = ₹50. Net profit per unit = 50 − 25 = ₹25, so for 100 units profit = ₹2,500. ₹5,000 ignores the premium paid.

Question 2

A gold call option has a strike of ₹71,000 while the underlying futures trade at ₹72,500 and the premium is ₹1,900. The time value of the option is:

  1. ₹1,500
  2. ₹1,900
  3. ₹3,400
  4. ₹400
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. ₹400

Intrinsic value = 72,500 − 71,000 = ₹1,500. Time value = premium − intrinsic value = 1,900 − 1,500 = ₹400.

Question 3

Other things remaining the same, an increase in the expected volatility of the underlying commodity will:

  1. Increase call premiums but reduce put premiums
  2. Reduce the premiums of both calls and puts
  3. Increase the premiums of both calls and puts
  4. Have no effect, as volatility affects only futures prices
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Increase the premiums of both calls and puts

Higher volatility raises the chance of large favourable moves for both calls and puts, so both premiums rise. The holder's loss is limited to the premium, so the extra volatility is valuable to both.

Question 4

An option writer who has sold a call is most concerned about which Greek increasing sharply as expiry approaches for an at-the-money option?

  1. Gamma
  2. Rho
  3. Vega of a far-month option
  4. Lambda of the futures
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Gamma

Gamma, the rate of change of delta, is highest for at-the-money options near expiry, so the writer's exposure can swing rapidly. Rho measures interest-rate sensitivity and is usually minor.

Question 5

A put option on crude oil futures has a strike of ₹6,500 and the futures are trading at ₹6,700. The put is:

  1. In the money
  2. Out of the money
  3. At the money
  4. Deep in the money
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Out of the money

A put has intrinsic value only when the underlying is below the strike. With futures at ₹6,700 above the ₹6,500 strike, the put is out of the money.

Question 6

In options on commodity futures in India, a long call that is exercised results in the holder getting:

  1. A long futures position at the strike price
  2. Delivery of the physical commodity at the strike price immediately
  3. A short futures position at the prevailing market price
  4. Cash equal to the premium paid, returned to him by the exchange
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. A long futures position at the strike price

Exercised options on futures devolve into futures positions: the call buyer gets a long futures position at the strike price and the writer gets the matching short position.

Question 7

Time decay (theta) generally works in favour of:

  1. Option buyers
  2. Option writers
  3. Holders of long futures positions
  4. Both option buyers and writers equally
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Option writers

As time passes, the time value of options erodes. Writers have received the premium and gain as time value falls, while buyers lose. Futures positions have no time value.

Question 8

Using put-call parity for European options on futures, if the call and put on the same strike have equal premiums, the futures price is most likely:

  1. Well above the strike price
  2. Equal to the strike price
  3. Well below the strike price
  4. Equal to zero, as parity has broken down
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Equal to the strike price

Put-call parity for options on futures gives C − P = (F − K) discounted. If C equals P, then F equals K, so the futures price is at the strike.
