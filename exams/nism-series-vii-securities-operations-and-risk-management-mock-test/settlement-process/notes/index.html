Settlement Process: NISM Series VII revision notes

Settlement is where trades are finally completed through the exchange of funds and securities. This chapter covers the T+1 cycle and the optional T+0 framework, pay-in and pay-out timings, what happens when a seller fails to deliver, and how holidays and corporate actions affect settlement. Expect date calculations and 'who bears the cost' questions.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Rolling settlement

Under rolling settlement, each trading day's trades settle separately after a fixed number of days. India moved fully to T+1 for equities in January 2023. A trade on Wednesday settles on Thursday; a trade on Friday settles on the next Monday if it is a working day.

2. Optional T+0

SEBI introduced an optional same-day settlement cycle for a limited list of stocks, running alongside T+1. Investors choose the cycle when placing orders, and the two cycles have separate price discovery. Most trading still happens in T+1.

3. Pay-in and pay-out

On the settlement day, sellers' securities and buyers' funds move to the clearing corporation in the pay-in; the clearing corporation then releases securities to buyers and funds to sellers in the pay-out, usually within a few hours. Brokers must then pass funds and securities to clients promptly.

4. Direct pay-out

Securities bought by a client are credited directly from the clearing corporation to the client's demat account, not to the broker's pool account. This removes the risk of brokers misusing purchased shares. Unpaid securities follow a separate prescribed process.

5. Short delivery and auction

If a seller delivers fewer shares than sold, the clearing corporation conducts an auction to buy the shortfall for the buyer. The defaulting member pays the difference between the auction price and the original sale price, plus any penalty, so the buyer receives shares at the agreed cost.

6. Close-out

If the auction fails because no one offers shares, the trade is closed out in cash. The close-out price is typically the higher of the highest price from trade date to auction day, or a fixed premium (such as 20%) over the closing price on the auction day. The defaulter bears this cost.

7. Bad delivery

Bad delivery used to be common with physical certificates due to signature mismatches or damaged documents. With demat settlement, bad delivery is virtually eliminated, though deliveries can still fail due to frozen accounts or wrong instructions.

8. Settlement holidays

Settlement holidays are days when banks or clearing are closed, even if exchanges might be open. Pay-in and pay-out move to the next working day. When calculating T+1 settlement dates, skip both weekends and settlement holidays.

9. Corporate actions and open positions

When a stock goes ex-dividend or ex-bonus, prices are adjusted. A buyer who buys on or after the ex-date does not get the benefit. With T+1, the ex-date and record date are usually the same day, so buying the day before is necessary to receive the benefit.

Common traps in the exam

  • Counting a weekend as T+1. Friday trades settle on Monday, not Saturday.
  • Believing the buyer bears auction costs on a short delivery. The defaulting seller pays the difference and penalties.
  • Assuming T+0 has replaced T+1. T+0 is optional for selected stocks; T+1 remains the main cycle.
  • Thinking ex-date and record date are always several days apart. Under T+1 they usually coincide.

Quick recap

T+1 is the default; T+0 is optional for listed stocks. Pay-in moves sellers' shares and buyers' funds to the clearing corporation; pay-out delivers to buyers' demat accounts directly. Short deliveries are auctioned; failed auctions are closed out at a premium. Defaulters bear costs. Skip weekends and settlement holidays when counting days.
