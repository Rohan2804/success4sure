Retail Banking & Wealth Management: JAIIB revision notes

Retail Banking and Wealth Management covers the products and processes that serve individual customers: deposits, retail loans, cards, digital banking, marketing and relationship management, and wealth management including third-party products. Questions mix product rules, pricing, regulatory caps and selling ethics. Several limits come from RBI circulars that are updated, so verify current values.

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Retail banking scope

Retail banking serves individuals and small businesses with deposits, loans, cards, remittances and investment products through branches and digital channels. It offers stable low-cost deposits, diversified credit risk and cross-selling opportunities, but needs strong technology and processes to handle high volumes.

2. Retail loan products

Home loans, vehicle loans, personal loans, education loans, gold loans and loans against property are the main products. Each has its own eligibility, margin, tenure and security. Education loans follow the Indian Banks' Association model scheme with moratorium during the course.

3. Loan pricing

Floating-rate retail loans to individuals and MSMEs must be linked to an external benchmark such as the RBI repo rate or a government security yield (EBLR), with a spread fixed for the loan unless credit risk changes. Rates must be reset at least once every three months. This improves transmission of policy rate changes.

4. Home loan LTV

RBI caps loan-to-value ratios for housing loans by loan size: up to 90% for smaller loans, 80% for middle bands and 75% for larger loans (as of 2026 — check RBI). Stamp duty, registration and documentation charges are normally excluded from the property cost for LTV, though they may be included for low-value homes costing up to ₹10 lakh.

5. Repayment capacity

Banks assess fixed obligation to income ratio (FOIR): total EMIs as a share of monthly income, often capped at 50% to 60% depending on income. If a borrower earns ₹1 lakh a month and has existing EMIs of ₹15,000, a 50% FOIR leaves room for a new EMI of ₹35,000.

6. Credit and debit cards

Credit cards offer revolving credit with interest-free periods; debit cards draw from the account. RBI's directions on cards require consent before issuing or upgrading cards, transparent charges, and closure requests to be honoured within set days. Card tokenisation protects card details online.

7. Digital channels

Internet banking, mobile banking, UPI, ATMs and cash recyclers, and video KYC have moved most retail transactions out of branches. Banks must manage cyber risk, customer authentication and fraud monitoring, and educate customers on not sharing OTPs.

8. Marketing and CRM

Retail banks segment customers by income, age and behaviour, use data analytics to offer the right product, and track customer lifetime value. Customer relationship management systems help in cross-selling and retention. Mis-selling damages trust and attracts regulatory action.

9. Wealth management

Wealth management offers financial planning, investment advice, portfolio management, insurance and estate planning to affluent customers. Banks distribute mutual funds (as AMFI-registered distributors), insurance (as corporate agents) and other third-party products, and must follow suitability and disclosure norms.

10. Financial planning basics

Planning involves understanding goals, risk profile and time horizon, allocating assets, and reviewing regularly. Emergency fund, adequate insurance and debt management come before investing. Tax planning uses provisions such as 80C and NPS within the chosen regime.

11. Selling third-party products ethically

RBI requires banks to keep investment and insurance selling separate from compulsion: no tying products to loans, proper risk profiling, disclosure of commissions and avoiding mis-selling to senior citizens or unsophisticated customers.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • FOIR = (existing EMIs + proposed EMI) / gross or net monthly income.
  • LTV = loan amount / property value x 100.

Common traps in the exam

  • EBLR loans must reset at least every three months; the spread cannot be changed arbitrarily.
  • LTV caps fall as loan size rises, not the other way round.
  • Banks must not force customers to buy insurance or investments as a condition for a loan.
  • Card upgrades or new cards need explicit customer consent.

Quick recap

Retail banking serves individuals with deposits, loans, cards and digital services. Floating retail loans are priced over an external benchmark with quarterly resets. Home loan LTV caps fall with size. FOIR measures repayment capacity. Cards need consent and transparent charges. Digital channels need fraud controls. Wealth management covers planning, MFs, insurance and suitability, without tying or mis-selling.
