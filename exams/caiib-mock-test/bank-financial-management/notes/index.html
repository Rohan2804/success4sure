Bank Financial Management: CAIIB revision notes

Bank Financial Management, a compulsory CAIIB paper, covers international banking and forex, risk management, treasury management and balance sheet management. Numerical questions are frequent: exchange rate arithmetic, forward premiums, gap analysis and capital ratios. Conceptual questions test Basel III components and liquidity standards. RBI's capital and liquidity numbers are stable but verify any recent changes.

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Exchange rate quotations

A direct quote states the price of one unit of foreign currency in home currency (USD/INR 83.00). Banks quote two-way rates: buying (bid) and selling (ask). For merchant transactions, TT rates apply when no delay in funds, and bill rates when there is a delay. Rupee has been on a market-determined exchange rate since 1993.

2. Forward premium

Forward rates reflect interest rate differentials. Annualised forward premium = (forward - spot) / spot x 12 / months x 100. With spot 83.00 and three-month forward 83.50: 0.50/83 x 4 x 100 = about 2.41% a year. A currency at a premium is costlier in the forward market.

3. Trade finance

Letters of credit under UCP 600 make the issuing bank liable to pay against compliant documents. Pre-shipment finance (packing credit) funds production; post-shipment finance funds the period until export proceeds arrive. ECGC provides credit insurance to exporters and banks. Nostro accounts are a bank's accounts with foreign banks in foreign currency.

4. FEMA framework

The Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 classifies transactions as current account (largely free, with some restrictions) or capital account (permitted as specified). Authorised dealer banks handle forex transactions under RBI's directions, including the Liberalised Remittance Scheme for individuals.

5. Risk types

Credit risk is the risk of borrower default; market risk arises from changes in interest rates, exchange rates and equity prices; operational risk from failed processes, people, systems or external events; liquidity risk from inability to meet obligations. Banks manage each with limits, models and capital.

6. Basel III capital

Under RBI's Basel III rules, minimum Common Equity Tier 1 is 5.5% of risk-weighted assets, Tier 1 7% and total capital 9%. A capital conservation buffer of 2.5% in CET1 takes CET1 to 8% and total capital to 11.5%. D-SIBs hold an additional surcharge, and a countercyclical buffer can be activated.

7. Leverage and liquidity ratios

The leverage ratio (Tier 1 capital / total exposure) must be at least 4% for D-SIBs and 3.5% for other banks under RBI norms. The Liquidity Coverage Ratio requires high-quality liquid assets at least equal to net cash outflows over 30 days of stress (100%). The Net Stable Funding Ratio requires available stable funding at least equal to required stable funding.

8. ALM and gap analysis

Asset-liability management matches maturities and rate sensitivities. Liquidity gap = assets minus liabilities in each time bucket. Interest rate sensitivity gap = rate-sensitive assets minus rate-sensitive liabilities. A positive gap gains when rates rise; a negative gap gains when rates fall. Change in NII is roughly gap x change in rate.

9. Duration

Duration measures the price sensitivity of a bond or portfolio to interest rate changes. Modified duration = Macaulay duration / (1 + yield). Duration gap = duration of assets - (liabilities/assets) x duration of liabilities. A positive duration gap means equity value falls when rates rise.

10. Value at risk

VaR estimates the maximum loss over a given period at a given confidence level under normal conditions. A one-day 99% VaR of ₹5 crore means losses should exceed ₹5 crore on only about 1 in 100 days. VaR does not show how large losses can be beyond that threshold.

11. Treasury management

Bank treasuries manage liquidity (CRR, SLR, call money, repos), investments in government securities, forex dealing and derivatives. Front office deals, mid office measures risk and limits, back office settles and accounts. Separation of these functions prevents fraud.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Annualised forward premium % = (F - S)/S x 12/n x 100.
  • Rate sensitivity gap = RSA - RSL; change in NII = gap x change in interest rate.
  • Duration gap = D_A - (L/A) x D_L.
  • Leverage ratio = Tier 1 capital / total exposure.

Common traps in the exam

  • CET1 minimum is 5.5%, but with the 2.5% conservation buffer it becomes 8%.
  • A negative interest rate gap benefits from falling rates, not rising.
  • LCR covers a 30-day stress period; NSFR is about one-year funding stability.
  • VaR does not measure the size of losses in the tail beyond the confidence level.
  • In a direct quote the foreign currency is the base; buying rate is lower than selling rate for the bank.

Quick recap

Forex: direct quotes, bid and ask, TT and bill rates; forward premium = (F-S)/S x 12/n. Trade finance via LCs (UCP 600), packing credit, ECGC. FEMA current vs capital account. Basel III: CET1 5.5% + 2.5% CCB, total 11.5%; leverage ratio 4% D-SIBs, 3.5% others; LCR and NSFR 100%. ALM: gap = RSA - RSL; duration gap. VaR and treasury front, mid, back office.
