Strategies Using Interest Rate Derivatives: NISM Series IV practice questions

Last updated 16 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter applies interest rate futures, swaps and options to real decisions: hedging bond portfolios, locking future borrowing or investment rates, changing portfolio duration, trading yield-curve views, and arbitraging mispriced futures. Learn the direction of each hedge (sell futures to protect against rising yields, buy to protect against falling yields), how to compute hedge ratios using duration or PVBP, and how to read profit and loss on a closed position. Curve trades (steepening and flattening) and calendar spreads are frequent scenario questions.

Strategies Using Interest Rate Derivatives questions

Question 1

You believe the gap between 10-year and 2-year G-sec yields will narrow because 10-year yields will drop more. Which futures position fits this view?

  1. Buy long-tenor bond futures and sell short-tenor futures
  2. Sell long-tenor bond futures and buy short-tenor futures
  3. Sell both long-tenor and short-tenor futures equally
  4. Buy only short-tenor futures and hold to expiry
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Buy long-tenor bond futures and sell short-tenor futures

If long yields fall relative to short yields, long-tenor futures will outperform. Buying them and selling short-tenor futures profits from the flattening while reducing exposure to a parallel shift.

Question 2

A ₹25 crore bond portfolio has a modified duration of 4.8. A bond futures contract has a value of ₹2 lakh and a modified duration of 7.5. How many contracts are needed to fully hedge?

  1. 640
  2. 1,250
  3. 800
  4. 960
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. 800

Contracts = (portfolio value × portfolio MD) / (contract value × futures MD) = (25,00,00,000 × 4.8) / (2,00,000 × 7.5) = 1,20,00,00,000 / 15,00,000 = 800 contracts, to be sold.

Question 3

A fund manager wants to reduce the duration of a bond portfolio quickly without selling bonds. The manager should:

  1. Buy interest rate futures
  2. Buy call options on bond futures
  3. Enter an IRS to receive fixed
  4. Sell interest rate futures
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Sell interest rate futures

Short futures have negative duration, so adding them lowers the portfolio's overall duration. Buying futures or receiving fixed in a swap would increase duration instead.

Question 4

Bond futures are trading below their theoretical fair value. The reverse cash-and-carry arbitrage is to:

  1. Sell the futures, buy the bond in the cash market and fund it via repo
  2. Buy the futures, short-sell the bond and invest the proceeds
  3. Buy both the futures and the bond at the same time
  4. Sell the futures and hold cash until expiry
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Buy the futures, short-sell the bond and invest the proceeds

When futures are cheap, the arbitrageur buys futures and sells the bond in the cash market, investing the proceeds (reverse repo). At expiry the bond is received under the futures and returned, locking the mispricing.

Question 5

A company plans to raise money by issuing bonds in two months and fears that yields will rise before then. Using interest rate futures, it should:

  1. Buy futures now and sell them at issue
  2. Wait and buy futures on the issue date
  3. Sell futures now and buy them back at issue
  4. Buy call options on bond futures now
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Sell futures now and buy them back at issue

If yields rise, the cost of borrowing rises but futures prices fall, so a short futures position gains and offsets the higher cost. Buying futures or calls would gain only if yields fell.

Question 6

A bank sold bond futures to hedge its G-sec holdings. Yields then rise sharply. What is the most likely overall outcome?

  1. Both the bond holdings and the short futures position make losses
  2. Bonds gain while the short futures lose value
  3. Short futures lose, doubling the bond losses
  4. Loss on bonds is largely offset by a gain on short futures
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Loss on bonds is largely offset by a gain on short futures

Rising yields push bond prices down, causing a loss on the holdings. The same move lowers futures prices, so the short futures position gains. A well-sized hedge offsets most of the loss, leaving basis risk.

Question 7

Simultaneously buying the near-month contract and selling the far-month contract of the same interest rate futures is called a:

  1. Butterfly spread
  2. Calendar spread
  3. Basis trade
  4. Straddle
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Calendar spread

A calendar spread uses two expiries of the same contract. Its margin is usually lower than for outright positions because most of the price risk offsets. A basis trade combines a cash bond with futures.

Question 8

A trader sold 50 bond futures contracts (face value ₹2 lakh each, quoted per ₹100) at 99.20 and bought them back at 98.40. Ignoring costs, the result is:

  1. Profit of ₹80,000
  2. Profit of ₹8,000
  3. Loss of ₹80,000
  4. Profit of ₹8,00,000
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Profit of ₹80,000

Price fell by 0.80 per ₹100. Profit = 50 × 2,00,000 × 0.80 / 100 = ₹80,000. Being short, the trader gains when the price falls; ₹8,00,000 forgets to divide by 100.
