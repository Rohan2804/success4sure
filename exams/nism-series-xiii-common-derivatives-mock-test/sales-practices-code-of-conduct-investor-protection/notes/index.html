Sales Practices, Code of Conduct & Investor Protection: NISM Series XIII revision notes

Derivatives can cause losses larger than an investor expects, so NISM XIII ends with how intermediaries should sell and service them. The chapter covers KYC, suitability, risk disclosure, the code of conduct for brokers and staff, investor grievance mechanisms and investor protection funds. Questions are scenario-based: pick the most ethical or compliant action.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Know your client

Before activating derivatives trading, the broker must complete KYC and collect financial details. SEBI requires proof of financial capacity, such as bank statements, ITR or net worth certificate, for the derivatives segment. A student with no income asking to trade options should prompt closer review.

2. Suitability

Staff should match products to the client's knowledge, objectives and risk capacity. Recommending option selling to a retiree who needs stable income is unsuitable even if the client is willing. Documenting the assessment protects both client and firm.

3. Risk disclosure

Clients must receive the risk disclosure document explaining leverage, possibility of losing more than the margin, liquidity risk and the effect of margin calls. SEBI also requires brokers to display a risk disclosure about F&O losses on login, based on SEBI's study showing that a large majority of individual F&O traders made net losses.

4. No assured returns

Sales staff must never promise profits, guaranteed income or 'sure-shot' tips. Advertising past profits of other clients or showing back-tested results as if achieved is misleading. Stock tips through messaging groups by unregistered persons are a regulatory concern.

5. Code of conduct for brokers

Brokers must act with integrity, due skill and care, avoid conflicts, not churn client accounts for brokerage, execute orders promptly at the best available price, maintain confidentiality, and issue contract notes and statements on time. Employees should follow the firm's personal trading policy.

6. Handling client funds

Client funds must be kept in client accounts and used only for that client's obligations. Brokers should not lend client securities or use them for their own margin without authorisation through the prescribed mechanism. Running accounts must be settled periodically.

7. Grievance redressal

Clients should first complain to the broker. Unresolved issues can go to the exchange investor services cell, to SEBI's SCORES portal, and then to the online dispute resolution platform (conciliation and arbitration) set up through MIIs. Each step has defined timelines.

8. Investor protection funds

Exchanges and depositories maintain investor protection funds to compensate investors when a broker is declared a defaulter, within limits per investor. The funds also support investor awareness programmes. Compensation covers genuine claims against defaulting members, not trading losses.

Common traps in the exam

  • Investor protection funds compensate for a broker's default, not for market losses on trades.
  • A client's consent does not make an unsuitable recommendation acceptable.
  • Financial proof is needed to enable derivatives trading, not merely a PAN and address.
  • Escalation starts with the broker; SCORES comes after the intermediary has had a chance to resolve the complaint.

Quick recap

Complete KYC and financial proof before enabling F&O. Recommend only suitable products and document it. Give the risk disclosure document and never promise returns. Follow the broker code: integrity, no churning, best execution, timely contract notes, segregated client funds. Complaints: broker, exchange, SCORES, ODR. IPFs compensate for broker default, not trading losses.
