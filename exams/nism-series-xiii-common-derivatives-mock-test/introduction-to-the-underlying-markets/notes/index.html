Introduction to the Underlying Markets: NISM Series XIII revision notes

Before trading derivatives you must understand the underlying markets: equity indices and stocks, the foreign exchange market and the government bond market. This chapter is wide, covering index construction, exchange rate quotes and bond price-yield relationships. Questions often include small calculations on cross rates, index levels or bond yields, and definitions such as impact cost and free float.

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Equity indices

An index tracks a basket of stocks. Most Indian indices, including Nifty 50 and Sensex, are free-float market-capitalisation weighted: only shares readily available for trading count. If a company has ₹2 lakh crore market cap but promoters hold 60%, its free-float market cap is ₹80,000 crore, which determines its weight.

2. Index qualities

A good index is diversified, liquid and representative. Impact cost measures liquidity: the percentage price movement caused by executing a given order size compared with the ideal price. Lower impact cost means more liquid. Index levels are adjusted for corporate actions so that a split or bonus does not change the index value.

3. Foreign exchange market basics

The forex market is OTC, dominated by banks. In a direct quote like USD/INR 84.00, the base currency (USD) is quoted in terms of the price currency (INR). Bid is the rate at which the dealer buys the base currency, ask is the rate at which it sells. Spread = ask minus bid.

4. Spot, forward and cross rates

Spot settles two business days after the trade. Forward rates differ from spot because of interest rate differentials. Cross rates derive a third rate: if USD/INR is 84 and EUR/USD is 1.10, then EUR/INR is about 84 x 1.10 = 92.40. Questions often test this multiplication.

5. Reference rate

Exchange-traded USD/INR futures and options are cash settled in rupees using the reference rate published by FBIL (Financial Benchmarks India Ltd) on the expiry date. Other currency pairs use rates specified by exchanges. Know that the settlement is in INR, not USD.

6. Government securities

G-secs are issued by RBI on behalf of the Government through auctions. T-bills are short-term discount instruments (91, 182, 364 days); dated securities carry a fixed or floating coupon. Trading happens on NDS-OM and OTC, and settlement is through CCIL. Stock exchanges also offer retail access.

7. Price and yield

Bond prices and yields move inversely. A bond with ₹100 face value and 7% coupon trading at ₹98 has a yield to maturity above 7%. When market yields rise, prices of existing bonds fall, and longer-duration bonds fall more. Duration measures this sensitivity.

8. Benchmark interest rates

Short-term benchmarks include MIBOR and the overnight rates in the call and TREPS markets, administered or published by FBIL and CCIL. Interest rate futures in India are mainly on G-sec bonds and T-bills. These rates also drive pricing of currency forwards through interest rate differentials.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Cross rate: EUR/INR = EUR/USD x USD/INR.
  • Free-float market cap = total market cap x (1 - promoter and locked-in holding %).
  • Current yield = annual coupon / market price.

Common traps in the exam

  • Index weights use free-float market cap, not full market cap.
  • Bid is where the dealer buys the base currency; the customer selling USD gets the bid.
  • USD/INR exchange-traded derivatives settle in rupees using the FBIL reference rate, without delivery of dollars.
  • If yields rise, bond prices fall; a question showing both rising is wrong.

Quick recap

Indices like Nifty and Sensex are free-float market-cap weighted; impact cost measures liquidity. Forex: direct quotes, bid below ask, spot T+2, cross rates by multiplication, FBIL reference rate for settlement. G-secs via RBI auctions, traded on NDS-OM, settled by CCIL. Bond prices and yields move inversely; duration measures sensitivity. MIBOR and TREPS rates are short-term benchmarks.
