Option Trading Strategies: NISM Series XIII revision notes

This chapter builds strategies from single options and futures, and NISM XIII tests them through payoff questions: what is the maximum loss, where is the breakeven, which view does a strategy express. Work through each with simple numbers. Most strategies fall into three groups: directional (spreads), volatility (straddles, strangles) and protective (covered call, protective put, collar).

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Protective put

Own the stock and buy a put. It works like insurance: the put caps the downside at strike minus premium paid. Example: hold shares at ₹500, buy a 480 put for ₹10. Maximum loss is 500 - 480 + 10 = ₹30 per share, while upside stays open less the premium.

2. Covered call

Own the stock and sell a call. The premium earns extra income, but gains above the strike are given up. Suitable when the view is neutral to mildly bullish. Maximum profit = strike - purchase price + premium; the downside is that of the stock less the premium received.

3. Bull call spread

Buy a lower-strike call and sell a higher-strike call, same expiry. Cost is lower than a naked call, but profit is capped. With a 24,000 call bought at 300 and 24,300 call sold at 150: net debit 150, maximum profit 300 - 150 = 150 points, breakeven 24,150.

4. Bear put spread

Buy a higher-strike put and sell a lower-strike put. Used for a moderately bearish view. Maximum profit is the difference in strikes minus net debit; maximum loss is the net debit. A bear call spread (sell lower call, buy higher call) is the credit version of the same view.

5. Long straddle

Buy a call and a put at the same strike and expiry. It profits from a large move in either direction, for example before a major result or policy announcement. Maximum loss is the total premium if the price ends at the strike. Breakevens are strike plus and minus total premium.

6. Long strangle

Buy an OTM call and an OTM put. Cheaper than a straddle but needs a bigger move. A short straddle or short strangle is the reverse view: the seller expects low volatility, collects premium and faces large losses if the market moves sharply.

7. Butterfly spread

A long call butterfly buys one lower strike, sells two middle strikes and buys one higher strike. It profits if the price stays near the middle strike at expiry, with limited risk and limited reward. It is a low-cost, low-volatility view.

8. Collar

Own the stock, buy a protective put and sell a call to fund it. Both downside and upside are capped. If both premiums are equal, it is a zero-cost collar. Useful for a promoter or investor who wants to protect gains without selling.

9. Synthetic positions

Long call plus short put at the same strike behaves like long futures. Long stock plus long put behaves like a long call. These equivalences, based on put-call parity, appear as matching questions.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Bull call spread: max profit = (higher strike - lower strike) - net debit; breakeven = lower strike + net debit.
  • Long straddle breakevens = strike +/- (call premium + put premium).
  • Long strangle breakevens = call strike + total premium and put strike - total premium.

Common traps in the exam

  • A long straddle needs volatility, not direction; a short straddle loses on big moves either way.
  • Covered call caps upside but does not protect much downside; the protective put protects downside.
  • In a bull call spread the maximum loss is the net premium paid, not the full premium of the bought call.
  • Long call plus short put is synthetic long futures, not a straddle.

Quick recap

Protective put = stock plus put (insurance). Covered call = stock plus short call (income, capped upside). Bull call and bear put spreads are cheaper directional bets with capped profit. Long straddle and strangle profit from big moves; short versions bet on calm markets with large risk. Butterfly bets on staying near the middle strike. Collar caps both sides.
