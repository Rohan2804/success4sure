What this chapter covers

This chapter introduces derivatives across asset classes covered in the common derivatives exam: equity, currency, interest rate and commodity. It explains forwards, futures, options and swaps, exchange-traded versus OTC contracts, the participants (hedgers, speculators, arbitrageurs) and the economic functions of derivatives. It also traces how derivatives markets developed in India. When revising, focus on definitions, the differences between contract types, and the benefits and risks of derivatives for different users.

Basics of Derivatives questions

Question 1

Which of the following derivatives is usually traded over the counter rather than on an exchange?

  1. Nifty index futures traded on the NSE
  2. USD/INR exchange-traded options
  3. A currency swap between two companies
  4. Gold futures on a commodity exchange
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. A currency swap between two companies

Swaps are customised bilateral contracts, generally traded OTC. Index futures, exchange-traded currency options and commodity futures are standardised exchange products.

Question 2

A rice exporter sells USD forward to protect against rupee appreciation. Her role is that of a:

  1. Hedger
  2. Speculator
  3. Arbitrageur
  4. Market maker
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Hedger

She has a genuine foreign currency exposure and uses a forward to offset it, which is hedging. Speculators take positions without an underlying exposure.

Question 3

Which feature makes derivatives useful for transferring risk?

  1. They remove risk from the economy entirely
  2. Risk passes to those willing to bear it
  3. They guarantee profits to both parties
  4. They can be used only by banks
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Risk passes to those willing to bear it

Derivatives move price risk from hedgers to speculators or other hedgers with opposite exposures. Total risk in the economy is not eliminated.

Question 4

An option differs from a futures contract because the option buyer:

  1. Has an obligation to buy or sell at expiry
  2. Pays no money at the start
  3. Faces unlimited losses
  4. Has a right but not an obligation
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Has a right but not an obligation

An option buyer pays a premium for the right to exercise, with no obligation. Futures bind both parties, and option buyers' losses are limited to the premium.

Question 5

Commodity derivatives on Indian exchanges are now regulated by:

  1. The Forward Markets Commission
  2. SEBI
  3. The RBI
  4. The Ministry of Consumer Affairs
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. SEBI

The Forward Markets Commission merged into SEBI in 2015, and SEBI now regulates commodity derivatives exchanges and brokers along with securities markets.

Question 6

Arbitrage in derivatives markets helps by:

  1. Increasing price differences between markets
  2. Removing the need for any regulation
  3. Guaranteeing that prices always rise
  4. Keeping related prices in line
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Keeping related prices in line

Arbitrageurs exploit price gaps between related markets; their trades push prices back into alignment, improving market efficiency.

Question 7

Which risk is a key concern in OTC derivatives but much lower in exchange-traded ones?

  1. Price risk on the underlying
  2. Changes in interest rates
  3. Counterparty default risk
  4. Volatility of the underlying
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Counterparty default risk

Exchange trades are guaranteed by a clearing corporation through novation and margins. OTC deals rely on the counterparty's creditworthiness, though central clearing has reduced this for some products.

Question 8

The underlying of an interest rate futures contract in India is typically:

  1. A government security or interest rate
  2. A basket of listed equity shares in the Nifty
  3. A tonne of steel
  4. A foreign currency pair
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. A government security or interest rate

Interest rate futures are based on government bonds or reference rates, letting users hedge interest rate risk. Equity, commodity and currency futures have other underlyings.
