Basics of Derivatives: NISM Series XIII revision notes

NISM Series XIII (Common Derivatives) covers equity, currency and interest rate derivatives together. This first chapter defines a derivative, compares the basic contract types and explains why markets need them. Questions are mostly conceptual: identify the instrument from a description, classify a participant by motive, or contrast exchange-traded and OTC contracts.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Definition

A derivative is a contract whose value depends on an underlying asset, index or rate: shares, Nifty, USD/INR, government bond prices, interest rates or commodities. Under SCRA, derivatives are securities. The contract itself usually requires little or no upfront payment beyond margin or premium, which creates leverage.

2. Forwards

A forward is a customised bilateral agreement to buy or sell an asset at a fixed price on a future date. An importer agreeing with a bank today to buy USD 50,000 in three months at ₹84.20 has a forward. Terms are flexible, but there is counterparty risk and no easy exit before maturity.

3. Futures

Futures are standardised forwards traded on an exchange, with fixed lot size, expiry and settlement, and a clearing corporation guaranteeing performance. Daily mark-to-market settles gains and losses, so credit risk is limited. Positions can be closed any time before expiry by an opposite trade.

4. Options

An option gives the buyer the right, not the obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) the underlying at a strike price on or before expiry, in exchange for a premium. The seller (writer) has the obligation if exercised. Buyers have limited loss; sellers have limited gain and potentially large losses.

5. Swaps

A swap exchanges one set of cash flows for another, for example fixed interest for floating (interest rate swap) or principal and interest in one currency for another (currency swap). Swaps are mostly OTC between banks and corporates in India, though they are covered in the syllabus as a basic type.

6. Exchange-traded versus OTC

Exchange-traded contracts are standardised, transparent, margined and centrally cleared. OTC contracts are customised and negotiated privately, with higher counterparty and liquidity risk, though many OTC derivatives are now reported to trade repositories and some are centrally cleared through CCIL.

7. Market participants

Hedgers use derivatives to reduce existing risk, such as an exporter selling USD futures. Speculators take positions to profit from expected price moves, accepting risk. Arbitrageurs lock in riskless profit from price differences between markets, such as buying shares in cash and selling futures when futures are overpriced. Each role adds liquidity.

8. Economic functions

Derivatives help price discovery, transfer risk to those willing to bear it, increase market liquidity and lower transaction costs. Misused, leverage can amplify losses, which is why margins, position limits and suitability checks exist. Index futures began in India in June 2000, followed by index options, stock options and stock futures.

Common traps in the exam

  • Forwards carry counterparty risk; exchange-traded futures replace it with the clearing corporation's guarantee.
  • The option buyer pays premium and has a right; the seller receives premium and has an obligation.
  • An arbitrageur seeks riskless profit; a speculator accepts risk for expected profit.
  • Swaps are derivatives too, even though most trade OTC.

Quick recap

A derivative derives value from an underlying. Forwards are customised OTC contracts with counterparty risk; futures are standardised, exchange-traded and marked to market daily; options give buyers a right for a premium; swaps exchange cash flows. Hedgers reduce risk, speculators take it, arbitrageurs exploit mispricing. Benefits: price discovery, risk transfer, liquidity.
