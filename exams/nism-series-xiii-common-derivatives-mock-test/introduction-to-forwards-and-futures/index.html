What this chapter covers

This chapter covers forward and futures contracts across equity, currency, interest rate and commodity markets. It explains contract features, pricing using cost of carry and interest rate parity, convenience yield for commodities, basis, contango and backwardation, mark-to-market and final settlement. When revising, practise fair-value and forward premium calculations, understand how carrying costs and income affect pricing in each asset class, and know how settlement differs across contracts.

Introduction to Forwards and Futures questions

Question 1

A USD/INR futures contract has a lot size of USD 1,000. A trader buys one lot at 84.50 and sells it at 84.80. Ignoring costs, his profit is:

  1. ₹30
  2. ₹300
  3. ₹3,000
  4. ₹0.30
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. ₹300

Profit = (84.80 − 84.50) × 1,000 = ₹300. The rate rose 30 paise per dollar on a 1,000-dollar contract.

Question 2

USD/INR spot is 84.00 and the six-month forward is 85.26. The annualised forward premium is about:

  1. 1.5%
  2. 6.0%
  3. 3.0%
  4. 1.26%
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. 3.0%

Premium = (85.26 − 84.00) ÷ 84.00 = 1.5% for six months; annualised = 1.5% × 2 = 3.0%. 1.5% is the unannualised figure.

Question 3

Convenience yield in commodity futures refers to:

  1. The benefit of holding the physical good
  2. Brokerage charged by the exchange
  3. Interest earned on margin money kept with the broker
  4. Profit made by a speculator
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. The benefit of holding the physical good

Convenience yield is the benefit to users of holding physical stock, such as avoiding production disruption. It can push futures prices below full cost of carry.

Question 4

In a contango market, futures prices are:

  1. Lower than spot prices
  2. Equal to spot prices at all times
  3. Not linked to spot prices
  4. Higher than spot prices
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Higher than spot prices

Contango means futures trade above spot, usually reflecting carrying costs. Backwardation is the reverse, often due to supply shortages or high convenience yield.

Question 5

Under interest rate parity, if Indian interest rates are higher than US rates, the USD/INR forward will trade at:

  1. A discount to spot
  2. Exactly the spot rate
  3. Zero
  4. A premium to spot
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. A premium to spot

The currency with the higher interest rate trades at a forward discount, so the dollar trades at a forward premium against the rupee. This offsets the interest differential.

Question 6

Physical delivery of a commodity futures contract means that at expiry:

  1. The seller delivers the commodity to the buyer
  2. Only price differences are paid in cash
  3. The contract is rolled over automatically to the next month
  4. The exchange buys the commodity
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. The seller delivers the commodity to the buyer

In physically settled contracts, sellers deliver the actual commodity meeting quality norms at designated warehouses, and buyers pay the settlement value.

Question 7

Why must a futures trader maintain a margin account?

  1. To earn guaranteed interest
  2. To cover daily losses
  3. To pay dividends on the underlying
  4. To pay the full contract value at expiry
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. To cover daily losses

Initial margin and daily mark-to-market ensure losses are settled every day, protecting the clearing corporation. It is not a savings or dividend account.

Question 8

Interest rate futures prices rise when:

  1. Bond yields rise
  2. Inflation expectations rise sharply
  3. Bond yields fall
  4. The RBI raises the repo rate
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Bond yields fall

IRF prices track bond prices, which rise when yields fall. Rate hikes and higher inflation expectations usually push yields up and IRF prices down.
