Strategies Using Futures: NISM Series XIII revision notes

Once pricing is clear, the next question is how to use futures. This chapter covers hedging equity portfolios with index futures, hedging single stocks, cash-and-carry arbitrage, currency hedges for importers and exporters, and hedging bond portfolios with interest rate futures. Expect calculation questions on the number of contracts and on arbitrage profit.

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Short hedge

A short hedge sells futures to protect an asset already held or to be sold. A mutual fund holding ₹10 crore of large-cap stocks that fears a fall over the next month can sell Nifty futures. Losses on the portfolio are offset by gains on the short futures, though basis risk remains.

2. Long hedge

A long hedge buys futures to lock in the price of something to be bought later. An investor expecting ₹50 lakh of bonus in two months, who wants to invest in the index at today's level, can buy index futures now. If the market rises, futures gains compensate for the higher purchase price.

3. Beta-based hedge ratio

Portfolios move with the market according to beta. Number of contracts = beta x portfolio value / (futures price x lot size). For a ₹12 crore portfolio with beta 1.25, Nifty futures at 24,000 and an assumed lot of 75: 1.25 x 12,00,00,000 / (24,000 x 75) = about 83 lots.

4. Changing portfolio beta

Futures can also raise or lower beta without trading the underlying shares. To reduce beta from 1.2 to 0.8, sell futures equal to (1.2 - 0.8) x portfolio value / futures contract value. Buying futures increases beta. This is cheaper and faster than selling shares.

5. Cash and carry arbitrage

If futures trade above fair value, buy the stock in cash and sell futures; hold till expiry and deliver or square off. Example: stock at ₹1,000, futures ₹1,020 for one month, carry cost ₹6. Arbitrage profit is about ₹14 per share before costs. Reverse arbitrage (sell cash, buy futures) works when futures are below fair value.

6. Exporters and importers

An exporter expecting USD receipts fears a fall in USD/INR, so sells USD/INR futures. An importer with USD payables fears a rise, so buys USD/INR futures. Example: importer buys 100 lots (USD 1,00,000) at 84.30; if spot rises to 85.00 at maturity, the futures gain of ₹70,000 offsets the higher rupee cost.

7. Hedging with interest rate futures

A bank holding G-secs fears rising yields, which cut bond prices. It sells IRF so that gains on futures offset losses on bonds. The number of contracts is based on matching modified duration or PV01 of the portfolio with that of the futures contract.

8. Basis risk and limits of hedging

A hedge is rarely perfect. If the portfolio is not identical to the index, or expiry does not match the exposure period, the gain on futures will not exactly offset the loss. Hedging also gives up upside: a short hedge loses on futures when markets rise.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Number of index futures contracts = beta x portfolio value / (futures price x lot size).
  • Contracts to change beta = (target beta - current beta) x portfolio value / (futures price x lot size); negative means sell.
  • Arbitrage profit = futures price - spot price - cost of carry (before transaction costs).

Common traps in the exam

  • Exporters sell USD/INR futures; importers buy them. Reversing this is a frequent wrong option.
  • Hedging removes upside as well as downside; it is not free insurance.
  • A long hedge protects against a price rise; a short hedge protects against a fall.
  • For a beta hedge, multiply portfolio value by beta before dividing by contract value.

Quick recap

Short hedge protects holdings against a fall; long hedge locks in purchase price. Contracts = beta x portfolio value / (futures price x lot size). Futures can raise or lower beta cheaply. Cash-and-carry when futures are rich, reverse when cheap. Exporters sell, importers buy USD/INR futures. IRF hedges use duration or PV01 matching. Basis risk means hedges are imperfect.
