What this chapter covers

This chapter covers practical strategies with futures: hedging long and short exposures, beta-adjusted hedging of equity portfolios, currency hedging by exporters and importers, interest rate hedging, cash-and-carry and reverse arbitrage, calendar spreads, inter-commodity spreads and pair trading. When revising, identify whether a user should buy or sell futures for a given exposure, compute the number of contracts needed, and understand the risks that remain, such as basis risk.

Strategies Using Futures questions

Question 1

A portfolio worth ₹60 lakh has a beta of 1.5. Each index futures contract is worth ₹15 lakh. How many contracts should be sold for a full hedge?

  1. 6
  2. 4
  3. 9
  4. 3
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. 6

Contracts = value × beta ÷ contract value = 60 × 1.5 ÷ 15 = 6. Ignoring beta gives 4, which would leave the portfolio under-hedged.

Question 2

An importer who must pay USD in three months fears a rising USD/INR. He should:

  1. Sell USD/INR futures
  2. Buy USD/INR futures
  3. Buy INR call options from a friend
  4. Wait and buy dollars later
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Buy USD/INR futures

Buying USD/INR futures locks in the rupee cost of dollars. If the rupee weakens, futures gains offset the higher cost of buying dollars.

Question 3

Basis risk in a hedge arises because:

  1. The exchange may close permanently
  2. Margins are not required in exchange-traded futures
  3. Spot and futures may not move identically
  4. Futures prices never change
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Spot and futures may not move identically

If the hedged asset and the futures contract do not move in exact step, the hedge is imperfect. This basis risk remains even with the correct number of contracts.

Question 4

A trader buys near-month gold futures and sells far-month gold futures. This is a:

  1. Inter-commodity spread
  2. Protective put
  3. Covered call
  4. Calendar spread
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Calendar spread

A calendar spread takes opposite positions in the same commodity across different expiries, profiting from changes in their price difference.

Question 5

A bond portfolio manager expects interest rates to rise. To hedge, he should:

  1. Buy interest rate futures
  2. Buy more long-term bonds
  3. Do nothing, as bonds rise with rates
  4. Sell interest rate futures
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Sell interest rate futures

Rising rates lower bond and IRF prices, so a short IRF position gains and offsets portfolio losses. Buying futures or bonds would increase exposure.

Question 6

A trader buys crude oil futures and sells natural gas futures expecting their price relationship to change. This is:

  1. An inter-commodity spread
  2. A calendar spread
  3. A cash-and-carry arbitrage
  4. A naked short position
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. An inter-commodity spread

Positions in two related commodities aim to profit from changes in their relative prices. Calendar spreads use the same commodity at different expiries.

Question 7

An investor holding shares worth ₹30 lakh wants to raise market exposure temporarily without buying more shares. She can:

  1. Sell index futures
  2. Sell her shares
  3. Buy index futures
  4. Buy put options on the index
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Buy index futures

Buying index futures adds market exposure with only margin. Selling futures, selling shares or buying puts would reduce exposure.

Question 8

An exporter hedges USD receivables by selling USD/INR futures. If the rupee weakens sharply instead, the exporter will:

  1. Gain on both the futures and the dollar receivables
  2. Lose on futures but gain on receivables
  3. Lose on both futures and receivables
  4. Be unaffected on both positions
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Lose on futures but gain on receivables

A weaker rupee raises the rupee value of receivables but causes losses on short futures. The hedge locks in a rate, giving up gains from favourable moves.
