Indices and Benchmarking: NISM Series XIX-E practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on Indices and Benchmarking, from the NISM Series XIX-E (Category III Alternative Investment Fund Managers) syllabus.

Last updated 16 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter explains how market indices are built and how Category III funds should choose benchmarks. Topics include price-weighted, equal-weighted and free-float market-cap-weighted indices, price return versus total return indices, rebalancing and reconstitution, survivorship bias in hedge-fund indices, and the qualities of a good benchmark (specified in advance, investable, measurable, appropriate to the strategy). Focus on matching benchmarks to strategies such as long-short or arbitrage, and on SEBI's push for standardised benchmarking of AIF performance.

Indices and Benchmarking questions

Question 1

A good benchmark for a Category III fund should be:

  1. Chosen after the year-end, based on whichever index the fund managed to beat during that year
  2. The same broad equity index for every type of AIF strategy
  3. Changed every quarter to show the fund in the best light
  4. Specified in advance, measurable and appropriate to the fund's strategy
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Specified in advance, measurable and appropriate to the fund's strategy

A valid benchmark is unambiguous, investable, measurable and set before performance is evaluated. Choosing or changing it after the fact misleads investors about the manager's skill.

Question 2

Survivorship bias in a hedge-fund database index tends to:

  1. Overstate the average returns of the hedge-fund universe
  2. Understate returns because only failed funds are included
  3. Have no effect because all funds report every month
  4. Reduce the measured volatility to zero
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Overstate the average returns of the hedge-fund universe

Funds that close, often after poor performance, drop out of databases, leaving mostly survivors. The index then looks better than the true experience of investors across all funds.

Question 3

In a free-float market capitalisation weighted index, a company with large promoter holding will have a weight that is:

  1. Higher than its weight by total market capitalisation
  2. Equal for all companies in the index
  3. Based only on its share price
  4. Lower than its weight by total market capitalisation
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Lower than its weight by total market capitalisation

Free-float weighting counts only shares available for trading, excluding promoter and locked-in holdings. Companies with high promoter stakes therefore get a smaller weight than total market cap suggests.

Question 4

A cash-futures arbitrage Category III fund benchmarks itself against the Nifty 50 TRI. Why is this inappropriate?

  1. The fund's returns resemble money-market returns, not equity market returns
  2. The Nifty 50 TRI does not include dividends
  3. Arbitrage funds are not allowed by SEBI to use or disclose any benchmark index at all
  4. The Nifty 50 contains only 10 stocks
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. The fund's returns resemble money-market returns, not equity market returns

An arbitrage fund locks in the spread between cash and futures, giving low-risk, debt-like returns. Comparing it with an equity index is misleading; a liquid or arbitrage index is more suitable. The TRI does include dividends.

Question 5

An equal-weighted index, compared with a market-cap weighted one, gives:

  1. More influence to smaller companies in the index
  2. Almost all influence to the largest companies
  3. Influence based on each stock's share price
  4. No role to index rebalancing
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. More influence to smaller companies in the index

Equal weighting assigns the same weight to every constituent, so smaller companies matter more than under cap weighting. It also requires regular rebalancing to restore equal weights.

Question 6

Index 'reconstitution' refers to:

  1. Recalculating the index only for dividends
  2. Converting a price return index into a currency-adjusted index for overseas investors
  3. Suspending the index during market holidays
  4. Changing the list of constituents according to the index rules
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Changing the list of constituents according to the index rules

Reconstitution periodically adds and removes constituents based on criteria such as size and liquidity. Rebalancing adjusts weights of existing constituents. Both affect funds tracking the index.

Question 7

A long-short equity fund that is typically 40% net long could reasonably construct a blended benchmark of:

  1. 40% equity index TRI and 60% money-market index
  2. 100% equity index TRI with double leverage
  3. 100% gold price index
  4. 60% equity index and 40% real estate index
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. 40% equity index TRI and 60% money-market index

A blended benchmark matching the fund's typical net exposure gives a fairer test of stock-selection skill. A full equity benchmark would penalise or flatter the fund depending on market direction.

Question 8

An index provider caps the weight of any single stock in a sector index. The main purpose of such capping is to:

  1. Limit concentration so the index stays diversified
  2. Guarantee that the index will rise faster than the broad market every year
  3. Give the largest company a bigger weight than its free-float market value
  4. Remove the need for periodic index rebalancing or reconstitution
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Limit concentration so the index stays diversified

Without caps, one or two dominant companies can drive a sector index, making it concentrated and harder for funds to replicate within their own limits. Capping keeps the index diversified; it does not promise returns or remove rebalancing.
