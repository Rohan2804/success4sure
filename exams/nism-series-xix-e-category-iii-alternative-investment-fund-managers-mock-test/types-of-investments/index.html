Types of Investments: NISM Series XIX-E practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on Types of Investments, from the NISM Series XIX-E (Category III Alternative Investment Fund Managers) syllabus.

Last updated 16 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter introduces the instruments a Category III manager can use: listed equity, debt and money-market instruments, futures, options, swaps and structured exposures, as well as cash-equivalents used as collateral. Focus on how each instrument changes the fund's exposure — for example, how futures give leveraged exposure for a small margin, how short positions profit from falling prices, and how option buyers and sellers face very different risk profiles. Practise simple pay-off calculations for futures and options, and understand why derivatives are used both for hedging and for taking views.

Types of Investments questions

Question 1

A fund sells (writes) a call option on a stock it does not own. The theoretical maximum loss on this position is:

  1. Limited to the premium received at the time of writing
  2. Limited to the strike price of the option
  3. Zero, because the option seller always keeps the premium
  4. Unlimited, because the stock price can keep rising
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Unlimited, because the stock price can keep rising

A naked call writer must deliver the stock at the strike price however high the market price goes, so losses have no upper limit. The premium received is the writer's maximum gain, not the maximum loss.

Question 2

A fund buys 1 lot of index futures (lot value ₹15 lakh) by paying an initial margin of ₹2 lakh. If the index rises 4%, the gain as a percentage of margin is closest to:

  1. 4%
  2. 8%
  3. 30%
  4. 15%
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. 30%

Gain = 4% × ₹15 lakh = ₹60,000. As a share of the ₹2 lakh margin, that is 30%. This shows how futures provide leveraged exposure: a small margin controls a much larger notional value.

Question 3

A Category III fund wants to profit from a fall in the price of a stock it does not own. The standard way to do this in Indian markets is to:

  1. Sell the stock's futures contract
  2. Buy the stock in the cash market
  3. Buy a call option on the stock
  4. Subscribe to the stock's rights issue
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Sell the stock's futures contract

Selling futures creates a short exposure that gains when the price falls. Buying the stock or a call option are bullish positions, and a rights issue adds to the holding.

Question 4

In an interest rate swap, a fund that pays fixed and receives floating will benefit when:

  1. Floating interest rates fall sharply after the swap starts
  2. The fixed rate is reset to a higher level by the counterparty
  3. Bond yields remain exactly unchanged for the full tenor
  4. Floating interest rates rise above the fixed rate
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Floating interest rates rise above the fixed rate

Paying fixed and receiving floating gains when floating payments received exceed the fixed payments made. If floating rates fall, the fund receives less while still paying the same fixed rate.

Question 5

A fund buys a stock at ₹800 and simultaneously buys a put option with strike ₹760 for a premium of ₹15. The maximum loss per share on the combined position is:

  1. ₹15
  2. ₹55
  3. ₹40
  4. ₹775
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. ₹55

Below ₹760 the put offsets further falls, so the loss is the ₹40 drop to the strike plus the ₹15 premium = ₹55. This 'protective put' caps downside at a known cost.

Question 6

Treasury bills are often held by Category III funds alongside derivative positions mainly because they:

  1. Are near-cash, low-risk instruments useful as margin and liquidity buffers
  2. Pay the highest coupon among all fixed income instruments issued in India today
  3. Are equity-linked and increase the fund's market exposure
  4. Cannot be pledged and therefore protect the fund from margin calls
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Are near-cash, low-risk instruments useful as margin and liquidity buffers

T-bills are short-term sovereign instruments with minimal credit and interest rate risk, making them suitable collateral and cash management tools. They are issued at a discount and carry no coupon at all.

Question 7

An option's 'time value' is best defined as:

  1. The full premium paid for an at-the-money option
  2. The part of the premium above the option's intrinsic value
  3. The difference between strike price and futures price
  4. The interest earned on the margin money deposited with the broker until expiry
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. The part of the premium above the option's intrinsic value

Option premium = intrinsic value + time value. Time value reflects the chance that the option becomes more valuable before expiry, and it decays to zero by the expiry date.

Question 8

A convertible bond is attractive to some hedge strategies because it:

  1. Combines a fixed income claim with an embedded option to convert into equity
  2. Carries no credit risk at all since it is always backed by a full government guarantee
  3. Must be converted into shares on the date of issue
  4. Is a pure equity instrument with voting rights from day one
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Combines a fixed income claim with an embedded option to convert into equity

A convertible pays interest like a bond but lets the holder convert into shares, so its value has a bond floor plus an equity option. Convertible arbitrage strategies try to exploit mispricing of that embedded option.
