Fund Monitoring, Reporting and Exit: NISM Series XIX-E practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on Fund Monitoring, Reporting and Exit, from the NISM Series XIX-E (Category III Alternative Investment Fund Managers) syllabus.

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter covers how a Category III fund monitors risk and reports to investors and SEBI, and how investors exit. Monitoring tools include exposure limits, leverage tracking, Value at Risk, stress testing, liquidity analysis and drawdown limits. Reporting covers NAV statements, portfolio and risk disclosures, periodic reports to investors and regulatory filings. Exit topics include redemptions in open-ended funds, winding up of close-ended funds, in-specie distributions and dealing with illiquid residual assets. Focus on interpreting risk measures and on fair treatment at exit.

Fund Monitoring, Reporting and Exit questions

Question 1

A fund's one-day 99% VaR is ₹5 crore. This means:

  1. On 1 day in 100, losses are expected to exceed ₹5 crore
  2. The fund can never lose more than ₹5 crore in a day
  3. The fund will lose ₹5 crore on 99 days out of 100
  4. The fund's average daily loss is ₹5 crore
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. On 1 day in 100, losses are expected to exceed ₹5 crore

VaR gives a loss threshold at a confidence level. At 99%, losses should exceed ₹5 crore on about 1% of days. VaR does not show how large those tail losses could be.

Question 2

A fund's one-day VaR is ₹2 crore. Using the square-root-of-time rule, its approximate 25-day VaR is:

  1. ₹10 crore
  2. ₹50 crore
  3. ₹25 crore
  4. ₹4 crore
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. ₹10 crore

Under the square-root-of-time rule, VaR scales with the square root of the horizon: ₹2 crore × √25 = ₹2 crore × 5 = ₹10 crore. This assumes independent, identically distributed returns.

Question 3

Which is a key limitation of Value at Risk?

  1. It says nothing about the size of losses beyond the threshold
  2. It cannot be calculated for equity portfolios
  3. It always overstates risk in calm markets
  4. It requires the fund to hold no derivatives, short positions or leverage at the time of calculation
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. It says nothing about the size of losses beyond the threshold

VaR tells you the threshold but not how bad the tail can get. Expected shortfall (conditional VaR) and stress tests complement it by looking at losses beyond VaR.

Question 4

Liquidity monitoring of an open-ended Category III fund mainly compares:

  1. The fund's returns against the Nifty 50
  2. How quickly holdings can be sold against likely redemption demands
  3. The manager's salary against the fee income
  4. The number of investors against the custodian's capacity
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. How quickly holdings can be sold against likely redemption demands

Funds must be able to meet redemptions without fire sales. Liquidity analysis maps the time needed to liquidate positions against expected and stressed outflows.

Question 5

A close-ended Category III fund reaches the end of its tenure with some unlisted holdings unsold. One possible exit route permitted under SEBI's framework is:

  1. Selling the assets to the sponsor at any price it chooses
  2. In-specie distribution of the unsold assets to investors, with their consent
  3. Extending the fund's tenure indefinitely without seeking any approval from the investors
  4. Writing the assets off and charging investors a penalty
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. In-specie distribution of the unsold assets to investors, with their consent

SEBI's framework for unliquidated investments allows options such as in-specie distribution or transfer to a liquidation scheme, subject to investor consent. Unilateral extension or related-party sales at arbitrary prices are not acceptable.

Question 6

A stress test that applies the market moves of a past crisis to today's portfolio is called:

  1. Monte Carlo simulation
  2. Historical scenario analysis
  3. Back-testing of a trading signal
  4. Attribution analysis
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Historical scenario analysis

Historical scenarios replay real past events, such as a market crash, to see how the current portfolio would fare. Monte Carlo uses random simulations, and attribution explains past returns.

Question 7

Investors receive a report showing that a fund's leverage rose from 1.2x to 1.9x NAV in one quarter. A sensible investor action is to:

  1. Ignore it because leverage always improves returns
  2. Redeem immediately without asking any questions, since higher leverage always means fraud
  3. Ask the manager why leverage rose and whether it fits the stated strategy
  4. Ask the custodian to reduce leverage directly
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Ask the manager why leverage rose and whether it fits the stated strategy

A sharp rise in leverage may signal style drift or increased risk. Investors should seek explanation and check consistency with the PPM before deciding on action.

Question 8

Expected shortfall (conditional VaR) is best described as:

  1. The average loss in the worst cases beyond the VaR threshold
  2. The smallest loss the fund can make in a year
  3. The loss on the single best day of the year
  4. The fund's expected return minus its fees
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. The average loss in the worst cases beyond the VaR threshold

Expected shortfall averages the losses in the tail beyond VaR, giving a better view of extreme risk. It is increasingly preferred because it captures tail severity that VaR ignores.
