AIF Structuring: NISM Series XIX-E practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on AIF Structuring, from the NISM Series XIX-E (Category III Alternative Investment Fund Managers) syllabus.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter explains how Category III AIFs are legally and economically structured. It covers the legal forms (trust, LLP, company, body corporate), open-ended versus close-ended funds, schemes under one registration, master-feeder and parallel structures, classes of units, lock-ins and redemption terms, and how commitment and drawdown work for close-ended funds. Pay attention to how structure affects taxation, investor rights, liquidity and fairness between investors entering at different times.

AIF Structuring questions

Question 1

The most common legal form used for AIFs in India is a:

  1. Public limited company listed on an exchange
  2. Cooperative society registered with a state
  3. Sole proprietorship of the fund manager
  4. Trust registered under the Indian Trusts Act
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Trust registered under the Indian Trusts Act

Most Indian AIFs are set up as trusts because of flexibility and familiarity with pass-through and trustee-based governance. LLPs and companies are permitted but less common.

Question 2

In a master-feeder structure, the feeder fund:

  1. Pools money from investors and invests it into the master fund
  2. Makes all trades directly while the master only holds cash
  3. Acts as the custodian for the master fund's assets
  4. Lends money to the master fund at a fixed rate
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Pools money from investors and invests it into the master fund

Feeders collect capital, often from different investor groups, and channel it into one master fund that runs the portfolio. This allows one strategy to serve several investor types efficiently.

Question 3

A close-ended Category III fund calls only 30% of committed capital at the first close. The remaining commitments will be drawn:

  1. Automatically from the investor's bank account each month
  2. Only if the fund's NAV falls below the first-close NAV
  3. Through later drawdown notices as investment opportunities arise
  4. From the sponsor, since investors are required to pay only the first instalment of their commitment
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Through later drawdown notices as investment opportunities arise

Commitment-based funds draw capital in stages via drawdown notices. Investors are legally bound to meet calls up to their commitment; the sponsor does not cover investor shortfalls.

Question 4

Issuing different classes of units within a Category III fund is commonly used to:

  1. Avoid disclosing the portfolio to any investor
  2. Give some investors a guaranteed share of profits
  3. Bypass the minimum investment requirement
  4. Offer different fee structures or terms to different investor groups
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Offer different fee structures or terms to different investor groups

Unit classes allow differences in fees, currency or lock-in while sharing a portfolio. They must be disclosed and cannot be used to guarantee returns or evade regulatory minimums.

Question 5

An open-ended Category III fund receives large subscriptions immediately after a sharp NAV rise. Fair treatment of existing investors in performance-fee calculation is mainly addressed through:

  1. Converting the fund to a close-ended structure
  2. Suspending NAV calculation for a quarter
  3. Paying new investors a bonus in units
  4. Equalisation or series accounting
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Equalisation or series accounting

Equalisation or series accounting ensures each investor pays performance fees only on gains they actually earned. Without it, incoming investors may pay fees on gains they did not enjoy, or escape fees on later gains.

Question 6

A parallel fund structure is generally used when:

  1. The manager wants to avoid all investor reporting
  2. Different investor groups need separate vehicles that invest side by side
  3. The fund wants to invest only in a single stock
  4. Investors want to redeem their units every day without giving any notice to the manager
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Different investor groups need separate vehicles that invest side by side

Parallel funds invest alongside the main fund in proportion, often to accommodate tax, regulatory or currency needs of particular investors. They do not reduce reporting obligations.

Question 7

Why might a manager choose a close-ended rather than open-ended structure for a Category III fund holding less liquid listed small-caps?

  1. To charge a higher management fee that open-ended Category III funds are not allowed to charge
  2. To match fund liquidity with the time needed to exit the holdings
  3. To avoid having any NAV computed during the tenure
  4. To be able to accept retail investors with small tickets
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. To match fund liquidity with the time needed to exit the holdings

Illiquid holdings can be hard to sell quickly to meet redemptions. A close-ended structure avoids forced selling and liquidity mismatch, while NAV reporting and investor minimums still apply.

Question 8

A Category III AIF launches a new scheme under its existing registration. Which document must be filed with SEBI for the new scheme?

  1. A red herring prospectus for listing the scheme's units on the main board of a stock exchange
  2. A scheme information document approved by AMFI
  3. A fresh certificate of incorporation from the ROC
  4. A placement memorandum for the scheme, filed through a merchant banker
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. A placement memorandum for the scheme, filed through a merchant banker

AIF schemes are launched through a private placement memorandum, filed with SEBI via a merchant banker. Prospectuses and scheme information documents relate to public issues and mutual funds.
