AIFs in India and Its Suitability: NISM Series XIX-E practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on AIFs in India and Its Suitability, from the NISM Series XIX-E (Category III Alternative Investment Fund Managers) syllabus.

Last updated 16 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter explains how SEBI classifies Alternative Investment Funds into Categories I, II and III, what distinguishes Category III (diverse or complex trading strategies, use of leverage, investment in listed and unlisted derivatives), and how these funds compare with mutual funds and PMS. It also covers who should invest: high-net-worth and institutional investors able to bear risk, illiquidity and higher fees. Focus on suitability — matching strategy risk, lock-in and liquidity to investor profile — and on the minimum investment and investor-count rules in concept.

AIFs in India and Its Suitability questions

Question 1

A Category III AIF is distinguished from Category I and II AIFs mainly because it:

  1. May use leverage and complex trading strategies, including in listed securities and derivatives
  2. Is required to invest only in start-ups and social ventures
  3. Can only invest in unlisted debt of infrastructure companies and in government-approved social sector projects
  4. Is not allowed to raise money from domestic investors at all
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. May use leverage and complex trading strategies, including in listed securities and derivatives

Category III covers funds that employ diverse or complex trading strategies and may employ leverage, such as hedge funds and long-short funds. Category I covers start-up, social and infrastructure-focused funds, and Category II covers private equity and private debt.

Question 2

Which investor is generally most suitable for a market-neutral Category III fund?

  1. A retiree who needs a fixed monthly income and full safety of capital from the investment
  2. A family office seeking returns with low correlation to equity markets
  3. A first-time investor with ₹50,000 in savings
  4. A college student who wants daily liquidity for expenses
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. A family office seeking returns with low correlation to equity markets

Market-neutral funds aim to generate returns that are independent of market direction, which suits sophisticated investors wanting diversification. They need high minimum ticket sizes and are not suitable for investors who need capital safety or regular income.

Question 3

Compared with an equity mutual fund, a long-only Category III AIF typically allows the manager to:

  1. Offer the scheme to retail investors with a ₹500 SIP
  2. Guarantee a minimum return to investors each year
  3. Avoid all disclosure of fees to investors
  4. Run a more concentrated portfolio with fewer diversification limits
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Run a more concentrated portfolio with fewer diversification limits

Mutual funds face strict concentration limits for retail protection. Category III AIFs, aimed at sophisticated investors, can hold more concentrated positions, but still disclose fees and cannot guarantee returns.

Question 4

An investor wants exposure to a strategy that profits mainly from mispricing between cash and futures markets. The most relevant AIF category is:

  1. Category III, which covers trading and arbitrage strategies
  2. Category I, which covers venture capital and SME funds
  3. Category II, which covers private equity and private debt
  4. A Social Impact Fund under Category I
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Category III, which covers trading and arbitrage strategies

Cash-futures arbitrage is a trading strategy in listed markets, which falls within Category III. Categories I and II mainly invest in unlisted companies with long holding periods.

Question 5

A key suitability risk of recommending a leveraged Category III fund to a client is that:

  1. Leverage always guarantees higher returns than the benchmark
  2. Leverage removes the need for any risk management because borrowed money always boosts the final return
  3. Losses can be magnified beyond what the client expects from the market move
  4. Leveraged funds cannot ever fall below their initial NAV
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Losses can be magnified beyond what the client expects from the market move

Leverage magnifies both gains and losses. A client who does not understand this can face drawdowns larger than the underlying market fall, so suitability assessment must cover loss-bearing capacity.

Question 6

Why are AIFs restricted to investors with large minimum commitments?

  1. To limit participation to investors who can bear complex risks and illiquidity
  2. To ensure that the returns delivered by AIFs are always higher than those of comparable mutual funds
  3. Because AIFs are exempt from all SEBI regulation
  4. To allow AIFs to be listed on the main board of exchanges
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. To limit participation to investors who can bear complex risks and illiquidity

High minimum ticket sizes act as a filter for sophistication and capacity to absorb losses. AIFs remain SEBI-regulated, and a high ticket size does not imply superior returns.

Question 7

Which pair correctly matches an AIF strategy with its usual category?

  1. Venture capital fund — Category III
  2. Private debt fund — Category I
  3. Infrastructure fund — Category III
  4. Long-short equity fund — Category III
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Long-short equity fund — Category III

Long-short equity is a classic hedge-fund style strategy and falls in Category III. Venture capital and infrastructure funds belong to Category I, while private debt and private equity sit in Category II.

Question 8

A client with a ₹3 crore portfolio wants to put ₹2.5 crore into a single high-volatility Category III fund. The most appropriate response is to:

  1. Accept the investment as proposed, since the client comfortably meets the minimum ticket size for AIFs
  2. Advise borrowing more money to increase the allocation further
  3. Recommend the fund because past returns have been strong
  4. Explain concentration risk and suggest a smaller allocation suited to the overall plan
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Explain concentration risk and suggest a smaller allocation suited to the overall plan

Meeting the minimum investment does not make a large concentrated allocation suitable. Putting over 80% of wealth in one volatile fund creates excessive risk, so the investor should be guided to a proportionate allocation.
