Investment Strategies: NISM Series XIX-E practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on Investment Strategies, from the NISM Series XIX-E (Category III Alternative Investment Fund Managers) syllabus.

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter surveys the strategies used by Category III funds: long-only and long-short equity, market-neutral, pairs trading, statistical and quantitative strategies, merger and event-driven arbitrage, cash-futures arbitrage, volatility and options strategies, fixed income relative value, global macro and managed futures. Learners should understand each strategy's source of return, its main risks, typical leverage and exposure, and how gross and net exposure are computed. Expect scenario questions describing a position and asking which strategy it represents or how it performs in a given market move.

Investment Strategies questions

Question 1

Company A announces it will acquire Company B at ₹500 per share in cash; B trades at ₹470. A merger arbitrage fund buys B. Its main risk is that:

  1. The broad equity market rises 5% next week
  2. B's price moves up to exactly ₹500 on completion
  3. Interest rates on government bonds fall slightly
  4. The deal fails and B's price falls back sharply
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. The deal fails and B's price falls back sharply

Merger arbitrage earns the spread if the deal completes. The key risk is deal failure from regulatory, financing or shareholder issues, which can send the target's price down well below the current price.

Question 2

A fund has NAV ₹200 crore, long positions of ₹240 crore and short positions of ₹160 crore. Its gross exposure as a percentage of NAV is:

  1. 40%
  2. 120%
  3. 200%
  4. 80%
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. 200%

Gross exposure = (long + short) / NAV = (240 + 160) / 200 = 200%. Net exposure would be (240 − 160) / 200 = 40%. Gross shows total risk and leverage, net shows directional bias.

Question 3

A volatility-selling strategy that writes options regularly tends to show:

  1. Occasional large gains with frequent small losses
  2. No losses at all over time, because the option premiums received are always earned by the seller
  3. Returns that rise sharply in market crashes
  4. Steady small gains with occasional large losses in market shocks
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Steady small gains with occasional large losses in market shocks

Option sellers collect premium most of the time but face large pay-outs when markets move sharply. This creates negatively skewed returns, often described as 'picking up coins in front of a steamroller'.

Question 4

A statistical arbitrage strategy typically relies on:

  1. Long-term ownership of a single unlisted company
  2. Physical storage of agricultural commodities
  3. Quantitative models exploiting short-term price relationships across many securities
  4. Lending money to infrastructure projects for ten years or more and holding the loans to maturity
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Quantitative models exploiting short-term price relationships across many securities

Stat-arb uses models to identify small, temporary mispricings, often betting that prices will revert to historical relationships. Positions are many, short-dated and usually close to market-neutral.

Question 5

Managed futures or CTA-style strategies typically make money by:

  1. Collecting rental income from commercial property
  2. Following price trends across futures markets, both long and short
  3. Buying start-ups and holding them for a decade
  4. Holding only fixed deposits until maturity
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Following price trends across futures markets, both long and short

Trend-following strategies go long rising markets and short falling ones across equity, bond, currency and commodity futures. They have historically performed well in sustained trending periods, including some crises.

Question 6

A fund buys a 10-year government bond and sells a 5-year government bond expecting the yield curve to flatten. This is an example of:

  1. Fixed income relative value
  2. Merger arbitrage
  3. Long-only small-cap investing
  4. Distressed equity investing
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Fixed income relative value

Relative value trades take offsetting positions in related instruments to profit from changes in their relationship, here the spread between 10-year and 5-year yields, rather than the direction of rates.

Question 7

A long-short equity fund is 120% long and 90% short. If the whole market falls 10% and both books move with the market, the approximate impact on NAV is:

  1. −10%
  2. −3%
  3. −12%
  4. +9%
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. −3%

Net exposure = 120% − 90% = 30%. A 10% market fall costs about 30% × 10% = 3% of NAV. The long book loses 12% of NAV and the short book gains 9%, netting to −3%.

Question 8

A global macro manager believes the RBI will cut rates more than the market expects. A consistent trade would be to:

  1. Buy long-duration government bonds
  2. Sell long-duration government bonds
  3. Buy floating-rate notes only
  4. Short bank fixed deposits
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Buy long-duration government bonds

If rates fall more than expected, bond yields fall and long-duration bond prices rise most. Selling bonds or buying floating-rate notes would not benefit from an unexpected rate cut.
