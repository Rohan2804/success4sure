Investments Landscape: NISM Series XIX-E practice questions

8 free questions with answers and explanations on Investments Landscape, from the NISM Series XIX-E (Category III Alternative Investment Fund Managers) syllabus.

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter sets the scene for alternative investing. It explains how investment differs from saving and speculation, the difference between real and financial assets, nominal versus real returns, and how traditional assets (listed equity, bonds, cash) compare with alternatives such as hedge-fund style strategies, private equity and real estate. Learners should be clear on the typical features of alternatives — lower liquidity, higher fees, less transparency and complex valuation — and on investor concepts like risk tolerance, risk capacity, time horizon and the idea of absolute versus relative return mandates. Revise the definitions carefully, because later chapters build on them.

Investments Landscape questions

Question 1

Which of the following is a real asset rather than a financial asset?

  1. A listed corporate bond of a logistics company
  2. Units of a liquid mutual fund scheme
  3. A fixed deposit receipt issued by a scheduled bank
  4. A warehouse leased out to a logistics company
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. A warehouse leased out to a logistics company

A real asset is a physical or tangible asset that produces value directly, such as property, land or machinery. Bonds, mutual fund units and deposits are financial claims on someone else's assets or cash flows.

Question 2

Compared with long-only listed equity funds, alternative investment strategies typically show:

  1. Daily liquidity, lower fees and fully standardised valuation
  2. Guaranteed returns with complete transparency of every position
  3. Lower minimum ticket sizes aimed at small retail savers
  4. Lower liquidity, higher fees and more complex valuation
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Lower liquidity, higher fees and more complex valuation

Alternatives usually carry lock-ins or redemption limits, management plus performance fees and positions that are harder to value. They are meant for sophisticated investors, not small savers, and returns are never guaranteed.

Question 3

A portfolio earned a nominal return of 12% in a year when inflation was 5%. Its real return, computed exactly, is closest to:

  1. 7.00%
  2. 17.60%
  3. 6.67%
  4. 5.71%
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. 6.67%

Real return = (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) − 1 = 1.12 / 1.05 − 1 = 6.67%. Simply subtracting 5 from 12 gives 7%, which is only an approximation that overstates the real return.

Question 4

A Category III fund states that its objective is to deliver positive returns in both rising and falling markets. This is best described as:

  1. An absolute return mandate
  2. A relative return mandate measured only against the Nifty 50
  3. A passive index-replication mandate with low tracking error
  4. A capital protection mandate backed by a bank guarantee
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. An absolute return mandate

An absolute return mandate targets positive returns regardless of market direction, often using shorting and hedging. A relative return mandate only seeks to beat a benchmark, even if the benchmark itself falls.

Question 5

The hedge fund structure is commonly traced back to Alfred Winslow Jones, whose fund combined:

  1. Only government bonds held to maturity
  2. Commodity futures and physical gold storage
  3. Long stock positions, short sales and leverage
  4. Real estate rental income and infrastructure loans
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Long stock positions, short sales and leverage

Jones bought stocks he liked, shorted stocks he disliked to reduce market exposure, and used leverage to magnify stock-picking returns. This 'hedged' long-short idea gave hedge funds their name.

Question 6

An investor needs a large part of her portfolio to pay for her daughter's overseas education in 18 months. Which constraint should dominate any decision to invest in a Category III AIF?

  1. Her preference for funds with famous managers
  2. The fund's past three-year return compared with peers
  3. Liquidity needs and the short time horizon
  4. Whether the fund invests mainly in large-cap stocks
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Liquidity needs and the short time horizon

Money needed within 18 months should not be locked in vehicles with lock-ins, gates or volatile returns. Liquidity and horizon are binding constraints; past returns or manager reputation cannot override them.

Question 7

Why do investors generally demand a 'liquidity premium' from alternative investments?

  1. Because SEBI mandates a minimum return for all alternative funds
  2. Because liquid investments are always taxed at higher rates in India
  3. They accept that money may be hard to withdraw quickly at fair value
  4. Because illiquid assets have no price volatility at all
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. They accept that money may be hard to withdraw quickly at fair value

When capital can be locked in or sold only at a discount, investors expect extra return as compensation. No regulation guarantees AIF returns, and illiquid assets still carry risk even if prices are observed less often.

Question 8

Under SEBI's accredited investor framework, accreditation certificates are issued by:

  1. The AIF manager after reviewing the investor's bank statements
  2. The Income Tax Department through the annual tax return
  3. Accreditation agencies recognised for this purpose by SEBI
  4. Any registered stock broker with whom the investor trades
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Accreditation agencies recognised for this purpose by SEBI

SEBI designates accreditation agencies (subsidiaries of depositories and exchanges) to verify income or net worth and issue accreditation. A fund manager cannot self-certify an investor as accredited.
