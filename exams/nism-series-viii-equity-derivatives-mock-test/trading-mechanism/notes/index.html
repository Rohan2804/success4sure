Trading Mechanism: NISM Series VIII revision notes

This chapter explains how derivative orders are placed and matched on Indian exchanges, the types of members, order conditions and the limits that control excessive positions. Questions are factual: which order type behaves how, who a clearing member is, and what happens when a stock crosses its market-wide position limit.

Last updated 19 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Screen-based trading

NSE's derivatives segment uses an anonymous, order-driven electronic system (NEAT and its successors), and BSE uses BOLT Plus. Orders are matched on price-time priority: the best price first, and among equal prices, the earliest order first. Members access the system through terminals and approved APIs.

2. Member categories

Trading members execute trades for themselves and clients. Clearing members clear and settle trades. Trading-cum-clearing members do both. Professional clearing members (often banks or custodians) clear and settle trades of other trading members but do not trade themselves. Each category has separate net worth requirements.

3. Order types

A limit order executes at the specified price or better. A market order executes immediately at the best available price. A stop-loss order becomes active only when the trigger price is reached, then becomes a limit or market order. Exchanges may restrict market orders in illiquid contracts.

4. Time and other conditions

Day orders are valid until the end of the trading day. Immediate or Cancel (IOC) orders execute fully or partly at once and any unfilled portion is cancelled. Exchanges in India generally do not allow orders to remain beyond the day for derivatives.

5. Client identification

Every order must carry the client's Unique Client Code, registered with the exchange along with PAN. Brokers must complete KYC, a client registration form and a risk disclosure document before a client trades. Pro orders (for the member's own account) must be distinguished from client orders.

6. Eligibility of stocks

SEBI sets criteria for stocks to have derivatives: market capitalisation, average daily turnover and delivery value, and market-wide position limits, among others. Stocks failing the criteria for a set period are excluded from F&O after existing contracts expire. Check current criteria with SEBI.

7. Market-wide position limit

Each stock has a market-wide position limit (MWPL) based on its free float or delivery data. When open interest crosses 95% of MWPL, the stock enters a ban period: no fresh positions are allowed, only positions that reduce exposure, until open interest falls below 80%.

8. Client and member limits

Exchanges set position limits at client, trading member and market-wide levels for index and stock derivatives. These prevent any single participant from cornering a contract. Limits for index derivatives are generally higher than for individual stocks. Breaches attract penalties and forced reduction of positions.

9. Base price and price bands

Derivative contracts have operating ranges; orders outside the range are rejected or need confirmation. Index futures have flexible price bands tied to the underlying's movement. Options do not have fixed price bands but have operating ranges to prevent fat-finger errors.

Common traps in the exam

  • Thinking a professional clearing member can trade on its own account - it only clears and settles for trading members.
  • Believing a stop-loss order is executed immediately; it waits until the trigger price is touched.
  • Assuming existing positions must be closed during an F&O ban; only fresh positions are barred.
  • Mixing up the 95% trigger that starts a ban with the 80% level that ends it.

Quick recap

Orders match on price-time priority in anonymous electronic systems. Trading members trade, clearing members settle, PCMs only clear. Limit, market, stop-loss, day and IOC orders behave differently. Every order needs a UCC. F&O ban starts when open interest crosses 95% of MWPL and ends below 80%.
