Sales Practices and Investor Protection: NISM Series VIII revision notes

This chapter focuses on how brokers must deal with derivatives clients: KYC, assessing suitability, risk disclosure, fair conduct and handling complaints. Questions test the documents a client must receive and the escalation path for grievances.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Know your client

Before trading, brokers must complete KYC through a KRA, collect PAN and verify identity, and capture income and net worth details. Clients trading F&O must provide proof of financial capacity, such as income tax returns, salary slips or bank statements, before the segment is activated.

2. Suitability

Derivatives are leveraged and complex. Brokers should assess whether a client understands the products and can bear losses, and should not encourage inexperienced clients to trade F&O. Unsolicited tips promising profits are a sales malpractice. Brokers must also review clients' financial details periodically, particularly when trading volumes rise sharply compared with declared income.

3. Risk disclosure document

Every client must receive the Risk Disclosure Document explaining leverage, margin calls, the possibility of losing more than the initial margin and the risks of options writing. SEBI also requires brokers to show a risk disclosure on F&O losses to clients, based on SEBI's studies.

4. Fair dealing

Brokers must execute orders as instructed, send contract notes within the prescribed time, settle client funds periodically and segregate client money and securities. Trading in clients' accounts without authorisation and misusing client collateral are serious violations. Clients should check contract notes and the exchange's trade verification alerts sent by SMS and email.

5. Margin and collateral rules

Client margins must be collected upfront and reported to the exchange; client securities pledged as margin stay in the client's demat account via the margin pledge system rather than being transferred to the broker. This reduces misuse. Clients can verify their balances through statements from the exchange and depository.

6. Grievance redressal

A client should first complain to the broker. If not satisfied, the complaint can be escalated to the exchange's investor service cell and to SEBI through SCORES, and then resolved through the SMART ODR platform for online conciliation and arbitration.

7. Avoiding tips and finfluencers

SEBI has barred regulated entities such as brokers from associating with unregistered persons who give investment advice or make claims about returns, including many social media 'finfluencers'. Clients should treat promises of sure-shot option profits, paid tip groups and requests to share login credentials as red flags and report them.

8. Investor Protection Fund

Exchanges maintain an Investor Protection Fund to compensate investors with legitimate claims when a broker is declared a defaulter, up to specified limits. The IPF does not cover trading losses from market movements. Claims are scrutinised by the exchange's committee before any payout is approved, and limits per investor are reviewed periodically.

Common traps in the exam

  • Thinking the IPF compensates trading losses - it covers claims against defaulting brokers only.
  • Skipping the broker and going straight to SMART ODR; the broker and SCORES come first.
  • Believing a client cannot lose more than the margin deposited; futures losses can exceed initial margin.
  • Assuming F&O can be activated with only PAN and address proof; proof of financial capacity is required.

Quick recap

KYC plus proof of income before F&O activation. Assess suitability, give the Risk Disclosure Document and never promise profits. Segregate client funds; margins stay pledged in client demat. Complaints: broker, then exchange/SCORES, then SMART ODR. IPF pays for broker defaults, not market losses.
