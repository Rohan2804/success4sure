What this chapter covers

This chapter introduces what derivatives are, how their value depends on an underlying asset, and the main types: forwards, futures, options and swaps. It contrasts exchange-traded and OTC contracts, explains the roles of hedgers, speculators and arbitrageurs, and covers the economic functions of derivatives such as price discovery and risk transfer. When revising, be clear on counterparty risk, standardisation, leverage and why derivatives markets attract both risk managers and traders. Expect conceptual questions with short scenarios.

Basics of Derivatives questions

Question 1

Which of the following is an example of a derivative whose underlying is not a financial asset?

  1. An equity share of an oil company
  2. A Treasury Bill issued by the RBI
  3. A futures contract on crude oil
  4. A unit of a liquid mutual fund scheme
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. A futures contract on crude oil

A crude oil future derives its value from a physical commodity. An equity share, a T-Bill and a mutual fund unit are themselves primary securities, not contracts deriving value from something else.

Question 2

A jeweller expects to buy gold in three months and fears a price rise. Using derivatives, she is acting as a hedger if she:

  1. Sells gold futures to profit from an expected fall
  2. Buys gold futures to lock in her purchase cost
  3. Buys gold futures purely because she expects prices to rise
  4. Buys gold in one market and sells it in another at the same time
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Buys gold futures to lock in her purchase cost

A hedger uses derivatives to offset an existing or expected exposure. Buying futures fixes the cost of a future purchase. Taking a view without exposure is speculation, and simultaneous buy-sell for a price gap is arbitrage.

Question 3

Why are swaps generally classified as OTC derivatives rather than exchange-traded ones?

  1. They can be traded only by the Reserve Bank of India
  2. They never involve exchange of any cash flows between parties
  3. They are always settled by physical delivery of the underlying
  4. Their terms are privately negotiated between two parties
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Their terms are privately negotiated between two parties

Swaps are bilateral agreements whose notional, tenor and payment terms are tailored to the parties' needs, which is the hallmark of OTC contracts. They do involve exchange of cash flows, usually net, not physical delivery.

Question 4

What does 'leverage' in a futures position mean for a trader?

  1. A small margin controls a large contract value
  2. The trader pays the full contract value upfront and earns interest on it
  3. Losses are capped at the margin deposited with the broker
  4. The exchange guarantees a minimum return on every position
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. A small margin controls a large contract value

Futures require only a margin, a fraction of the contract value, so price changes on the full value produce large percentage gains or losses on capital. Losses are not capped at the margin; further mark-to-market calls can arise.

Question 5

Price discovery as a function of derivatives markets refers to:

  1. The exchange fixing the price of the underlying at the start of each month
  2. Derivatives prices reflecting expectations about future spot prices
  3. SEBI announcing a fair value for every listed derivatives contract
  4. The issuer of the underlying deciding the price of its own futures
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Derivatives prices reflecting expectations about future spot prices

Active trading by many participants with different views makes derivatives prices reflect collective expectations about the underlying. Neither the exchange, SEBI nor the issuer sets these prices administratively.

Question 6

An arbitrageur notices that a stock's futures price is well below its theoretical fair value. Which trade locks in a riskless profit, ignoring costs?

  1. Buy the futures and sell the stock in the cash market
  2. Buy the stock in the cash market and sell the futures
  3. Buy both the stock and the futures together
  4. Sell the futures and wait for the price to fall
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Buy the futures and sell the stock in the cash market

When futures are cheap relative to fair value, the arbitrageur buys the underpriced futures and sells the relatively expensive spot, unwinding at expiry when prices converge. Buying spot and selling futures is the trade for overpriced futures.

Question 7

Which risk is substantially lower in exchange-traded derivatives than in OTC derivatives, because of the clearing corporation?

  1. Market price risk
  2. Basis risk
  3. Volatility risk
  4. Counterparty default risk
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Counterparty default risk

The clearing corporation becomes the counterparty to every trade through novation and guarantees settlement, so default risk falls sharply. Market, basis and volatility risks come from price behaviour and remain regardless of where the contract trades.

Question 8

A speculator differs from a hedger mainly because the speculator:

  1. Always holds both the derivative and the underlying asset
  2. Is prohibited from trading in exchange-traded derivatives
  3. Takes on price risk without an exposure to protect
  4. Uses only options and never trades futures contracts
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Takes on price risk without an exposure to protect

Speculators take on risk in the hope of profiting from price movements; they do not have an underlying exposure to protect. They may use futures or options and are an important source of liquidity in exchange markets.
