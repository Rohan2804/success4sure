Basics of Derivatives: NISM Series VIII revision notes

The first chapter of NISM Series VIII defines derivatives, traces their history in India and introduces the main contract types and participants. Questions are conceptual: identify the instrument from its description, distinguish OTC from exchange-traded contracts, and classify a trader as a hedger, speculator or arbitrageur.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. What is a derivative

A derivative is a contract whose value is derived from an underlying asset - a share, index, currency, commodity or interest rate. The Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956 includes derivatives within the definition of securities, which is why SEBI regulates exchange-traded equity derivatives in India.

2. Four basic types

Forwards are customised bilateral agreements to buy or sell later at a fixed price. Futures are standardised, exchange-traded forwards. Options give the buyer a right, without obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put). Swaps exchange cash-flow streams, such as fixed for floating interest payments.

3. OTC vs exchange-traded

OTC contracts are negotiated privately, so terms can be tailored, but each party faces the other's default risk and prices are less transparent. Exchange-traded contracts are standardised, cleared through a clearing corporation that guarantees settlement, and marked to market daily.

4. History in India

Index futures began trading in June 2000 on the Nifty and Sensex, following the L.C. Gupta Committee's recommendations. Index options followed in 2001, then stock options and stock futures. The J.R. Varma Committee laid down the risk containment framework of margins and limits.

5. Hedgers

Hedgers use derivatives to reduce an existing risk. A fund manager holding ₹10 crore of shares who fears a fall can sell index futures; losses on the portfolio are then offset by gains on the futures. Hedging gives up some upside in return for protection.

6. Speculators

Speculators take positions to profit from expected price moves, accepting risk. Derivatives give leverage: a margin of perhaps 15% to 20% controls the full contract value, so a 5% move can mean a 25% to 35% gain or loss on capital deployed.

7. Arbitrageurs

Arbitrageurs exploit price differences between markets. If a stock trades at ₹1,000 in the cash market and its one-month future at ₹1,030 when fair value is ₹1,008, the arbitrageur buys cash and sells futures, locking in the mispricing. Their activity keeps prices aligned.

8. Economic functions

Derivatives aid price discovery, transfer risk from those who want to shed it to those willing to bear it, increase market liquidity and reduce transaction costs compared with trading the underlying. They also encourage informed participation in the cash market.

9. Risks of derivatives

Leverage magnifies losses; OTC contracts carry counterparty risk; complex products can be mispriced or misunderstood. SEBI studies have found that most individual traders in equity F&O lose money, a point every distributor and investor should take seriously. Position sizing and stop-losses are essential.

Common traps in the exam

  • Treating a forward and a future as identical - futures are standardised, exchange-traded and marked to market daily; forwards are customised OTC deals.
  • Saying an option buyer has an obligation; only the seller has an obligation, the buyer has a right.
  • Classifying an investor who sells futures against an existing portfolio as a speculator - that is hedging.
  • Forgetting that index futures, not stock options, were the first equity derivative launched in India (June 2000).

Quick recap

A derivative's value comes from an underlying. Forwards are custom OTC, futures standardised on exchange, options give rights, swaps exchange cash flows. Hedgers reduce risk, speculators take it, arbitrageurs exploit mispricing. India started with index futures in 2000. Leverage cuts both ways.
