Understanding Index: NISM Series VIII revision notes

Index derivatives are the most actively traded equity derivatives in India, so Series VIII explains how an index is built. Questions test weighting methods, the free-float concept, index maintenance, and what makes a good index for derivatives, sometimes with a small calculation of index value change.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. What an index measures

An index tracks the value of a basket of stocks relative to a base period, showing how the market or a segment has moved. The Nifty 50 started at a base value of 1,000 in November 1995; the Sensex at 100 with 1978-79 as the base year.

2. Free-float market capitalisation

Most Indian indices use free-float market cap weighting: only shares available for trading are counted, excluding promoter holdings and other locked-in shares. A company with ₹1 lakh crore total market cap but 50% promoter holding contributes only about ₹50,000 crore to index weight.

3. Other weighting methods

Price-weighted indices (like the Dow Jones Industrial Average) give more weight to high-priced stocks. Equal-weighted indices give every stock the same weight, increasing the influence of smaller companies. Full market cap weighting ignores free float. Nifty and Sensex use neither; both are free-float weighted.

4. Index calculation

Index value = current free-float market value of constituents / base market value x base index value. The divisor is adjusted for corporate actions such as rights issues or constituent changes, so the index does not jump simply because the basket changed.

5. Index maintenance

Index providers periodically review constituents for eligibility - size, liquidity, free float and listing history. Stocks are replaced when they no longer qualify. Bonus issues and stock splits do not change market cap and need no divisor adjustment. Changes are announced in advance so index funds can rebalance.

6. Impact cost

Impact cost measures liquidity: the percentage difference between the actual execution price for a given order size and the ideal mid-price. Lower impact cost means a more liquid stock. Index inclusion criteria often require low impact cost so that the index is tradable.

7. Desirable index attributes

A good index for derivatives is well diversified, made of liquid stocks, has low impact cost, captures the market's behaviour and is professionally maintained. Diversification reduces manipulation risk and makes the index a good hedge for broad portfolios. A narrow sectoral index is less suitable as a hedge for a diversified portfolio.

8. Uses of an index

Indices serve as benchmarks for mutual funds, underlyings for index funds, ETFs and derivatives, and barometers of the economy. Index futures and options let investors hedge or take a view on the whole market without buying every stock. Index derivatives also avoid stock-specific risk.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Index value = (Current market value / Base market value) x Base index value
  • Free-float market cap = Share price x Shares outstanding x Investable weight factor

Common traps in the exam

  • Assuming a stock split or bonus issue changes a company's index weight - market cap is unchanged, so weight stays the same.
  • Confusing impact cost (a liquidity measure) with brokerage or transaction charges.
  • Thinking promoter holdings are included in free-float weights; they are excluded.
  • Believing a price-weighted index gives more weight to larger companies; it favours higher-priced shares.

Quick recap

Indian indices are free-float market cap weighted. Index = current value / base value x base index. The divisor adjusts for corporate actions; splits and bonuses need none. Impact cost measures liquidity - lower is better. Good derivative indices are diversified, liquid and well maintained.
