Accounting and Taxation: NISM Series VIII revision notes

This chapter covers how futures and options are accounted for and taxed in India. Questions are few but predictable: business income versus speculation, how STT applies, and how margins and premiums appear in the books. Tax rates and STT rates change, so learn the structure and check current rates for 2026.

Last updated 19 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Accounting for futures

As per ICAI's guidance, initial margin paid is shown as a current asset ('initial margin - equity derivatives'). Daily MTM receipts and payments go through a mark-to-market margin account, and the net profit or loss on settlement or squaring off is recognised in the profit and loss account.

2. Accounting for options

Premium paid by the buyer is shown as an asset until the option is exercised, squared off or expires. Premium received by the writer is shown as a liability until then. On expiry or squaring off, the difference is taken to the profit and loss account; open positions may need provisions for losses.

3. Business, not speculative, income

Trading in equity derivatives on a recognised stock exchange is excluded from 'speculative transaction' under the Income-tax law. Profits are therefore taxed as non-speculative business income at slab rates, and losses can be set off against other business income (not salary) and carried forward for eight years.

4. Securities Transaction Tax

STT applies to equity derivatives: on the sale of futures based on traded price, on the sale of options based on premium, and a higher rate on the intrinsic value of options exercised. STT rates have been raised in recent Budgets - check current rates with the income tax department as of 2026.

5. Turnover for tax audit

For F&O traders, turnover is generally computed as the sum of absolute profits and losses on trades (plus premium on options sold, as per ICAI guidance), not the notional contract value. Turnover determines tax audit applicability. Audit thresholds are set in the Act; check the current limits.

6. Expenses and deductions

Since F&O income is business income, related expenses - brokerage, internet, advisory subscriptions, depreciation on computers - can be claimed. STT paid is also allowed as a deduction when computing business income. Keeping clean records of each trade, contract note and expense makes computing business income and filing the return far easier at year end.

7. Disclosure in financial statements

Entities holding open derivative positions at the balance sheet date should disclose the number of contracts, underlying, long or short side and the margin balances. Investors and auditors use these disclosures to judge the size of derivative exposure relative to the business, since notional values can be large compared with the margins paid.

8. Hedging transactions

Derivatives used to hedge holdings in the same securities are also treated as non-speculative under the exclusion. Gains on the underlying shares, however, remain capital gains, so a hedger may have business income or loss on futures and capital gains on shares.

Common traps in the exam

  • Calling exchange-traded F&O profits 'speculative income' - they are non-speculative business income.
  • Using total contract value as turnover; the absolute sum of profits and losses is used.
  • Recording option premium paid as an immediate expense; it is carried as an asset until exercise, expiry or squaring off.
  • Assuming STT applies equally to buyers and sellers of options; it is mainly on the sell side and on exercise.

Quick recap

Initial margin is a current asset; MTM flows through a margin account. Option premium paid is an asset, received is a liability, until closure. Exchange-traded F&O income is non-speculative business income; losses set off against business income and carried forward eight years. STT applies on sale and exercise; rates change - check current ones.
