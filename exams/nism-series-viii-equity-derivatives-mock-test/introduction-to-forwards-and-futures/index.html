What this chapter covers

This chapter covers forward and futures contracts: their features, differences, payoff profiles and pricing. Learn the cost-of-carry model, the effect of dividends and interest on fair value, basis and convergence, contango and backwardation, mark-to-market settlement and the use of futures for hedging with beta. Practise numerical questions on fair value, implied interest rates, daily mark-to-market and hedge ratios, and be clear about how stock and index futures are settled in India.

Introduction to Forwards and Futures questions

Question 1

A stock trades at ₹1,000 and its two-month futures trade at ₹1,012. Ignoring dividends, what annualised simple interest rate is implied in the futures price?

  1. 7.2% per annum
  2. 1.2% per annum
  3. 6.0% per annum
  4. 14.4% per annum
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. 7.2% per annum

Implied two-month return = 12 ÷ 1,000 = 1.2%. Annualised simply, 1.2% × 12/2 = 7.2% per annum. 1.2% is the unannualised figure; 14.4% wrongly annualises by multiplying by 12.

Question 2

A trader is long futures on 250 shares bought at ₹820. Settlement prices are ₹832 on day 1 and ₹815 on day 2. What is the mark-to-market amount on day 2?

  1. Loss of ₹1,250
  2. Gain of ₹3,000
  3. Loss of ₹4,250
  4. Loss of ₹1,750
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Loss of ₹4,250

Day 2 MTM compares the day 2 settlement price with the day 1 settlement price: (815 − 832) × 250 = −₹4,250. The cumulative loss from the purchase price is ₹1,250, but that is not the day 2 MTM figure.

Question 3

Other things equal, if a stock is expected to pay a large dividend before the futures expiry, the fair futures price will be:

  1. Higher than it would be without the dividend
  2. Lower than it would be without the dividend
  3. Exactly equal to the spot price
  4. Unaffected, because dividends go to the futures buyer
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Lower than it would be without the dividend

In the cost-of-carry model, income from holding the underlying reduces the carry, so fair value = spot + interest − dividends. A futures buyer does not receive the dividend, so the futures price adjusts down.

Question 4

Which feature is found in forward contracts but not in exchange-traded futures?

  1. Daily mark-to-market settlement through a clearing corporation
  2. Standardised lot sizes and expiry dates
  3. Guarantee of performance by a clearing corporation
  4. Terms such as quantity and delivery date tailored to the two parties
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Terms such as quantity and delivery date tailored to the two parties

Forwards are private, customised agreements. Futures are standardised exchange contracts with daily mark-to-market and a clearing corporation guarantee, which is why futures carry far less counterparty risk.

Question 5

A portfolio manager holds equity worth ₹5 crore with beta 0.9 and wants to hedge fully using index futures. Each contract has a value of ₹15 lakh. Approximately how many contracts should she sell?

  1. 33 contracts
  2. 27 contracts
  3. 36 contracts
  4. 30 contracts
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. 30 contracts

Contracts = portfolio value × beta ÷ contract value = 5,00,00,000 × 0.9 ÷ 15,00,000 = 30. Ignoring beta gives about 33, which would over-hedge a portfolio that is less volatile than the index.

Question 6

At expiry of a futures contract, the futures price and spot price of the underlying:

  1. Diverge by the full cost of carry
  2. Converge, so the basis becomes approximately zero
  3. Remain apart by the amount of the initial margin
  4. Differ by the dividend expected next year
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Converge, so the basis becomes approximately zero

As time to expiry shrinks, the cost of carry falls to zero, so the futures price converges to the spot price and the basis disappears. Arbitrage ensures this convergence on the final settlement day.

Question 7

An investor holding a stock portfolio sells index futures equal to its beta-adjusted value. If the market falls 5%, the hedge is intended to:

  1. Offset portfolio losses with gains on the short futures
  2. Double the gain made on the portfolio
  3. Convert the whole portfolio into a risk-free bond paying 5%
  4. Eliminate company-specific risk in the stocks held
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Offset portfolio losses with gains on the short futures

A short index futures hedge gains when the market falls, offsetting the market-related loss on the portfolio. It removes systematic exposure only; stock-specific risk not explained by beta remains.

Question 8

A trader buys Nifty futures and holds them overnight. The next day, the settlement price falls. What happens to his account?

  1. Nothing happens until he closes the position
  2. His initial margin is refunded and the position is closed
  3. His account is debited with the mark-to-market loss for the day
  4. He receives the dividend on index stocks to offset the loss
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. His account is debited with the mark-to-market loss for the day

Futures positions are marked to market daily: losses are debited and gains credited based on the daily settlement price. The position remains open unless he squares it off or fails to meet margin requirements.
