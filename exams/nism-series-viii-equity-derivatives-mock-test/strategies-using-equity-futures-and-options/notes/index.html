Strategies using Equity Futures and Options: NISM Series VIII revision notes

This chapter applies futures and options to real situations: hedging portfolios, generating income, and expressing views on direction or volatility. Exam questions give strike prices and premiums and ask for maximum profit, maximum loss or break-even, or ask which strategy fits a view. Draw quick payoff tables to avoid errors.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Hedging with futures and beta

Number of futures contracts = portfolio value x beta / (futures price x lot size). A ₹2 crore portfolio with beta 1.2, index futures at 24,000 and an illustrative lot of 50 needs 2,00,00,000 x 1.2 / (24,000 x 50) = 20 contracts sold to hedge fully. Use the exchange's current lot size in practice.

2. Long stock plus short futures

An investor holding shares who sells the same stock's futures locks in the futures price; gains and losses offset. Beta-weighted index hedges protect against market risk but leave stock-specific risk, since the portfolio may not move exactly with the index.

3. Protective put

Buying a put on shares already held sets a floor: holding Nifty-linked investments and buying an at-the-money put means losses below the strike are offset, while upside remains open minus the premium paid. It works like insurance and costs the premium.

4. Covered call

Holding shares and selling an out-of-the-money call earns premium income but caps upside at the strike. Holding a stock at ₹1,000 and selling a ₹1,100 call for ₹25 yields ₹25 extra if the price stays below ₹1,100, but gains above ₹1,100 are given up.

5. Bull call spread

Buy a lower-strike call and sell a higher-strike call of the same expiry. Buying the 100 call at ₹6 and selling the 110 call at ₹2 costs ₹4 net. Maximum profit = 10 - 4 = ₹6; maximum loss = ₹4; break-even = 104. It suits a moderately bullish view.

6. Bear put spread

Buy a higher-strike put and sell a lower-strike put. It profits from a moderate fall, with both profit and loss capped. Maximum profit equals the difference between strikes minus net premium paid; maximum loss is the net premium. Buying the 100 put at ₹7 and selling the 90 put at ₹2 costs ₹5, giving a maximum profit of ₹5 and break-even at 95.

7. Long straddle and strangle

A long straddle buys a call and a put at the same strike; a long strangle buys an out-of-the-money call and put at different strikes. Both profit from big moves in either direction, for example before a major result or policy announcement. Strangles are cheaper but need a larger move.

8. Short straddle and strangle

Selling both options earns premium when the market stays range-bound, but losses can be very large if prices move sharply. Straddle break-evens are strike plus and minus the total premium received. A short strangle widens the profit range but collects less premium. Both strategies need disciplined risk management and higher margins, since writers face open-ended exposure on at least one side.

9. Butterfly and calendar spreads

A long call butterfly buys one lower and one higher strike call and sells two middle-strike calls, profiting if the price ends near the middle strike with limited risk. A calendar spread sells a near-month option or future and buys a far-month one, trading the time difference.

10. Arbitrage strategies

Cash-futures arbitrage buys in the cash market and sells futures when futures trade above fair value, earning risk-free carry - this is how arbitrage funds work. Reverse arbitrage sells cash and buys futures when futures are underpriced. Returns shrink as more arbitrageurs compete.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Hedge contracts = (Portfolio value x Beta) / (Futures price x Lot size)
  • Bull call spread: max profit = strike difference - net debit; max loss = net debit
  • Long straddle break-evens = Strike +/- total premium paid

Common traps in the exam

  • Ignoring beta when hedging - a high-beta portfolio needs more index futures than its rupee value suggests.
  • Thinking a covered call protects against falls; it only offsets the fall by the premium received.
  • Using a straddle when the view is directional; straddles profit from volatility, not a specific direction.
  • Calculating spread maximum profit without subtracting the net premium paid.

Quick recap

Hedge size = portfolio x beta / (futures price x lot). Protective put = insurance; covered call = income with capped upside. Bull call and bear put spreads limit both profit and loss. Long straddles and strangles bet on big moves; short ones bet on calm markets with large risk. Arbitrage keeps futures near fair value.
