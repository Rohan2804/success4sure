What this chapter covers

This chapter applies futures and options to trading and hedging. It covers directional trades, hedging with futures, protective puts and covered calls, spreads (bull, bear, calendar), straddles, strangles, butterflies and collars, and arbitrage strategies. Revise each strategy's market view, maximum profit, maximum loss and break-even points. Many exam questions ask you to choose a strategy for a given view on direction and volatility, or to compute payoffs, so practise drawing simple payoff tables.

Strategies using Equity Futures and Options questions

Question 1

A trader buys a ₹1,000 call at ₹30 and sells a ₹1,050 call at ₹12, same stock and expiry. What is his maximum profit per share?

  1. ₹50
  2. ₹18
  3. ₹32
  4. ₹42
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. ₹32

Net debit = 30 − 12 = ₹18. Maximum profit = difference in strikes − net debit = 50 − 18 = ₹32, earned if the stock ends at or above ₹1,050. ₹18 is the maximum loss, not the profit.

Question 2

A trader buys a ₹500 call at ₹20 and a ₹500 put at ₹18, same expiry. What are his break-even prices at expiry?

  1. ₹480 and ₹520
  2. ₹482 and ₹518
  3. ₹462 and ₹520
  4. ₹462 and ₹538
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. ₹462 and ₹538

Total premium = 20 + 18 = ₹38. Break-evens of a long straddle are strike ± total premium: 500 − 38 = ₹462 and 500 + 38 = ₹538. Using only one leg's premium gives the wrong figures.

Question 3

An investor expects a stock to fall modestly but wants to limit the cost of the position. Which strategy suits this view best?

  1. Long straddle
  2. Bear put spread
  3. Bull call spread
  4. Short strangle
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Bear put spread

A bear put spread (buy higher-strike put, sell lower-strike put) profits from a moderate fall at lower cost than a single put. A straddle bets on volatility, a bull spread on a rise, and a short strangle on a quiet market.

Question 4

A calendar spread using calls typically involves:

  1. Selling a near-month and buying a far-month call, same strike
  2. Buying and selling calls of different strikes in the same month
  3. Buying a call and a put of the same strike and month
  4. Selling calls on two different underlying stocks
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Selling a near-month and buying a far-month call, same strike

A calendar (time) spread uses the same strike but different expiries, aiming to profit from faster time decay in the near-month option. Different strikes in the same month form a vertical spread, not a calendar spread.

Question 5

An investor owns shares at ₹400, buys a ₹380 put for ₹8 and sells a ₹440 call for ₹8. Ignoring costs, what is his maximum loss per share?

  1. ₹20
  2. ₹28
  3. ₹12
  4. ₹8
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. ₹20

This zero-cost collar has a net premium of nil. If the stock falls below ₹380, the put lets him sell at ₹380, so maximum loss = 400 − 380 = ₹20. Gains are capped at ₹40 by the short call.

Question 6

Which strategy profits most if the underlying ends exactly at the middle strike at expiry?

  1. Long strangle
  2. Protective put
  3. Long butterfly spread
  4. Short futures
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Long butterfly spread

A long butterfly (buy one low strike, sell two middle strikes, buy one high strike) has its peak payoff at the middle strike. A long strangle needs a large move, a protective put protects against falls, and short futures profits from falls.

Question 7

A trader sells a ₹2,000 put and receives ₹35. He is effectively:

  1. Protecting a stock he already owns from a fall
  2. Betting on a large rise in the stock's implied volatility
  3. Locking in a sale price of ₹2,035 for his shares
  4. Ready to buy the stock at a net ₹1,965 if assigned
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Ready to buy the stock at a net ₹1,965 if assigned

If the stock falls below ₹2,000, the put writer must buy at ₹2,000, but the ₹35 premium lowers the effective cost to ₹1,965. Selling puts is a bullish-to-neutral, short-volatility position.

Question 8

Short selling a stock's futures while buying the stock in the cash market, when futures are overpriced, is known as:

  1. Reverse cash-and-carry arbitrage
  2. Cash-and-carry arbitrage
  3. Calendar spread trading
  4. Pair trading
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. Cash-and-carry arbitrage

In cash-and-carry arbitrage, the trader buys the spot, sells the expensive futures and holds until expiry, earning more than the financing cost. Reverse cash-and-carry is used when futures are cheap.
