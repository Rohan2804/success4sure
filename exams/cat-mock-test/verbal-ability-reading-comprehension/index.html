Verbal Ability & Reading Comprehension: CAT practice questions

15 free questions with answers and explanations on Verbal Ability & Reading Comprehension, from the CAT (Common Admission Test) syllabus.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this section covers

Verbal Ability and Reading Comprehension (VARC) is the first section of CAT. Most questions are based on reading passages of roughly 400 to 600 words on topics such as economics, science, philosophy and culture, testing main idea, inference, tone and the effect of new information. The remaining questions are verbal ability items, usually para-summary, para-jumbles and the odd sentence out, often answered by typing a number rather than choosing an option. Revise by reading varied long-form articles daily, summarising each paragraph in one line, and practising how to eliminate options that are too broad, too extreme or merely restate a detail.

Verbal Ability & Reading Comprehension questions

Question 1

Read the passage and answer the question. Before the railways, every town in Britain kept its own time, set by the sun at local noon. Bristol's clocks ran about ten minutes behind London's, and nobody minded, because few people travelled fast enough for the difference to matter. The railways changed this. A timetable listing departures in a dozen local times invited missed connections and, worse, collisions on single-track lines. Railway companies therefore adopted London time across their networks in the 1840s, and towns gradually followed, often reluctantly; some public clocks displayed two minute hands, one for local time and one for 'railway time'. Uniform time was not required by law until 1880. What looks today like a natural feature of the world was a commercial convenience that became a social habit long before it became a legal rule. The primary purpose of the passage is to:

  1. show that standard time began as a practical arrangement before it acquired legal force
  2. argue that the railways caused most of the collisions recorded in nineteenth-century Britain
  3. explain why Bristol resisted London time longer than other towns
  4. describe how clocks with two minute hands were engineered
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. show that standard time began as a practical arrangement before it acquired legal force

The passage traces uniform time from a railway convenience to a social habit and only later to law (1880). Collisions, Bristol and the dual-hand clocks are supporting details, not the main purpose.

Question 2

Read the passage and answer the question. Before the railways, every town in Britain kept its own time, set by the sun at local noon. Bristol's clocks ran about ten minutes behind London's, and nobody minded, because few people travelled fast enough for the difference to matter. The railways changed this. A timetable listing departures in a dozen local times invited missed connections and, worse, collisions on single-track lines. Railway companies therefore adopted London time across their networks in the 1840s, and towns gradually followed, often reluctantly; some public clocks displayed two minute hands, one for local time and one for 'railway time'. Uniform time was not required by law until 1880. What looks today like a natural feature of the world was a commercial convenience that became a social habit long before it became a legal rule. The reference to public clocks with two minute hands mainly serves to illustrate:

  1. that Bristol's local time ran ahead of London's
  2. a transition period when local and railway time existed side by side
  3. the technical superiority of railway clocks over town clocks
  4. the legal penalty for towns that kept local time
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. a transition period when local and railway time existed side by side

Two minute hands, one for local and one for railway time, show both systems in use at once while towns 'gradually followed, often reluctantly'. Bristol actually ran behind London, so the last option misreads the passage.

Question 3

Read the passage and answer the question. Before the railways, every town in Britain kept its own time, set by the sun at local noon. Bristol's clocks ran about ten minutes behind London's, and nobody minded, because few people travelled fast enough for the difference to matter. The railways changed this. A timetable listing departures in a dozen local times invited missed connections and, worse, collisions on single-track lines. Railway companies therefore adopted London time across their networks in the 1840s, and towns gradually followed, often reluctantly; some public clocks displayed two minute hands, one for local time and one for 'railway time'. Uniform time was not required by law until 1880. What looks today like a natural feature of the world was a commercial convenience that became a social habit long before it became a legal rule. Which of the following can be inferred from the passage?

  1. Most travellers before the railway era carried watches set to London time.
  2. Collisions on single-track lines stopped completely once London time was adopted by the railways.
  3. Before 1880, a British town could lawfully keep a time different from London's.
  4. Local time was abandoned because it was shown to be astronomically inaccurate.
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Before 1880, a British town could lawfully keep a time different from London's.

Since uniform time was not required by law until 1880, keeping local time earlier was lawful. The passage never claims collisions ended entirely, and local time was in fact astronomically accurate (set by the sun).

Question 4

Read the passage and answer the question. When employees must actively sign up for a pension plan, participation is often modest; when they are enrolled automatically and must actively opt out, participation rises sharply, even though the choice available to them is identical. Economists once explained such gaps by the cost of filling in forms, but the paperwork involved is usually trivial. A more persuasive account is that a default carries an implicit message: people read it as the option their employer, or the experts behind the plan, consider sensible. Inertia and procrastination then do the rest. This explains why defaults are powerful, and also why they can do harm. A default contribution rate set too low may anchor savers at that level for years, leaving them worse off than if they had been made to choose for themselves. According to the passage, the 'cost of filling in forms' explanation is weak because:

  1. employees rarely read the forms that their employers hand out at joining
  2. procrastination has no effect on pension decisions
  3. automatic enrolment removes any option of leaving the plan
  4. the paperwork involved is usually too small to account for the gap
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. the paperwork involved is usually too small to account for the gap

The author dismisses the form-cost explanation because 'the paperwork involved is usually trivial'. Automatic enrolment still allows opting out, and the passage says inertia and procrastination matter, so those options contradict it.

Question 5

Read the passage and answer the question. When employees must actively sign up for a pension plan, participation is often modest; when they are enrolled automatically and must actively opt out, participation rises sharply, even though the choice available to them is identical. Economists once explained such gaps by the cost of filling in forms, but the paperwork involved is usually trivial. A default contribution rate set too low may anchor savers at that level for years, leaving them worse off than if they had been made to choose for themselves. The author's attitude towards default options is best described as:

  1. neutral, because the passage never evaluates defaults at all
  2. appreciative of their power but alert to their risks
  3. dismissive, since defaults merely reflect the cost of paperwork
  4. wholly enthusiastic about automatic enrolment in every form
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. appreciative of their power but alert to their risks

The author says the account 'explains why defaults are powerful, and also why they can do harm', citing a low default rate that anchors savers. This balanced view rules out dismissive, wholly enthusiastic or neutral readings.

Question 6

Read the passage and answer the question. When employees must actively sign up for a pension plan, participation is often modest; when they are enrolled automatically and must actively opt out, participation rises sharply, even though the choice available to them is identical. Economists once explained such gaps by the cost of filling in forms, but the paperwork involved is usually trivial. A more persuasive account is that a default carries an implicit message: people read it as the option their employer, or the experts behind the plan, consider sensible. Inertia and procrastination then do the rest. This explains why defaults are powerful, and also why they can do harm. A default contribution rate set too low may anchor savers at that level for years, leaving them worse off than if they had been made to choose for themselves. Which of the following, if true, would most weaken the 'implicit message' explanation?

  1. Participation rises just as much when employees are told the default was picked at random.
  2. Employees who opt out of the plan tend to earn more than those who stay enrolled in it.
  3. Some firms set their default contribution rates higher than other firms do.
  4. Many employees say they trust their employer's advice on money matters.
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Participation rises just as much when employees are told the default was picked at random.

If a default known to be random works equally well, its power cannot come from people reading it as an expert endorsement. Trusting employer advice would strengthen the account; the other options are irrelevant to the mechanism.

Question 7

Choose the option that best captures the essence of the paragraph. Weather forecasts are often blamed when rain fails to arrive on a day marked '70 per cent chance of rain'. But such a forecast is not a promise; it means that on days with similar conditions, rain fell on about seven in ten. A forecaster whose 70 per cent days stay dry three times in ten is performing exactly as intended. Public frustration comes from reading a probability as a prediction about a single day.

  1. People trusted forecasts more when they were stated in plain words.
  2. Weather forecasts are unreliable, since rain falls on only seventy per cent of the days predicted.
  3. A probabilistic forecast should be judged over many similar days, not by whether it came true on one day.
  4. Forecasters should drop percentages and simply say whether it will rain.
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. A probabilistic forecast should be judged over many similar days, not by whether it came true on one day.

The paragraph's point is that a 70 per cent forecast is correct if it rains on about 70 per cent of such days, so it must be judged in aggregate. It does not call forecasts unreliable or propose dropping percentages.

Question 8

Choose the option that best captures the essence of the paragraph. Much of the world's software rests on open-source libraries maintained by a handful of unpaid volunteers. Companies that earn billions from products built on these libraries rarely contribute money or staff time to them. When a maintainer burns out or a critical flaw goes unnoticed, the damage spreads far beyond the project itself. The arrangement works only as long as goodwill holds.

  1. Open-source software is less secure than commercial software because volunteers write it.
  2. Companies should stop using open-source libraries in products that earn revenue.
  3. Most software flaws are caused by maintainers who are not paid.
  4. Widely used open-source software rests on a thin, unpaid base of maintainers, making it more fragile than it appears.
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Widely used open-source software rests on a thin, unpaid base of maintainers, making it more fragile than it appears.

The paragraph stresses dependence on a few unpaid maintainers and the wide damage if goodwill fails. It does not compare security with commercial software, prescribe abandoning open source, or blame most flaws on unpaid work.

Question 9

Choose the option that best captures the essence of the paragraph. A museum label once told visitors what an object was, where it came from and who made it. Increasingly, labels also explain how the object reached the museum: whether it was bought, gifted, or taken during war or colonial rule. Some curators see this as a distraction from the object itself; others argue that the journey is part of the object's meaning. Either way, the label has become a site of argument rather than a neutral caption.

  1. By addressing how objects were acquired, museum labels have turned from neutral captions into contested statements.
  2. Museums should return every object that was taken during war or colonial rule.
  3. Visitors spend more time reading labels than looking at objects.
  4. Curators now agree that an object's acquisition history matters more than the object itself.
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. By addressing how objects were acquired, museum labels have turned from neutral captions into contested statements.

The paragraph describes labels adding acquisition history and curators disagreeing about it, so labels have become contested. Curators are said to disagree, not agree, and the paragraph makes no recommendation about returning objects.

Question 10

The four sentences below, when properly sequenced, form a coherent paragraph. Choose the correct order. 1. This is why engineers have begun testing it as a substitute for steel reinforcement in low-cost buildings. 2. Bamboo is technically a grass, yet some species grow nearly a metre in a single day. 3. Its strength, too, is remarkable: weight for weight, certain species rival mild steel in tension. 4. Such rapid growth makes it one of the most renewable building materials available.

  1. 4231
  2. 2341
  3. 2413
  4. 2431
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. 2431

Sentence 2 introduces bamboo; 4 ('Such rapid growth') follows it. Sentence 3 adds a second quality with 'too', and 1 ('This is why') draws the conclusion from both renewability and strength. Hence 2-4-3-1.

Question 11

The four sentences below, when properly sequenced, form a coherent paragraph. Choose the correct order. 1. In them, merchants exchanged shipping news, and some became the seeds of insurance markets and stock exchanges. 2. Coffee houses spread rapidly through London in the late seventeenth century. 3. Unlike taverns, they offered a sober setting where strangers could talk for the price of a cup. 4. Lloyd's of London, for example, began in a coffee house run by Edward Lloyd.

  1. 2341
  2. 2134
  3. 2314
  4. 3214
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. 2314

Sentence 2 opens the topic; 3 describes what coffee houses offered ('they'); 1 ('In them') explains their commercial role; 4 gives Lloyd's as an example of an insurance market born there. Hence 2-3-1-4.

Question 12

The four sentences below, when properly sequenced, form a coherent paragraph. Choose the correct order. 1. In experiments, pigeons fitted with small magnets lost their way when released under overcast skies. 2. On sunny days, however, the same birds homed correctly, apparently by using the sun. 3. Homing pigeons appear to be able to sense the Earth's magnetic field. 4. They thus seem to carry more than one compass and use whichever is available.

  1. 3214
  2. 3124
  3. 3142
  4. 1324
Show answer & explanation

Answer: B. 3124

Sentence 3 states the claim; 1 gives the magnet experiment; 2 contrasts sunny days with 'however' and 'the same birds'; 4 concludes with 'thus'. Placing 2 before 1 would leave 'the same birds' without a referent.

Question 13

Four of the five sentences below can be arranged into a coherent paragraph. Identify the sentence that does NOT fit. 1. Fermentation was used to preserve food long before anyone understood the microbes responsible. 2. Yoghurt, idli batter and sauerkraut all depend on bacteria that turn sugars into acids. 3. The acidity they produce makes the food hostile to many organisms that cause spoilage. 4. Refrigerators became common in Indian middle-class homes only in the late twentieth century. 5. Ancient cooks thus harnessed microbiology without knowing it existed.

  1. Sentence 5
  2. Sentence 1
  3. Sentence 4
  4. Sentence 2
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Sentence 4

Sentences 1, 2, 3 and 5 form a chain on fermentation as unknowing use of microbes. Sentence 4 shifts to the spread of refrigerators, a different preservation story, and breaks that chain.

Question 14

Four of the five sentences below can be arranged into a coherent paragraph. Identify the sentence that does NOT fit. 1. Early chess programs relied on searching millions of positions using rules written by human experts. 2. Newer engines instead learn by playing millions of games against themselves. 3. Chess is believed to have originated in India as chaturanga around the sixth century. 4. Some of their moves, once dismissed as errors, are now studied closely by grandmasters. 5. The shift has changed not only how machines play but how humans understand the game.

  1. Sentence 1
  2. Sentence 4
  3. Sentence 5
  4. Sentence 3
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Sentence 3

Sentences 1, 2, 4 and 5 describe the shift from rule-based to self-taught engines and its effect on human play. The origin of chess in India is unrelated to that argument.

Question 15

Four of the five sentences below can be arranged into a coherent paragraph. Identify the sentence that does NOT fit. 1. Trials of a four-day working week have reported stable output alongside lower staff turnover. 2. Critics point out that most trials involved firms that volunteered, and so were likely to succeed. 3. Results from self-selected participants may not hold when a policy is applied across an economy. 4. Supporters respond that even self-selected trials show the idea works in some sectors. 5. Productivity growth in many economies slowed after the 2008 financial crisis.

  1. Sentence 5
  2. Sentence 2
  3. Sentence 4
  4. Sentence 3
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Sentence 5

Sentences 1-2-3-4 form a debate: trial results, a selection-bias critique, its implication, and the supporters' reply. The general post-2008 productivity slowdown does not belong to this exchange.
