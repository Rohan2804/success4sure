Investment Landscape: NISM Series V-A revision notes

This opening chapter of the NISM V-A workbook sets the stage: why households must invest rather than merely save, which asset classes exist in India, and what risks each carries. Questions are mostly conceptual, with a few quick calculations on real return, compounding and the effect of inflation. Expect scenario questions that ask which asset suits a described investor.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Saving versus investing

Saving is setting aside part of income; investing is deploying those savings so they earn a return. Money kept in a savings account at 3% while inflation runs at 5% is losing purchasing power every year, which is the core argument the workbook uses for investing.

2. Physical and financial assets

Physical (real) assets include land, a flat, gold jewellery and commodities. Financial assets are claims on an issuer: shares, bonds, bank deposits, mutual fund units and insurance policies. A rented flat is a real asset; a bank FD is a financial asset even though it feels equally 'safe'.

3. Equity as an asset class

Equity gives part-ownership of a company. Returns come from dividends and capital appreciation, both uncertain. Equity has the highest long-term growth potential among mainstream assets but also the highest short-term volatility, so it suits long horizons such as retirement 20 years away.

4. Debt as an asset class

Debt instruments (bonds, debentures, deposits, T-bills, commercial paper) promise interest and repayment of principal. Key risks are credit risk (issuer default) and interest rate risk (prices fall when rates rise). Government securities carry negligible credit risk but still carry interest rate risk.

5. Gold, real estate and alternatives

Gold is a hedge against currency weakness and crises and has no regular income. Real estate needs large ticket sizes, is illiquid and carries high transaction costs such as stamp duty. Alternatives include REITs, InvITs, AIFs and structured products, usually for more experienced investors.

6. Nominal and real return

Nominal return is what the instrument pays; real return adjusts for inflation. An FD at 7% with 5% inflation gives a real return of roughly 2%. Precisely, (1.07/1.05) - 1 = 1.9%. Exams usually accept the approximate subtraction. Negative real returns are common on savings accounts.

7. Main investment risks

Inflation risk erodes purchasing power; liquidity risk means you cannot sell quickly at a fair price; credit risk is default; interest rate risk hits bond prices; market risk affects all equities; reinvestment risk arises when coupons must be reinvested at lower rates.

8. Liquidity and marketability

Liquidity is how quickly an asset turns into cash without loss of value. A savings account is highly liquid; a flat may take months to sell and may need a price cut. An open-ended fund is liquid because the AMC redeems units at NAV.

9. Diversification

Spreading money across companies, sectors and asset classes reduces unsystematic (company-specific) risk. It cannot remove systematic risk such as a market-wide fall after an interest rate shock. Owning 30 stocks across sectors is diversified; 30 bank stocks is not. Asset-class diversification adds a further cushion.

10. Life cycle and goals

A young earner can take more equity risk because of a long horizon and steady income; a retiree needs regular income and capital safety. Financial planning links each goal - house, child's education, retirement - to an amount, a date and a suitable asset mix.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Real return (approx) = Nominal return - Inflation rate
  • Exact real return = (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) - 1
  • Future value = Present value x (1 + r)^n, where r is the annual rate and n the number of years

Common traps in the exam

  • Calling a bank FD or debt fund a 'real asset' - real assets are physical; every deposit, bond or unit is a financial asset.
  • Assuming government bonds have no risk: they are free of credit risk but their prices still fall when interest rates rise.
  • Mixing up diversification's effect: it reduces company-specific risk, never the market-wide systematic risk.
  • Forgetting that liquidity and safety differ: a listed small-cap share can be liquid yet highly risky.

Quick recap

Invest, don't just save, because inflation eats idle money. Real assets are physical; financial assets are claims. Equity grows most but swings most; debt carries credit and interest rate risk; gold hedges crises. Real return is roughly nominal minus inflation. Diversification cuts only unsystematic risk. Match the asset to the goal and the horizon.
