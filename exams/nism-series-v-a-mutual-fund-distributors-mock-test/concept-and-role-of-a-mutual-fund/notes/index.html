Concept and Role of a Mutual Fund: NISM Series V-A revision notes

This chapter explains what a mutual fund is, how units and NAV work, the main scheme types and SEBI's categorisation, and why funds suit retail investors. It is one of the most heavily tested chapters in V-A, with many direct questions on scheme categories and their minimum allocations, plus questions contrasting open-ended, closed-ended and interval schemes.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Pooling and units

A mutual fund pools money from many investors and invests it per a stated objective. Investors receive units; each unit represents a proportionate share of the scheme's net assets. If you hold 1% of the units you own 1% of the portfolio, gains and losses alike.

2. Benefits for small investors

Professional management, diversification with a small amount (SIPs can start at a few hundred rupees), liquidity in open-ended schemes, transparency through daily NAV and monthly portfolio disclosure, and SEBI regulation. A ₹500 SIP buys a slice of 50+ stocks. Each investor's holding is shown in a single statement.

3. Limitations

No control over the portfolio, costs through the expense ratio, returns that are not assured, and possible over-diversification. Investors also cannot tailor holdings for their own tax position. Funds are market-linked: NAV can fall below the purchase price. Exit loads and taxes can also reduce net returns.

4. Open-ended, closed-ended, interval

Open-ended schemes allow purchase and redemption on every business day at NAV-based prices. Closed-ended schemes have a fixed maturity; investors exit before maturity only by selling on the stock exchange where units are compulsorily listed. Interval schemes open for transactions only during specified intervals.

5. Active versus passive

Active funds try to beat a benchmark through stock selection and carry higher expense ratios. Passive funds - index funds and ETFs - replicate an index and aim for minimal tracking error, not outperformance. Lower cost is the main attraction of passive funds.

6. Equity categories

SEBI's 2017 categorisation defines large cap as the top 100 companies by full market capitalisation, mid cap as 101st to 250th and small cap as 251st onwards. A large cap fund holds at least 80% in large caps; mid cap and small cap funds hold at least 65% in their segment. Multi cap needs at least 25% each in large, mid and small caps; flexi cap needs at least 65% equity with no segment limits.

7. Debt categories

Debt categories are defined mainly by Macaulay duration or instrument type: overnight (1-day securities), liquid (up to 91 days maturity), ultra short, low, short, medium and long duration funds, plus money market, gilt, corporate bond, banking and PSU, credit risk and dynamic bond funds.

8. Hybrid and solution-oriented

Hybrid funds mix equity and debt: conservative, balanced or aggressive hybrid, dynamic asset allocation, multi-asset (at least three asset classes) and arbitrage funds. Solution-oriented funds (retirement, children's) carry a lock-in of at least five years. ELSS has a three-year lock-in.

9. Industry growth drivers

AMFI's investor awareness campaigns, the spread of SIPs, digital KYC and B-30 (beyond top 30 cities) incentives have pushed retail participation. Monthly SIP flows and folio counts are published by AMFI; quote them only from AMFI's latest data. Growth itself is not a reason to pick any particular scheme.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Large cap = top 100; Mid cap = 101-250; Small cap = 251 onwards (by full market capitalisation, list published by AMFI every six months)

Common traps in the exam

  • Confusing flexi cap (65% equity, no cap-segment minimum) with multi cap (minimum 25% each in large, mid and small cap).
  • Thinking investors can redeem a closed-ended scheme with the AMC before maturity - they must sell on the exchange.
  • Believing a passive fund tries to beat its index; it only aims to match it with low tracking error.
  • Assuming unit holders are lenders to the fund; they are part-owners who bear all gains and losses.

Quick recap

A mutual fund pools money and issues units; NAV reflects each unit's share. Open-ended means daily liquidity; closed-ended means exchange listing for exit. Remember the cap ranks 100/250 and the minimums: large cap 80%, mid and small 65%, multi cap 25% each, flexi cap 65% equity. ELSS locks in for three years; solution-oriented funds for five.
