Risk, Return and Performance of Funds: NISM Series V-A revision notes

This chapter explains where fund returns come from, which risks drive the ups and downs of equity, debt and hybrid funds, and how risk is measured. Questions combine concepts (what affects a gilt fund's NAV?) with simple calculations of absolute return, CAGR and the impact of interest rate changes.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Sources of return

Returns come from income (dividends, interest) and capital gains or losses. In the growth option both stay in the NAV; under IDCW part is paid out. Total return accounts for both price change and payouts. Comparing NAVs alone misses IDCW payouts.

2. Equity fund drivers

Equity NAVs move with company earnings, valuations (P/E multiples), economic growth, interest rates, liquidity flows and sentiment. Fundamental analysis studies a company's business and financials; technical analysis studies price and volume patterns. Over long periods, earnings growth explains most equity returns, while short-term moves are dominated by sentiment and flows.

3. Growth and value styles

Growth investing buys companies with fast-rising earnings, often at high P/E. Value investing buys companies trading below estimated intrinsic value. Style matters because one style can underperform for years. Value funds often do well after market crashes, while growth funds tend to lead in strong bull phases; mixing styles smooths results.

4. Debt fund drivers

Debt fund returns come from accrual (interest earned) and mark-to-market changes. When yields fall, bond prices and NAVs rise; when yields rise, NAVs fall. Longer duration means bigger price swings. When the RBI cuts the repo rate and yields fall, a long duration gilt fund can deliver a sharp short-term gain, and the reverse when rates rise.

5. Modified duration

Modified duration estimates the percentage price change for a 1% change in yield. A fund with modified duration of 5 will lose about 5% if yields rise by 1% (100 basis points). Liquid funds have very low duration and therefore low interest rate risk.

6. Credit risk

Lower-rated bonds pay higher yields but may default or be downgraded. A downgrade lowers the bond's price. Credit risk funds deliberately hold lower-rated papers; side-pocketing can separate a defaulted security from the main portfolio. Investors should check portfolio ratings in the monthly disclosure.

7. Standard deviation

Standard deviation measures total volatility of returns around the average. A fund with an average return of 12% and standard deviation of 18% swings much more than one with 12% and 8%. It assumes returns are roughly normally distributed and treats upside and downside swings alike, which is why some analysts prefer downside measures.

8. Beta

Beta measures sensitivity to the market. A beta of 1.2 means the fund tends to rise or fall about 12% when the market moves 10%. Beta captures only systematic risk, so it is meaningful for diversified portfolios. Low-beta funds suit cautious investors.

9. Hybrid and other funds

Hybrid fund returns depend on the equity-debt mix. Gold funds follow international gold prices and the rupee-dollar rate. International funds add currency risk: a falling rupee boosts returns from overseas assets. An aggressive hybrid fund with 75% equity will behave much like an equity fund, while a conservative hybrid with 20% equity behaves more like a debt fund.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Absolute return = (Ending NAV - Beginning NAV) / Beginning NAV x 100
  • CAGR = (Ending value / Beginning value)^(1/years) - 1
  • Approx price change of bond fund = - Modified duration x change in yield

Common traps in the exam

  • Saying bond prices rise when interest rates rise - the relationship is inverse.
  • Using absolute return for periods longer than a year; compare long periods using CAGR.
  • Treating standard deviation and beta as the same - SD is total risk, beta is market-related risk.
  • Assuming liquid funds carry no risk at all; they have low but not zero credit and interest rate risk.

Quick recap

Returns = income + capital gain. Equity NAVs follow earnings and valuations; debt NAVs move inversely to yields, more so for longer duration. Modified duration x yield change approximates the price change. Credit risk rises as ratings fall. SD measures total volatility, beta measures market sensitivity. Use CAGR beyond one year.
