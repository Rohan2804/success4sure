Mutual Fund Scheme Selection: NISM Series V-A revision notes

The final chapter brings everything together: how a distributor helps an investor choose a category, a scheme within it, and the right plan and option. Questions are scenario-based - an investor's age, goal and risk appetite are described and you must pick a suitable product or reject an unsuitable one.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Start with the investor

Selection begins with risk profiling: age, income stability, dependants, existing assets, investment horizon and comfort with losses. Suitability is the distributor's duty; an unsuitable sale is mis-selling even if the product is good. The profile should be recorded and refreshed when life events such as marriage, a child or retirement change the investor's situation.

2. Match horizon to category

Money needed within months suits overnight, liquid or money market funds. A three-to-five-year goal may suit short duration debt or conservative hybrid funds. Goals seven or more years away can carry meaningful equity exposure. Equity needs time to recover from falls, which is why short-term money should not be parked in equity funds however attractive recent returns look.

3. Asset allocation first

Decide the equity-debt-gold mix before picking schemes. For a 30-year-old saving for retirement, a high equity share is reasonable; for a 62-year-old needing monthly income, mostly debt with some equity for inflation protection is more typical. Studies of portfolios show allocation explains far more of the variation in outcomes than scheme choice.

4. Selecting within a category

Compare schemes on consistency of rolling returns, risk-adjusted measures, expense ratio, portfolio quality, fund manager tenure and AUM. For debt funds, check average maturity, duration and credit quality of holdings. A scheme with steady second-quartile performance across market cycles is often a better choice than one that alternates between top and bottom.

5. Active or passive

In segments where active managers struggle to beat the index after costs, such as large caps, low-cost index funds are a strong option. Active funds may add value in less efficient segments, but there is no assurance. Cost differences compound strongly over long horizons.

6. Plan and option choice

Direct plans suit investors who can choose and monitor schemes themselves. Regular plans include the distributor's service cost. The growth option suits long-term wealth building; IDCW suits those wanting periodic cash, bearing in mind payouts are not assured. IDCW payouts are taxed at slab rates, so growth plus SWP may be more tax-efficient for some investors.

7. Avoid common errors

Chasing last year's top performer, holding too many overlapping schemes, ignoring exit loads and taxes on switching, and stopping SIPs during market falls all hurt outcomes. Review the portfolio at least annually and rebalance. Distributors add the most value by keeping investors disciplined through volatile periods rather than by picking star funds.

8. Model portfolios

Distributors often build model portfolios by life stage - for example, aggressive for young earners, moderate for mid-career families and conservative for retirees. These are starting points and must be customised to the individual. Two investors of the same age may need very different portfolios if one has stable government employment and the other runs a seasonal business.

Common traps in the exam

  • Recommending a sectoral or small cap fund to a retiree needing capital safety because it 'performed best' recently.
  • Selecting a scheme before deciding the asset allocation - allocation drives most of the portfolio's risk and return.
  • Assuming IDCW payouts are regular income guaranteed by the fund; they depend on distributable surplus.
  • Owning several schemes of the same category, which adds overlap rather than diversification.

Quick recap

Profile the investor, then set asset allocation, then pick the category, then the scheme, then plan and option. Short horizons need liquid or short debt; long horizons can hold equity. Judge schemes on consistency, risk-adjusted returns, costs and portfolio quality. Avoid chasing past winners and review annually.
