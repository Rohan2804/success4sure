Legal and Regulatory Framework: NISM Series V-A revision notes

This chapter covers who regulates mutual funds, the investment restrictions that keep schemes diversified, AMFI's codes for AMCs and distributors, and the rights and grievance channels available to investors. Questions tend to be direct and rule-based, so memorise the escalation path for complaints and the basic investment limits.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Role of SEBI

SEBI registers and regulates mutual funds under the SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, framed under the SEBI Act, 1992. It approves fund structures, lays down investment and disclosure rules, and inspects AMCs. RBI regulates money and bank-related aspects, and the stock exchanges regulate listed units, but SEBI is the primary regulator.

2. Investment restrictions

SEBI limits concentration: a scheme generally cannot invest more than 10% of its NAV in equity shares of a single company (index funds and sector funds follow their own rules), and the fund as a whole cannot hold more than 10% of a company's voting capital. Debt exposure to a single issuer is capped based on rating. Check current limits with SEBI circulars.

3. AMFI Code of Ethics

The AMFI Code of Ethics (ACE) sets standards of integrity, due diligence, disclosure and professional conduct for AMCs. It covers fair treatment of all investors, avoiding conflicts of interest and not making misleading statements. AMCs are expected to treat a new investor and a large institution with the same fairness, and to keep investor information confidential.

4. Code of conduct for distributors

Distributors must follow AMFI's code: disclose commissions, recommend schemes suitable to the investor, avoid churning, never promise returns, and not rebate commissions. Breaches can lead to ARN suspension or cancellation. They must also keep records of client communication, update KYD details and avoid misrepresenting past performance or the risks of any scheme.

5. Investor rights

Unit holders have a proportional right to the scheme's assets, the right to receive redemption proceeds within three working days for most schemes, to receive account statements and annual reports, to inspect key documents and to exit without load if fundamental attributes change.

6. Investor obligations

Investors must read the scheme documents, complete KYC, provide correct details, keep records and update the folio when address, bank account or nominee changes. The workbook stresses that responsibility for investment decisions remains with the investor. Signing an application means the investor accepts the scheme's terms.

7. Complaint escalation

First approach the AMC or RTA (many AMCs have an investor relations officer). If unresolved, escalate to SEBI through SCORES, the online complaints portal. After that the investor may use the SMART ODR online dispute resolution platform for conciliation and arbitration.

8. Due diligence by AMCs

AMCs must carry out due diligence on distributors - checking for proper registration, business volumes and conduct - and can stop paying commissions to distributors who breach norms. The checks include the distributor's track record, any history of complaints, the number of clients served and whether its advisory and distribution roles are kept separate.

Common traps in the exam

  • Believing complaints go straight to SEBI; the AMC or RTA must be approached first, then SCORES.
  • Treating AMFI's code as optional guidance; breaching it can lead to cancellation of a distributor's ARN.
  • Saying unit holders can approve or veto every portfolio decision; they only get special rights on fundamental attribute changes.
  • Assuming RBI is the primary mutual fund regulator - it is SEBI.

Quick recap

SEBI regulates; AMFI sets codes and registers distributors. Schemes face concentration limits such as 10% of NAV in one company's shares. Distributors must disclose commission, sell only suitable products and never promise returns. Complaints go AMC/RTA first, then SEBI SCORES, then SMART ODR. Investors may exit without load on fundamental attribute changes.
