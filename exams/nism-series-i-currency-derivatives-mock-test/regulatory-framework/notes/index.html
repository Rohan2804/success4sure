Regulatory Framework: NISM Series I revision notes

Currency derivatives sit between two regulators: RBI, which governs foreign exchange, and SEBI, which governs exchanges. This chapter explains the legal basis, who regulates what and who may participate. Questions are few but predictable, mostly about regulatory roles and the laws involved.

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Joint regulation

Exchange-traded currency derivatives fall under the joint purview of RBI and SEBI. RBI decides the products and participant eligibility because they involve foreign exchange; SEBI regulates the exchanges, clearing corporations, members and trading practices. A joint RBI-SEBI standing technical committee coordinates policy.

2. Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999

RBI's authority over who can participate in currency derivatives and on what terms flows from FEMA, which replaced the older FERA in 2000. FEMA governs all foreign exchange transactions, distinguishing current account from capital account transactions. FERA treated violations as criminal; FEMA made them civil.

3. Securities laws

The Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956 and the SEBI Act, 1992 govern the exchanges on which currency derivatives trade, the conduct of brokers and the clearing process. Currency derivatives are treated as securities for the purpose of exchange trading. Exchange bye-laws add operational detail.

4. History of the market

USD/INR futures launched on Indian exchanges in August 2008, followed by EUR/INR, GBP/INR and JPY/INR futures in 2010 and USD/INR options in October 2010. Cross-currency pairs such as EUR/USD were added in 2018. Each new product followed a joint RBI-SEBI decision, and the range of pairs and expiries has widened gradually as liquidity grew.

5. Participants

Resident individuals, companies, banks and other entities may participate subject to RBI's rules. Foreign portfolio investors and NRIs can participate within the limits and purposes RBI specifies. Banks act both as members and as clients, under RBI's prudential norms. Each category has its own position limits.

6. Underlying exposure rule

Under RBI's directions, users of exchange-traded currency derivatives should have an underlying exposure to currency risk. Positions up to a specified amount can be taken without documentary evidence, but the responsibility to have the exposure remains with the user. Check current RBI limits as of 2026.

7. Product approvals

Exchanges cannot launch a new currency pair, contract type or expiry pattern on their own. New products and significant changes to contract specifications need SEBI approval, given in consultation with RBI because the products involve foreign exchange. This is why changes such as cross-currency pairs or weekly options arrive through joint circulars.

8. Member eligibility

Trading and clearing members in the currency segment must meet SEBI's net worth and infrastructure requirements and employ staff certified in NISM Series I. Banks need RBI approval to become trading or clearing members. Certification must be renewed before it expires to keep dealing with clients.

Common traps in the exam

  • Naming SEBI alone as the regulator - RBI and SEBI jointly regulate exchange-traded currency derivatives.
  • Attributing RBI's powers to the SEBI Act; they flow from FEMA, 1999.
  • Thinking FERA still governs forex - FEMA replaced it.
  • Assuming any participant may take unlimited speculative positions; RBI expects an underlying exposure.

Quick recap

RBI (under FEMA, 1999) decides products and participants; SEBI (under SCRA and the SEBI Act) regulates exchanges and members. A joint committee coordinates. USD/INR futures began in 2008, options in 2010, cross pairs in 2018. Members need net worth and NISM Series I-certified staff. Users should have underlying exposure.
