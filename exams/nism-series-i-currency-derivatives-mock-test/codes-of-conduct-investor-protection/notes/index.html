Codes of Conduct & Investor Protection: NISM Series I revision notes

The final chapter covers how brokers must treat clients in the currency segment and what protection investors have. Questions are direct: which document must be given before trading, where to complain and what the Investor Protection Fund covers. Read carefully - several options often sound similar.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Code of conduct for brokers

SEBI's stock broker regulations require brokers to act with integrity, due skill and care, avoid manipulative practices, maintain records, execute orders as instructed and issue contract notes on time. Brokers must not encourage excessive trading or make misleading claims about profits in currency trading.

2. Client onboarding

Before a client trades currency derivatives, the broker completes KYC through a KRA, registers the UCC with PAN, and provides the Risk Disclosure Document along with the rights and obligations document and any policies on margin and square-off. The client signs acknowledgements.

3. Risk disclosure

The Risk Disclosure Document explains leverage, the possibility of losing more than the initial margin, margin calls, the risk of illiquid contracts and the effect of exchange-rate volatility. It is meant to ensure informed consent rather than to discourage trading.

4. Handling client funds

Client money and collateral must be kept separate from the broker's own funds. Brokers must settle client accounts periodically, upload client-wise collateral data to the exchange, and send statements so clients can verify balances. Unused client funds must be returned as per SEBI's running-account settlement rules.

5. SCORES

SCORES is SEBI's online platform where investors lodge complaints against listed companies and market intermediaries, including brokers. Complaints are forwarded to the entity concerned and tracked to resolution, with escalation if the response is unsatisfactory. Brokers must respond within the timelines SEBI prescribes.

6. Escalation and SMART ODR

A client should first complain to the broker, then to the exchange's investor grievance cell or SCORES. If unresolved, the dispute can go to the SMART ODR platform for online conciliation and, if needed, arbitration. Each stage has defined response timelines.

7. Guarding against unauthorised trades

Exchanges send clients SMS and email alerts for trades executed in their accounts, and depositories and brokers send periodic statements. Clients should check these promptly and raise any unauthorised trade with the broker in writing at once, since delayed objections are harder to establish later.

8. Investor Protection Fund

Each exchange maintains an Investor Protection Fund to compensate investors whose claims against a defaulting broker are admitted, up to specified limits. It does not compensate losses from market movements or from the client's own trading decisions. Claims must be filed within the period the exchange notifies.

Common traps in the exam

  • Thinking the IPF covers trading losses - it covers admitted claims against defaulting brokers only.
  • Skipping the broker and exchange and approaching SMART ODR first.
  • Assuming the Risk Disclosure Document is optional for experienced clients; it is mandatory for all.
  • Confusing SCORES (SEBI complaint portal) with the Settlement Guarantee Fund.

Quick recap

Brokers must act fairly, keep records, issue timely contract notes and segregate client funds. Every client gets KYC, UCC registration, the Risk Disclosure Document and rights and obligations before trading. Complaints: broker, then exchange or SCORES, then SMART ODR. IPF pays admitted claims against defaulting brokers, not market losses.
