Introduction to Currency Markets: NISM Series I revision notes

The first chapter of NISM Series I builds the vocabulary of foreign exchange: how currency pairs are quoted, how banks make two-way prices, how cross rates are derived and how India's exchange rate regime evolved. Questions are a mix of definitions and quick calculations, so practise reading quotes carefully.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Base and quote currency

In the quote USD/INR = 84.10, USD is the base currency and INR is the quote (or price) currency: one US dollar costs ₹84.10. If the quote rises to 84.50, the dollar has appreciated and the rupee has depreciated. Always identify which currency is being priced before interpreting a move.

2. Direct and indirect quotes

A direct quote states the price of one unit of foreign currency in domestic currency, such as EUR/INR = 91.20 for an Indian resident. An indirect quote states how much foreign currency one unit of domestic currency buys, such as 1 INR = 0.01096 EUR. India uses direct quotes in practice.

3. Bid, ask and spread

A bank quoting USD/INR 83.40/83.45 buys dollars at 83.40 (bid) and sells at 83.45 (ask). A customer buying dollars pays the higher ask; one selling dollars receives the lower bid. The 5 paise spread is the dealer's margin and widens in volatile or illiquid conditions.

4. Cross rates

A cross rate is derived from two quotes involving a common currency. If EUR/USD is 1.1000 and USD/INR is 84.00, EUR/INR = 1.10 x 84.00 = 92.40. Cross rates let traders price pairs that are not directly quoted and spot arbitrage when quotes are inconsistent.

5. Settlement dates

A spot deal settles two business days after the trade date (T+2). Cash or value-today deals settle the same day; tom or value-tomorrow deals settle the next business day. Any settlement beyond spot is a forward. Holidays in either currency's centre push settlement out.

6. Market structure

The global forex market is decentralised and OTC, run mainly by large banks across time zones, with the US dollar on one side of most trades. In India, authorised dealer banks quote to customers, the interbank market sets rates, and exchanges offer standardised currency futures and options.

7. Exchange rate regimes

Under a fixed regime the central bank pegs the currency; under a free float, markets set the rate. India moved from a basket peg to the Liberalised Exchange Rate Management System in 1992 and a unified market-determined rate in 1993. Today the rupee is a managed float, with RBI intervening to curb volatility.

8. Convertibility

The rupee became fully convertible on the current account in 1994, meaning trade, travel and remittance payments are freely allowed. The capital account remains partially convertible, with limits on overseas investment by residents and on foreign borrowing, managed by RBI under FEMA.

9. What drives exchange rates

Inflation differentials, interest rate differentials, current account balance, capital flows such as FPI investment, crude oil prices and global risk sentiment all influence USD/INR. A rise in crude prices widens India's import bill and tends to weaken the rupee. RBI intervention can smooth these moves.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Cross rate A/C = A/B x B/C (e.g. EUR/INR = EUR/USD x USD/INR)
  • Spread = Ask - Bid

Common traps in the exam

  • Mixing up base and quote currency - in USD/INR, USD is the base; the number is rupees per dollar.
  • Thinking a customer buying dollars pays the bid; customers pay the higher ask.
  • Assuming spot settlement is the same day - spot is T+2; same-day is 'cash'.
  • Saying a rise in USD/INR means the rupee strengthened; it means the rupee weakened.

Quick recap

In USD/INR, USD is base, INR is quote. India uses direct quotes. Customers buy at ask, sell at bid. Cross rates multiply through a common currency. Spot = T+2, tom = T+1, cash = T+0. The rupee is a managed float, current-account convertible since 1994, capital account partly convertible.
