Exchange Traded Currency Options: NISM Series I revision notes

Currency options are tested with many small calculations. You must know the contract features of USD/INR options on Indian exchanges, compute payoffs and break-evens on a per-lot basis, identify moneyness and split premium into intrinsic and time value. A few questions also cover Greeks and margins.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Contract features

USD/INR options on Indian exchanges are European style, exercisable only on expiry, premium-settled in rupees and cash settled at the RBI/FBIL reference rate. The lot is USD 1,000. Options on EUR/INR, GBP/INR and JPY/INR are also listed, along with weekly USD/INR expiries.

2. Call payoff per lot

A trader buys one USD/INR 84.00 call for ₹0.30. If settlement is 84.60, payoff = (84.60 - 84.00 - 0.30) x 1,000 = ₹300 profit. If settlement is at or below 84.00, the call expires worthless and the loss is the ₹300 premium. Break-even is 84.30.

3. Put payoff

Buying an 83.50 put for ₹0.20 gives a break-even of 83.30 (strike minus premium). If the rupee strengthens and USD/INR settles at 83.00, profit is (83.50 - 83.00 - 0.20) x 1,000 = ₹300. Above 83.50, the put lapses. Maximum loss is the ₹200 premium.

4. Buyer and seller risk

The buyer of a currency call or put can lose at most the premium. The seller receives the premium but faces large losses if the rate moves against them, which is why sellers must deposit margin with the clearing corporation while buyers pay only the premium.

5. Moneyness

For a call, spot above strike means in-the-money; for a put, spot below strike. With USD/INR spot at 84.20, an 84.50 put is in-the-money by 0.30 and an 84.50 call is out-of-the-money. At-the-money options have strike close to spot. Only in-the-money options have intrinsic value.

6. Intrinsic and time value

A USD/INR 83.00 call trading at ₹1.40 with spot at 84.00 has intrinsic value 1.00 and time value 0.40. Time value reflects the chance of further favourable moves and decays to zero at expiry. An out-of-the-money option, such as an 85.00 call with spot at 84.00, has zero intrinsic value, so its whole premium is time value.

7. What moves premiums

Premiums rise with higher volatility and longer time to expiry. A higher spot raises call premiums and lowers put premiums. The interest rate differential between INR and USD also affects premiums through the forward rate, as in the Garman-Kohlhagen model, a currency version of Black-Scholes.

8. Delta and other Greeks

Delta measures the change in premium for a small change in the underlying exchange rate; an at-the-money call has delta around 0.5. Gamma measures the change in delta, theta the time decay, vega sensitivity to volatility, and rho to interest rates.

9. Hedging with options

An importer worried about a weaker rupee can buy USD/INR calls to cap the cost of dollars while benefiting if the rupee strengthens. An exporter can buy puts to set a floor on receivable value. The premium is the cost of keeping upside.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Call break-even = Strike + Premium; Put break-even = Strike - Premium
  • Option P&L per lot = Payoff per USD x 1,000
  • Time value = Premium - Intrinsic value

Common traps in the exam

  • Treating USD/INR options as American style - they are European, exercisable only at expiry.
  • Saying option buyers must post margin; only sellers do, buyers pay premium.
  • Calling a put in-the-money when spot is above strike - for puts, spot must be below strike.
  • Forgetting to multiply by the USD 1,000 lot when asked for rupee profit.

Quick recap

USD/INR options: European, lot USD 1,000, premium in rupees, cash settled at the reference rate. Call break-even = strike + premium; put = strike - premium. Buyers risk only premium; sellers post margin. Premium = intrinsic + time value. Volatility and time raise premiums. Importers buy calls, exporters buy puts.
