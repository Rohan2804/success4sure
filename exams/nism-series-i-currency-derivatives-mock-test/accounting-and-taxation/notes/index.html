Accounting and Taxation: NISM Series I revision notes

This short chapter covers how currency futures and options are recorded in the books and how profits are taxed. The key exam points are the margin and MTM accounting entries, the absence of STT, and the classification of profits as non-speculative business income. Tax rules can change, so check the income tax department's current position.

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Margin accounting

Initial margin paid to the broker or clearing corporation is shown as a current asset, not an expense. If margin is given as a bank guarantee or securities rather than cash, it is disclosed rather than recorded as a payment. Any margin shortfall is a liability.

2. MTM accounting

Daily MTM payments and receipts are recorded in a mark-to-market account. On final settlement or squaring off, the net balance is transferred to profit and loss. For open positions at year end, the accumulated MTM reflects unrealised gain or loss and prudence may require providing for losses.

3. Options premium

The buyer records premium paid as an asset until expiry, exercise or squaring off; the seller records premium received as a liability. On closure, the difference is recognised as profit or loss. Hedge accounting under Ind AS 109 applies to companies designating hedges.

4. Hedge accounting basics

Companies hedging foreign currency exposure can apply hedge accounting so that gains and losses on the derivative and the hedged item are recognised together. This avoids profit swings caused purely by marking derivatives to market while the underlying exposure is not.

5. No STT or CTT

Securities Transaction Tax applies to exchange-traded equity derivatives and Commodity Transaction Tax to commodity derivatives, but neither applies to exchange-traded currency derivatives. Trading costs therefore consist of brokerage, exchange charges, stamp duty, GST and SEBI fees. This makes currency trading costs comparatively low.

6. Tax treatment of profits

Profits from trading currency futures and options on a recognised stock exchange are generally treated as non-speculative business income, under the same exclusion that covers eligible derivatives. Losses can be set off against other business income and carried forward. Check the current Income-tax law before relying on this.

7. Hedgers versus traders

For a company that uses currency futures to hedge genuine export or import exposure, the derivative gain or loss is part of its business results and is usually recognised alongside the exchange difference on the hedged item. For an individual trader, the same contracts produce trading income or loss computed contract by contract.

8. Expenses and records

Traders can claim business expenses such as brokerage and data costs. Turnover for audit purposes is usually the sum of absolute profits and losses. Keeping contract notes and ledger statements makes reconciliation and filing simpler. Tax audit thresholds should be checked each year.

Common traps in the exam

  • Assuming STT applies to currency derivatives as it does to equity F&O - it does not.
  • Recording initial margin as an expense; it is a current asset.
  • Calling exchange-traded currency derivative profits speculative income; they are generally non-speculative business income.
  • Booking option premium paid directly to profit and loss on the day of purchase.

Quick recap

Initial margin is a current asset; MTM flows through an MTM account and goes to P&L on closure. Option premium paid is an asset, received is a liability, until closure. Hedge accounting matches derivative and exposure. No STT or CTT on currency derivatives. Profits are generally non-speculative business income.
