Foreign Exchange Derivatives: NISM Series I revision notes

This chapter introduces the family of foreign exchange derivatives - forwards, futures, options and swaps - and the pricing principle that ties forward rates to interest rates. Questions test interest rate parity calculations, forward premiums, the distinctions between OTC and exchange-traded contracts, and who hedges what.

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Why hedge currency risk

Exporters lose when the rupee strengthens before they convert their dollar receipts; importers lose when it weakens before they pay. Students paying overseas fees and companies with foreign loans face similar risks. Derivatives let them lock in or cap the exchange rate.

2. OTC forwards

A forward contract with a bank fixes the exchange rate for a future date and is customised in amount and maturity. Forwards carry counterparty risk and usually need an underlying exposure under RBI rules. They are the most widely used hedging tool for Indian corporates.

3. Futures versus forwards

Exchange-traded currency futures are standardised in lot size and expiry, guaranteed by a clearing corporation, marked to market daily and accessible to smaller participants. Forwards are bilateral, customised and settled only at maturity, without daily margin. Futures are also more transparent on price.

4. Interest rate parity

Forward rates reflect interest rate differentials. With USD/INR spot at 84.00, a one-year INR rate of 7% and USD rate of 4%, the one-year forward = 84 x 1.07 / 1.04 = 86.42. If the market forward differs, covered interest arbitrage would push it back.

5. Forward premium and discount

The currency with the higher interest rate trades at a forward discount, and the lower-rate currency at a forward premium. Because Indian rates are usually higher than US rates, the dollar normally trades at a forward premium against the rupee.

6. Currency swaps

In a cross-currency swap, two parties exchange principal in two currencies at the start, pay each other interest in the respective currencies during the term and re-exchange principal at maturity. Companies with foreign currency loans use swaps to convert them into rupee liabilities.

7. Currency options

Options give the buyer the right, not the obligation, to buy or sell a currency at a strike rate. They protect against adverse moves while letting the hedger benefit from favourable moves, in exchange for an upfront premium - like insurance on the exchange rate.

8. Hedging example

An exporter expecting USD receivables in three months sells USD/INR futures today. If the rupee strengthens, the lower rupee value of the receivables is offset by gains on the short futures. If the rupee weakens, the futures lose but the receivables are worth more, so the net rate is locked.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Forward rate = Spot x (1 + r domestic x t) / (1 + r foreign x t)
  • Annualised forward premium % = (Forward - Spot) / Spot x 12 / months x 100

Common traps in the exam

  • Assuming the higher-interest currency trades at a forward premium - it trades at a discount.
  • Treating a forward as exchange-traded with daily margins; that describes futures.
  • Thinking a hedger is speculating - an exporter selling futures against receivables reduces risk.
  • Forgetting that hedging locks the rate both ways, giving up gains from favourable moves (unless options are used).

Quick recap

Forwards are custom OTC hedges; futures are standardised, cleared and marked to market. Interest rate parity: forward = spot x (1 + r_d) / (1 + r_f). Higher-rate currency trades at a forward discount, so USD usually stands at a premium to INR. Swaps exchange principal and interest across currencies. Options hedge while keeping upside.
