8 free questions with answers and explanations on Foreign Exchange Derivatives, from the NISM Series I (Currency Derivatives) syllabus.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · How we create questions

What this chapter covers

This chapter explains what derivatives are and the main foreign exchange derivatives: forwards, futures, options and swaps. It compares over-the-counter and exchange-traded products, describes the role of hedgers, speculators and arbitrageurs, and introduces pricing through interest rate parity, forward premium and discount, and the offshore non-deliverable forward market. Focus on how payoffs differ between linear products (forwards and futures) and non-linear products (options), how forward premiums are annualised, and the practical differences in counterparty risk and cash flows between a bank forward and an exchange-traded future.

Foreign Exchange Derivatives questions

Question 1

A trader buys USD/INR futures on one exchange and simultaneously sells the same contract on another exchange where it is priced higher, locking in a risk-free gain. This trader is acting as:

  1. An arbitrageur
  2. A hedger
  3. A speculator
  4. A market regulator
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. An arbitrageur

An arbitrageur exploits a price mismatch between markets by taking offsetting positions, so there is little net exposure. A speculator takes an open directional view, while a hedger offsets an existing business exposure.

Question 2

What is the underlying asset of a USD/INR futures contract?

  1. The exchange rate between the US dollar and the Indian rupee
  2. US government treasury bills
  3. The difference between Indian and US interest rates over the contract tenor
  4. Shares of Indian companies listed abroad
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. The exchange rate between the US dollar and the Indian rupee

A derivative derives its value from an underlying. For USD/INR futures the underlying is the USD/INR exchange rate itself. Interest rates affect its price but are not the underlying.

Question 3

USD/INR spot is 84.00 and the three-month forward rate is 84.63. The annualised forward premium on the dollar is approximately:

  1. 3.0%
  2. 0.75%
  3. 6.3%
  4. 1.5%
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. 3.0%

Premium for three months = 0.63 / 84.00 = 0.75%. Annualising by multiplying by 12/3 gives about 3.0%. The 0.75% figure is the unannualised three-month premium.

Question 4

A non-deliverable forward (NDF) on the rupee is best described as a forward contract that:

  1. Is traded offshore and settled only by paying the difference in a convertible currency such as USD
  2. Is traded only on recognised Indian exchanges and is always settled by physical delivery of rupees at maturity
  3. Cannot be cancelled before maturity under any circumstances
  4. Is settled by delivering gold equal to the contract value
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Is traded offshore and settled only by paying the difference in a convertible currency such as USD

NDFs on restricted currencies like the rupee trade in centres such as Singapore and Dubai. No rupees change hands; at maturity the gain or loss against the fixing rate is settled in a convertible currency, usually USD.

Question 5

Which pair of instruments has a non-linear payoff profile with respect to the exchange rate?

  1. Bought call options and bought put options
  2. Currency futures and currency forwards
  3. Long futures and short forwards
  4. Spot purchases and spot sales
Show answer & explanation

Answer: A. Bought call options and bought put options

Futures and forwards gain or lose the same amount for each rupee move, giving straight-line payoffs. Options are non-linear: losses for a buyer stop at the premium, while gains continue as the option goes deeper in the money.

Question 6

An exporter switches his hedge from a bank forward to exchange-traded USD/INR futures. Which new requirement must he now plan for?

  1. Physical delivery of dollars to the exchange at expiry
  2. Paying an option premium at the start of the hedge
  3. Obtaining a fresh approval from RBI each time he places an order or modifies an existing one
  4. Daily cash flows from mark-to-market settlement until the position is closed
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. Daily cash flows from mark-to-market settlement until the position is closed

A forward is settled only at maturity, but futures are marked to market daily, so losses must be paid in cash each day. Currency futures in India are cash-settled in rupees, and no premium is involved.

Question 7

Other things unchanged, if Indian interest rates rise while US interest rates stay the same, the USD/INR forward premium will tend to:

  1. Narrow, because higher rupee rates attract dollars into India
  2. Remain unchanged, as forwards depend only on the spot rate
  3. Widen, because the interest rate differential in favour of INR has increased
  4. Turn into a discount on the US dollar immediately
Show answer & explanation

Answer: C. Widen, because the interest rate differential in favour of INR has increased

Under covered interest parity the forward premium reflects the interest differential. A larger gap between INR and USD rates means the dollar must trade at a bigger forward premium to prevent arbitrage.

Question 8

A bank buys USD 1 million spot and, in the same deal with the same counterparty, sells USD 1 million one month forward. This combined transaction is called:

  1. A cross-currency interest rate swap
  2. A currency option collar
  3. An outright forward purchase
  4. An FX swap
Show answer & explanation

Answer: D. An FX swap

An FX swap combines a spot deal with an opposite forward deal for the same amount. A cross-currency swap instead involves periodic interest exchanges over a longer tenor, and an outright forward has only one leg.
