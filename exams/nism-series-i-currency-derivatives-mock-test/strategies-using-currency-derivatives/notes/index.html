Strategies Using Currency Derivatives: NISM Series I revision notes

This chapter applies futures and options to trading and hedging situations. Expect several calculations: maximum profit and loss of spreads, break-evens of straddles, and identifying arbitrage. Questions also ask which strategy matches a stated view - moderately bullish, unsure of direction, or hedging with upside.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Bull call spread

A trader expecting USD/INR to rise moderately buys an 84.00 call at ₹0.50 and sells an 85.00 call at ₹0.15. Net cost is ₹0.35. Maximum profit = (1.00 - 0.35) x 1,000 = ₹650 above 85.00; maximum loss = ₹350 below 84.00; break-even 84.35.

2. Bear put spread

To profit from a moderate rupee appreciation, buy a higher-strike put and sell a lower-strike put. Both profit and loss are capped. The sold put reduces the cost of protection but gives up gains beyond the lower strike. Maximum profit is the strike difference minus net premium.

3. Long straddle

Ahead of a big policy announcement, a trader unsure of direction buys an 84.00 call at ₹0.40 and an 84.00 put at ₹0.35. Total premium is ₹0.75, so break-evens are 83.25 and 84.75. Profit comes only if USD/INR moves beyond either point.

4. Long strangle

A strangle buys an out-of-the-money call and an out-of-the-money put, for example an 84.50 call and an 83.50 put. It costs less than a straddle but needs a larger move to profit. Short straddles and strangles profit in calm markets but risk large losses.

5. Calendar spread

Selling a near-month USD/INR future and buying a far-month future (or vice versa) is a calendar spread. It profits from changes in the gap between the two contracts, which mainly reflects interest rate differentials, not the direction of the rupee. Margins are lower than for outright positions.

6. Cash and futures arbitrage

If USD/INR futures trade well above their interest-parity fair value, an arbitrageur borrows rupees, buys dollars in spot (or OTC) and sells futures, locking in a riskless spread. Such activity pulls futures back to fair value. When futures are below fair value, the reverse trade applies.

7. Hedging with protection and upside

An exporter wanting protection against rupee appreciation but still wanting to gain if the rupee depreciates should buy USD/INR put options. Selling futures would lock the rate but give up the upside; the put costs premium but keeps the benefit.

8. Range forwards and collars

Buying a put and selling a call (for an exporter) creates a collar: the receivable's value is kept within a band, and the call premium received offsets the put premium. Zero-cost collars are popular but cap the upside. Importers build the mirror image: buy a call, sell a put.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Bull call spread max profit = (Higher strike - Lower strike - Net premium) x Lot
  • Straddle break-evens = Strike +/- (Call premium + Put premium)

Common traps in the exam

  • Calling a near-sell, far-buy futures position a directional trade - it is a calendar spread.
  • Choosing futures when the hedger wants protection plus upside; options are needed.
  • Using only one premium when computing straddle break-evens - add both.
  • Forgetting that a bull call spread's maximum profit is capped at the higher strike.

Quick recap

Bull call spread: buy low strike, sell high strike, profit and loss capped. Straddles and strangles bet on big moves; break-evens use total premium. Calendar spreads trade the gap between expiries. Arbitrage sells overpriced futures against spot. Exporters wanting upside buy puts; collars cap both sides.
