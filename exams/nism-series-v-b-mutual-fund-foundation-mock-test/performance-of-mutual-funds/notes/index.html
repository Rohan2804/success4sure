Performance of Mutual Funds: NISM Series V-B revision notes

This chapter teaches distributors how to read and explain a fund's performance honestly. It covers simple return calculations, the role of benchmarks, why costs matter and how to compare like with like. Expect two or three easy calculations with round numbers, plus questions on choosing the right comparison.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Absolute return

Absolute return is the simple percentage change in NAV. If a growth option NAV rises from ₹20 to ₹23, the return is (23 - 20) / 20 = 15%. It suits periods of up to one year; longer periods should be compared using annualised returns.

2. Total return with IDCW

When a fund pays IDCW, the payout must be added back. A unit bought at ₹10, worth ₹12 after a year, which also paid ₹1 IDCW, earned (12 - 10 + 1) / 10 = 30%. Looking only at NAV would understate the return at 20%.

3. Annualised return (CAGR)

CAGR shows the steady yearly rate that turns the starting value into the ending value. ₹10,000 growing to ₹16,105 in five years is a CAGR of about 10%. CAGR hides the ups and downs along the way, so volatility should also be considered.

4. Benchmarks

A benchmark is the yardstick for a fund's performance - usually an index that represents the fund's investment universe. A large cap fund that returned 14% when its benchmark returned 16% has underperformed, even though 14% looks attractive in isolation.

5. Compare like with like

A liquid fund and a small cap fund have completely different objectives and risks, so comparing their returns is meaningless. Compare a scheme with its benchmark and with other schemes in the same SEBI category over the same period. Peer rankings are published by AMFI and data providers.

6. Expense ratio matters

The TER is deducted from scheme assets daily. Two schemes earning 12% on identical portfolios, one with TER 0.5% and the other 1.5%, will give investors roughly 11.5% and 10.5%. Over 15 to 20 years the gap compounds into a large difference.

7. Drivers of returns

Equity fund returns depend on company earnings, valuations and market mood; debt fund returns depend on interest income and changes in interest rates - bond prices fall when rates rise. Gold funds follow gold prices and the rupee. So the same news can affect equity and debt funds differently.

8. Past performance caution

Scheme documents carry the warning that past performance may or may not be sustained. Distributors should present returns over several periods, alongside the benchmark, and never use one great year to sell a scheme. Returns over one, three and five years, shown together, give a fairer picture of a scheme's consistency.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Absolute return = (End NAV - Start NAV) / Start NAV x 100
  • Total return = (End NAV - Start NAV + IDCW received) / Start NAV x 100
  • CAGR = (End value / Start value)^(1/years) - 1

Common traps in the exam

  • Ignoring IDCW payouts when calculating return - add them back to get total return.
  • Quoting absolute return for a five-year period as if it were yearly; annualise it with CAGR.
  • Comparing a fund with a benchmark from a different segment, such as a mid cap fund against the Nifty 50.
  • Assuming a fund with a lower NAV is cheaper and therefore better value.

Quick recap

Absolute return for up to a year, CAGR beyond. Add back IDCW for total return. Judge a fund against its benchmark and its category peers, not other categories. Lower expense ratio means more return for the investor. Past performance is not a promise.
