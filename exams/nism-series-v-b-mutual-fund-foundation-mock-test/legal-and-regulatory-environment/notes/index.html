Legal and Regulatory Environment: NISM Series V-B revision notes

The last V-B chapter covers the rules that keep the industry honest: who regulates mutual funds, what AMFI does, what rights investors have and how complaints are resolved. Questions are straightforward but easy to mix up, especially the sequence of the complaint process and the difference between SEBI and AMFI.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. SEBI as regulator

Mutual funds in India are governed by the SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations framed under the SEBI Act, 1992. SEBI registers fund houses, approves the structure, sets rules on investments, expenses and disclosures, and can penalise AMCs, trustees and distributors. It also inspects AMCs periodically.

2. AMFI's role

AMFI is the industry association of SEBI-registered AMCs. It registers distributors and issues ARNs, publishes the code of conduct for distributors and the AMFI Code of Ethics (ACE) for AMCs, runs investor awareness campaigns and publishes industry data. It is a self-regulatory body, not a statutory regulator.

3. AMFI Code of Ethics

ACE sets standards of integrity, fairness, due diligence and disclosure for AMCs and their employees. It expects them to put investors' interests first, avoid misleading advertisements and maintain confidentiality of investor information. It covers advertising, record keeping and the handling of investor complaints as well, so that standards are consistent across fund houses.

4. Distributor code of conduct

Distributors must act in the investor's interest, disclose commissions, provide scheme documents, recommend suitable schemes, avoid churning and never assure returns or misrepresent risk. Violations can lead to ARN suspension or cancellation. They must also keep KYD details current and maintain records of client interactions, so that recommendations can be reviewed if a complaint arises later.

5. Investor rights

Unit holders own a proportionate share of scheme assets, receive statements and annual reports, can inspect key documents and are entitled to timely payment of redemptions and IDCW, with interest for delays. Delays in redemption or IDCW payments attract interest at 15% a year, paid by the AMC from its own resources, not from the scheme.

6. Fundamental attribute changes

If an AMC proposes to change a fundamental attribute of a scheme, it needs trustee and SEBI approval, must inform unit holders in writing and must give them a window to exit at NAV without any exit load. The exit window is typically at least 30 days.

7. Complaint process

An investor with a complaint, such as delayed redemption, should first approach the AMC or its RTA. If not satisfied, the investor can escalate to SEBI through the SCORES online portal, and thereafter use the SMART ODR platform for online conciliation or arbitration.

8. Advertisements

Mutual fund advertisements must carry the standard warning that investments are subject to market risks and that investors should read scheme documents. Performance can be shown only in the format SEBI prescribes, alongside the benchmark. Promising or implying assured returns in an advertisement is prohibited.

Common traps in the exam

  • Calling AMFI the regulator of mutual funds - SEBI is the regulator; AMFI is the industry association.
  • Going straight to SEBI with a complaint without first approaching the AMC or RTA.
  • Assuming changes to fundamental attributes need only trustee approval; SEBI approval and a load-free exit window are also required.
  • Mixing up ACE (code for AMCs) and the distributor code of conduct.

Quick recap

SEBI regulates under the SEBI (MF) Regulations; AMFI registers distributors and sets codes but is not a regulator. Distributors must disclose commissions and sell suitably. Investors are entitled to timely payments and documents. Fundamental changes need approvals and a free exit. Complaints: AMC/RTA, then SCORES, then SMART ODR.
