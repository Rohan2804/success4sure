Investment Landscape: NISM Series V-B revision notes

V-B is written for first-time distributors, so this chapter keeps the basics simple: why money left idle loses value, how compounding builds wealth, and how common asset classes differ in risk, return and liquidity. Expect small calculations with round numbers and situational questions such as which asset suits a young saver or a retiree.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Why idle money shrinks

If a family keeps ₹1 lakh in a cupboard and prices rise 6% a year, after one year that cash buys what ₹94,000 or so bought earlier. Inflation quietly reduces purchasing power, which is why the workbook argues that savings need to be invested, not just stored.

2. Saving vs investing

Saving means not spending part of income. Investing means putting those savings into assets expected to earn a return - interest, dividends or price growth. A recurring deposit, a PPF account and an equity SIP are all investments, though with very different risk levels.

3. Compounding at work

Compounding means earning returns on earlier returns. ₹1,00,000 at 10% a year becomes ₹1,10,000 after one year and ₹1,21,000 after two, because the second year's interest is earned on ₹1,10,000. Starting early matters more than investing large amounts later. Ten years at 10% roughly multiplies money 2.6 times.

4. Common asset classes

Cash and bank deposits are safe and liquid but earn modestly. Debt (bonds, deposits) offers steadier income. Equity offers the highest long-run growth with sharp short-term swings. Gold acts as a hedge in crises. Real estate needs large sums and is hard to sell quickly.

5. Liquidity

Liquidity is how quickly an investment can be converted to cash at a fair value. A savings account is instantly liquid; an open-ended mutual fund pays within a few working days; a plot of land might take months to sell and may need a discount.

6. Risk and return trade-off

Higher expected return comes with higher risk. A bank FD gives predictable interest; an equity fund can gain 25% in one year and fall 20% the next. No product offers high returns with no risk, and claims of that kind are a warning sign.

7. Types of risk

Inflation risk erodes purchasing power. Market risk affects all shares together. Credit risk is the borrower failing to pay. Liquidity risk is being unable to sell. Concentration risk comes from putting too much into one company or sector, which diversification addresses.

8. Goals and time horizon

A goal needs an amount and a date. Money needed in six months for a wedding should stay in safe, liquid options; a retirement 35 years away can be built mostly with equity. A 25-year-old with stable income can usually accept more risk than a 60-year-old.

9. Diversification

Spreading money across many companies and sectors reduces the damage if one company fails. A mutual fund holding 50 stocks protects the investor far better than owning two or three shares directly, though it cannot avoid a fall in the whole market.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Value after n years = Amount x (1 + r)^n
  • Real return is roughly the interest rate minus the inflation rate

Common traps in the exam

  • Assuming money in a savings account is 'safe' from loss - its purchasing power can fall when inflation exceeds the interest rate.
  • Confusing liquidity with safety: a listed small company share is easy to sell but risky.
  • Thinking diversification removes all risk; it reduces company-specific risk but not market-wide falls.
  • Using simple interest when the question says compounded annually - interest is added to the principal each year.

Quick recap

Inflation shrinks idle money, so invest. Compounding rewards starting early. Cash and deposits are safe, debt is steady, equity grows most but swings most, gold hedges crises, property is illiquid. Match the product to the goal's date. Diversify across companies and sectors to cut concentration risk.
