Concept and Role of a Mutual Fund: NISM Series V-B revision notes

This chapter answers the question every new investor asks: what exactly is a mutual fund and why should I use one? It covers pooling, units, NAV, the main scheme structures and SEBI's categories for equity, debt and hybrid funds. Questions are direct definitions plus scenarios about exiting closed-ended schemes and identifying fund categories.

Last updated 19 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Pooling explained

Imagine 10,000 people each contributing ₹5,000. The ₹5 crore pool is invested by a professional fund manager across many securities. Each person receives units in proportion to the money contributed, so all investors share gains and losses in the same proportion. Unit holders are owners, not lenders.

2. Benefits

Small ticket sizes (SIPs from a few hundred rupees), professional management, instant diversification, easy liquidity in open-ended schemes, regular disclosures and SEBI regulation make funds attractive for small investors who cannot research and track dozens of shares themselves. Daily NAVs also make tracking easy.

3. Open-ended schemes

Open-ended schemes buy and sell units every business day at NAV-based prices, so the number of units keeps changing. There is no maturity date. Most retail investments - SIPs in equity funds, liquid funds for parking cash - are in open-ended schemes.

4. Closed-ended and interval schemes

A closed-ended scheme raises money only during its NFO and has a fixed maturity. Before maturity, investors exit by selling units on the stock exchange, where the price may be below NAV. Interval schemes allow transactions only during pre-set windows.

5. Equity fund categories

SEBI defines large caps as the top 100 listed companies by market capitalisation, mid caps as 101st to 250th and small caps as 251st onwards. Large cap funds keep at least 80% in large caps; mid cap and small cap funds at least 65% in their respective segments.

6. Flexi cap and multi cap

A flexi cap fund must hold at least 65% in equity but can move freely across large, mid and small caps. A multi cap fund must hold at least 25% each in large, mid and small caps, so it always carries meaningful small cap exposure.

7. Debt and hybrid categories

Debt funds range from overnight and liquid funds for very short periods to gilt and long duration funds for rate-sensitive investors. Hybrid funds mix equity and debt; arbitrage funds earn the price gap between cash and futures markets, so their returns resemble low-risk debt funds.

8. What funds cannot do

A mutual fund cannot assure returns on market-linked schemes, cannot prevent NAV falls, and investors have no say in which stocks are bought. Expenses are charged every day through the expense ratio, whether the fund gains or loses. Investors bear these costs in every market.

Common traps in the exam

  • Thinking a closed-ended scheme's units can be redeemed with the AMC anytime - before maturity, the exit is via the stock exchange.
  • Swapping the definitions of flexi cap (no segment minimum) and multi cap (25% each in large, mid and small).
  • Calling arbitrage funds high-risk equity funds; their hedged positions make returns closer to short-term debt.
  • Believing mutual fund investors are lenders who will get their principal back - they own units and bear losses.

Quick recap

A fund pools money and issues units; investors share gains and losses proportionally. Open-ended = daily transactions; closed-ended = exit via exchange. Large cap = top 100, mid = 101-250, small = 251+. Large cap fund 80% in large caps; flexi cap 65% equity anywhere; multi cap 25% each. No returns are assured.
