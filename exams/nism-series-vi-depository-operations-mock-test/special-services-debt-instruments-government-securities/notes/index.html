Special Services – Debt Instruments & Government Securities: NISM Series VI revision notes

Most debt securities in India now exist only in electronic form. This chapter covers corporate bonds and debentures, money market instruments and government securities, and how a depository handles interest payments and redemptions. Questions test features of each instrument, which must be in demat and how retail investors can hold G-secs.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Corporate bonds and debentures

Listed corporate debt securities must be issued and held in demat form. Interest is paid on coupon dates to beneficial owners as per the record date, and principal is repaid on maturity. Debentures can be secured or unsecured, and convertible or non-convertible; most retail trading is in non-convertible debentures (NCDs).

2. Commercial paper

Commercial paper is an unsecured short-term promissory note issued by companies, NBFCs and other eligible entities for maturities up to one year, at a discount to face value. CPs are issued only in demat form, and their issuance and holding are regulated by RBI, while listing comes under SEBI rules.

3. Certificates of deposit

CDs are negotiable short-term instruments issued by banks and certain financial institutions at a discount, for tenors from 7 days to one year for banks. They must be in demat form. Unlike a fixed deposit, a CD can be transferred, but cannot be prematurely redeemed by the issuer except as permitted.

4. Government securities

Central government dated securities and state development loans carry fixed or floating coupons and long maturities, while treasury bills are zero-coupon instruments of 91, 182 and 364 days issued at a discount. RBI acts as debt manager and maintains the Subsidiary General Ledger for banks and institutions.

5. How retail investors hold G-secs

Retail investors can hold G-secs in their demat accounts through depositories, which hold them in a constituent SGL account with RBI on their behalf. Alternatively, the RBI Retail Direct scheme lets individuals open a gilt account directly with RBI and buy in primary auctions and on the NDS-OM secondary platform.

6. Interest processing

For demat debt, the depository provides the list of beneficial owners as on the record date to the issuer's RTA or paying agent, which pays interest to the linked bank accounts. If the investor sells just before the record date, the buyer may receive the interest, with the price reflecting accrued interest.

7. Redemption as a corporate action

On maturity, redemption is processed as a corporate action: the depository debits the securities from beneficial owners' accounts and the issuer pays the redemption amount to their bank accounts. Partial redemptions reduce face value per unit without changing the number of units.

8. T-bill pricing example

A 364-day T-bill with face value ₹100 issued at ₹93.50 earns ₹6.50 at maturity. The discount yield is calculated on face value, while the investor's effective return is computed on the price paid: 6.50 ÷ 93.50 ≈ 6.95% for the year.

9. Sovereign gold bonds

SGBs are government securities denominated in grams of gold, issued by RBI on behalf of the government, and can be held in demat form and traded on exchanges. They carry a fixed interest on the issue price in addition to gold price returns. New issuance has been paused, but existing tranches trade and are held in demat.

Common traps in the exam

  • Treating CPs as secured. Commercial paper is unsecured and backed only by the issuer's credit.
  • Assuming T-bills pay a coupon. They are issued at a discount and redeemed at face value.
  • Forgetting that redemption debits the demat account. It is a debit corporate action, with money paid to the bank account.
  • Thinking retail investors cannot hold G-secs. They can, via demat accounts (CSGL through depositories) or RBI Retail Direct.

Quick recap

Listed corporate debt, CPs and CDs exist in demat form. G-secs are held by RBI in SGL accounts; retail can hold via depositories or RBI Retail Direct. T-bills are discounted zero-coupon instruments. Interest goes to beneficial owners on the record date; redemption debits securities and pays money as a corporate action.
