Introduction to the Indian Capital Market: NISM Series VI revision notes

This opening chapter of the Depository Operations exam maps the capital market so that later chapters make sense. Expect direct questions on the difference between primary and secondary markets, which institution is a market infrastructure institution, what SEBI regulates, and how investors apply in public issues. Most items test roles: who raises money, who trades, who clears, who holds the securities.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Two halves of the market

The primary market is where securities are created and sold for the first time, so money flows from investors to the issuer (IPO, FPO, rights issue, private placement). The secondary market is where existing securities change hands between investors on a stock exchange; the company receives nothing when you buy its shares from another investor on NSE or BSE.

2. Market infrastructure institutions

Stock exchanges, clearing corporations and depositories are called MIIs because the whole system runs through them. The exchange matches orders, the clearing corporation guarantees and settles trades, and the depository (NSDL or CDSL) holds securities electronically and moves them between accounts. All three are regulated by SEBI and are expected to put public interest ahead of profit.

3. SEBI and other regulators

SEBI, set up under the SEBI Act, 1992, protects investors and develops and regulates the securities market. RBI regulates government securities, money markets and foreign exchange, and the Ministry of Corporate Affairs administers the Companies Act. In GIFT City, IFSCA is the unified regulator. A question asking who registers a depository participant always points to SEBI.

4. Intermediaries

Key SEBI-registered intermediaries include stock brokers, depository participants, merchant bankers, registrars and share transfer agents (RTAs), bankers to an issue, custodians, portfolio managers, investment advisers and credit rating agencies. Each needs registration and must follow a code of conduct. A DP, for example, cannot operate without being registered with SEBI and admitted by a depository.

5. Raising money through ASBA and UPI

In public issues, investors apply through ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount): the bid money stays in the investor's bank account and is only blocked, not debited, until allotment. Retail and small individual bidders can use a UPI mandate through their broker, DP or RTA app. On allotment the blocked amount is debited; for unallotted bids the block is released.

6. Instruments traded

Equity shares give ownership and variable returns; debt instruments such as debentures and bonds give fixed claims with interest; hybrids such as convertible debentures and preference shares sit between. Units of mutual funds, ETFs, REITs and InvITs and government securities can also be held and traded in demat form. Derivatives are contracts, not securities held in a depository.

7. Why depositories matter

Before 1996, physical certificates caused bad deliveries, forgery, theft and long transfer delays. Depositories allow settlement by simple book entries, so ownership changes instantly at pay-out. Today listed securities must be held in demat form for transfer, which is why every trader needs a demat account with a DP alongside a trading account with a broker.

8. Investor protection layer

Investor protection rests on disclosure (offer documents, continuous listing disclosures), segregation of client assets, settlement guarantees by clearing corporations, investor protection funds at exchanges and depositories, and grievance redress through SCORES and online dispute resolution. Remember these as the safety net that surrounds the trading and holding system.

9. Trading and demat account link

A trading account is opened with a stock broker to place orders; a demat account is opened with a DP to hold securities. Many brokers are also DPs, so investors often open both together, but they remain separate accounts with separate rights and obligations documents. Settlement moves securities between the demat account and the clearing corporation.

Common traps in the exam

  • Confusing exchange and clearing corporation: the exchange matches trades, the clearing corporation guarantees and settles them through novation.
  • Assuming the company gets money in the secondary market. Only primary market issues bring fresh funds to the issuer; secondary trades are between investors.
  • Thinking ASBA debits the bid amount on application. It only blocks the money; the debit happens after allotment is finalised.
  • Calling a depository participant a depository. NSDL and CDSL are the depositories; DPs are their agents who deal with investors.

Quick recap

Primary market creates securities, secondary market trades them. Exchanges match, clearing corporations settle and guarantee, depositories hold electronically through DPs. SEBI regulates the securities market, RBI the money and G-sec markets. Public issue bids use ASBA or UPI so money is blocked, not debited, until allotment.
