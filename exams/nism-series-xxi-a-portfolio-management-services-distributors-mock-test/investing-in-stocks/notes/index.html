Investing in Stocks: NISM Series XXI-A revision notes

Most PMS strategies are equity-focused, so distributors must understand how stocks are analysed and selected. This chapter covers shareholder rights, sources of equity returns, fundamental analysis from economy to company, key ratios, technical analysis basics and investment styles. Learn which ratio suits which kind of company and how to explain a manager's style to clients.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Shareholder rights

Equity shareholders own part of the company and are entitled to vote on key matters, receive dividends when declared, receive bonus and rights entitlements, inspect certain records and share residual assets on liquidation after creditors. Their liability is limited to the amount invested.

2. Sources of return

Equity returns come from dividends and capital appreciation. Capital appreciation is driven by earnings growth and changes in the valuation multiple. If earnings grow 15% a year and the P/E stays constant, the share price should rise about 15% a year, plus dividends.

3. Economy-industry-company

Fundamental analysis looks at the economy (growth, inflation, rates), the industry (structure, competition, cycle) and the company (business model, management, financials). A PMS manager's investment approach often emphasises one layer, such as sector rotation or bottom-up stock picking.

4. Valuation ratios

P/E = price ÷ EPS suits stable earners. P/B = price ÷ book value per share suits banks and financials. EV/EBITDA suits capital-intensive businesses and allows comparison across different debt levels. Dividend yield = DPS ÷ price suits mature, cash-generating companies such as utilities.

5. Financial metrics

ROE measures profit relative to shareholders' equity; consistently high ROE with low debt suggests a strong business. Debt-to-equity measures leverage. Operating and net margins show pricing power and cost control. Sales and earnings growth show momentum. Cash flow from operations confirms profit quality.

6. Technical analysis basics

Technical analysis studies price charts, trends, support and resistance, moving averages and volume to time entries and exits. Some PMS strategies use momentum or trend signals. Technical analysis does not assess business value and is used mostly for timing.

7. Growth vs value

Growth investing targets companies with high earnings growth, often at higher valuations. Value investing buys companies trading below their intrinsic value, often at low P/E or P/B. Quality investing focuses on high ROE and strong balance sheets. Styles go in and out of favour over market cycles.

8. Market-cap styles

Large caps are established companies with lower volatility and higher liquidity. Mid and small caps offer higher growth potential but higher volatility and lower liquidity. In SEBI's classification, large caps are the top 100 companies by market capitalisation, mid caps 101–250 and small caps beyond 250.

9. Concentration in PMS

PMS portfolios are often concentrated in 15–30 stocks, unlike diversified mutual funds. Concentration can boost returns when picks work but increases volatility and stock-specific risk. Distributors must explain this clearly to clients.

Common traps in the exam

  • Using P/E for banks without considering P/B. Book value is central for financial companies.
  • Assuming dividends are the main source of equity returns. Earnings growth and re-rating usually dominate.
  • Confusing growth with quality. Growth is about earnings expansion; quality is about returns on capital and balance sheet strength.
  • Ignoring liquidity risk in small caps, especially in concentrated portfolios.

Quick recap

Shareholders own, vote and get dividends with limited liability. Returns = dividends + earnings growth + re-rating. Use economy-industry-company analysis. P/E for stable earners, P/B for banks, EV/EBITDA for capital-intensive firms, dividend yield for mature companies. Know growth, value and quality styles and SEBI's market-cap buckets. PMS concentration raises stock-specific risk.
