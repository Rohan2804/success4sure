Derivatives: NISM Series XXI-A revision notes

Portfolio managers use derivatives to hedge and rebalance, not to take leveraged bets on clients' money. This chapter introduces forwards, futures, options and swaps, their pricing basics, margins and mark-to-market, option terminology and simple strategies. Focus on hedging uses and on being able to explain derivative risks to clients clearly.

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Types of derivatives

A forward is a customised OTC agreement to buy or sell an asset at a future date at a fixed price. A future is a standardised exchange-traded forward with daily settlement. An option gives the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) at a strike price. A swap exchanges cash flows, such as fixed for floating interest.

2. Futures mechanics

Futures trade in fixed lot sizes with standard expiry dates. Traders deposit initial margin, and gains or losses are settled daily through mark-to-market. A 1% move in a Nifty futures contract with notional value ₹15 lakh means a ₹15,000 daily gain or loss.

3. Cost of carry

The theoretical futures price = spot price × (1 + cost of carry), where carry is the financing cost minus income such as dividends. If Nifty is at 24,000 and annual carry is 7%, the one-month futures price should be about 24,000 × (1 + 0.07/12) ≈ 24,140.

4. Option terms

The premium is the price paid for an option. A call is in the money when spot exceeds strike; a put is in the money when strike exceeds spot. Intrinsic value is the in-the-money amount; time value is premium minus intrinsic value, which decays as expiry approaches.

5. Option example

A call with strike ₹1,000 bought at ₹40 premium. If the stock is at ₹1,070 on expiry, intrinsic value is ₹70 and profit is ₹30 per share. If the stock ends at ₹980, the call expires worthless and the loss is limited to ₹40 premium.

6. Hedging uses in PMS

A manager worried about a market fall can sell index futures or buy index puts against an equity portfolio, protecting value without selling stocks and incurring taxes or transaction costs. Futures can also be used to quickly adjust exposure while rebalancing between stocks.

7. Basic strategies

A protective put (own stock plus buy put) caps downside. A covered call (own stock plus sell call) earns premium but caps upside. Collars combine both. These strategies change the risk-return profile rather than eliminate risk.

8. Leverage and risk

Derivatives require only a margin, so small price moves cause large percentage gains or losses relative to the money deposited. Option sellers face potentially large losses. Counterparty risk exists in OTC derivatives but is reduced by clearing corporations on exchanges.

9. PMS limits

SEBI rules allow portfolio managers to use derivatives for hedging and portfolio rebalancing within prescribed limits, and do not permit leveraging client portfolios. Disclosure documents must state derivatives use, and distributors must explain the related risks to clients.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Futures fair value ≈ spot × (1 + r × t) − expected dividends; option premium = intrinsic value + time value.

Common traps in the exam

  • Confusing the obligation of a futures buyer with an option buyer's right. Option buyers can walk away; futures buyers cannot.
  • Thinking time value increases near expiry. It decays as expiry approaches.
  • Assuming PMS can use derivatives to leverage portfolios. They are allowed only for hedging and rebalancing within limits.
  • Believing hedging removes all risk. It reduces specific risks, at a cost.

Quick recap

Forwards are OTC, futures are standardised with daily MTM, options give rights for a premium, swaps exchange cash flows. Futures price ≈ spot × (1 + carry). Call ITM when spot > strike; put ITM when strike > spot. Time value decays. PMS uses derivatives for hedging and rebalancing only, never leverage.
