Portfolio Management Process: NISM Series XXI-A revision notes

The portfolio management process is a disciplined cycle rather than a set of stock tips. This chapter covers understanding the client, writing an investment policy, setting strategic and tactical asset allocation, selecting securities, executing, monitoring, rebalancing and reviewing. Questions often present a client scenario and ask which step or constraint is most relevant.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Understanding the client

The process begins with the client's objectives (return needs, goals), risk profile (capacity and tolerance) and constraints. A 45-year-old business owner with surplus funds and a 15-year horizon may accept high equity exposure; a retired client relying on portfolio income needs stability.

2. Constraints

Constraints include liquidity needs (cash required in the near term), time horizon, tax position, legal and regulatory factors (such as restrictions on certain holdings) and unique circumstances (ethical preferences, concentrated employer stock). Ignoring a constraint can make an otherwise good portfolio unsuitable.

3. Investment policy statement

An IPS documents objectives, risk tolerance, constraints, asset allocation ranges, benchmarks, rebalancing rules and review frequency. It guides decisions, prevents emotional deviations and provides a basis for evaluating the manager's performance.

4. Strategic asset allocation

Strategic allocation sets the long-term mix of asset classes, such as 70% equity, 25% debt, 5% gold, based on the client's profile. It is the largest driver of long-run portfolio returns and volatility, more than individual security selection.

5. Tactical asset allocation

Tactical allocation makes temporary deviations from the strategic mix to take advantage of market conditions, for example raising equity to 75% when valuations are attractive. It must stay within the ranges set in the IPS.

6. Security selection

Within each asset class, the manager selects securities using its investment approach: growth, value, quality, momentum, thematic or a combination. For a PMS, the disclosure document describes this approach, and the distributor should explain it accurately.

7. Execution

Execution involves placing orders efficiently, minimising transaction costs and market impact, and following best execution obligations. For large orders in less liquid stocks, the manager may spread purchases over several days.

8. Monitoring and rebalancing

The manager monitors the portfolio against objectives and market changes. Rebalancing brings the portfolio back to target weights when market moves cause drift: if equity rises to 80% against a 70% target, some equity is sold and debt bought. Rebalancing enforces discipline of selling high and buying low.

9. Reviewing the plan

Client circumstances change through events like retirement, inheritance, health issues or business sale. The IPS and asset allocation should be reviewed periodically and after such events. A portfolio that suited the client five years ago may no longer be appropriate.

Common traps in the exam

  • Starting with stock selection before understanding the client. Profiling and constraints come first.
  • Confusing strategic with tactical allocation. Strategic is long-term; tactical is a temporary tilt within ranges.
  • Ignoring liquidity constraints. Near-term cash needs must be met before seeking returns.
  • Treating rebalancing as optional. It keeps risk aligned with the client's profile.

Quick recap

Understand objectives, risk and constraints (liquidity, horizon, tax, legal, unique). Document them in an IPS. Strategic allocation drives long-run results; tactical tilts stay within ranges. Select securities per the stated approach, execute efficiently, monitor and rebalance, and review after life events.
