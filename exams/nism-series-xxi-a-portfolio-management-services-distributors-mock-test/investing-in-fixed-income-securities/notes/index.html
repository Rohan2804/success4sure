Investing in Fixed Income Securities: NISM Series XXI-A revision notes

Some PMS strategies invest in debt or use it to balance equity. This chapter covers bond features, yields, the price-yield relationship, duration and convexity, credit ratings, the main Indian issuers and the risks involved. Distributors should be able to explain to clients, in plain words, why bond prices fall when interest rates rise.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Bond features

A bond has a face value (usually ₹1,000 or ₹1 lakh for corporate bonds, ₹100 for G-secs), a coupon rate paid on face value, a maturity date and an issuer. Some bonds are zero-coupon, issued at a discount and redeemed at face value. Others have call or put options or floating coupons.

2. Yields

Current yield = annual coupon ÷ market price. Yield to maturity is the discount rate that equates the present value of all cash flows to the price, assuming coupons are reinvested at the same rate. A ₹100 bond with ₹9 coupon priced at ₹104 has current yield of 8.65% and a YTM lower than the coupon.

3. Price-yield relationship

When market interest rates rise, existing bonds with lower coupons become less attractive, so their prices fall until their yield matches the market. When rates fall, prices rise. This inverse relationship is the core of interest rate risk.

4. Duration

Modified duration measures how much a bond's price changes for a 1% change in yield. A portfolio with duration of 4 years loses about 4% if yields rise by 1% and gains about 4% if they fall by 1%. Longer maturities and lower coupons mean higher duration.

5. Convexity

Convexity measures how duration changes as yields change. Bonds with positive convexity gain more when yields fall than they lose when yields rise by the same amount. Duration alone is an approximation; convexity improves it for large rate moves.

6. Credit ratings and spreads

Ratings from agencies such as CRISIL, ICRA, CARE and India Ratings indicate default risk. AAA is the highest safety; BBB- is the lowest investment grade. Lower-rated bonds pay higher yields, called credit spreads, to compensate for higher default risk. Downgrades cause price falls.

7. Indian issuers

Central government securities and treasury bills carry sovereign risk; state development loans are issued by states; PSU bonds and corporate bonds carry issuer credit risk; commercial paper and certificates of deposit are short-term money market instruments. Each differs in yield, liquidity and risk.

8. Other risks

Reinvestment risk arises when coupons must be reinvested at lower rates. Liquidity risk arises because many corporate bonds trade infrequently, making it hard to sell without a price discount. Inflation risk erodes real returns on fixed coupons.

9. Explaining to clients

A simple explanation: if new bonds pay 8% but yours pays 7%, a buyer will pay less for your bond so that their effective return reaches 8%. Hence rising rates hurt existing bondholders, and longer bonds are hurt more.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • Price change % ≈ − modified duration × change in yield (% points) + ½ × convexity × (change in yield)².

Common traps in the exam

  • Thinking higher rates increase existing bond prices. Prices fall when yields rise.
  • Assuming a bond priced above par has a YTM above its coupon. Premium bonds have YTM below coupon.
  • Treating all bonds as safe. Corporate bonds carry credit and liquidity risk.
  • Ignoring reinvestment risk in YTM. YTM assumes coupons are reinvested at the same yield.

Quick recap

Bonds pay coupons on face value; current yield = coupon ÷ price; YTM is the full return to maturity. Prices fall when yields rise. Modified duration estimates percentage price change per 1% yield change; convexity refines it. Ratings and spreads reflect credit risk. Know G-secs, SDLs, PSU and corporate bonds, CPs and CDs.
