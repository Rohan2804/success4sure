Fundamentals of Research: NISM Series XV revision notes

This chapter explains how an analyst actually carries out research. It covers the main approaches, the choice between top-down and bottom-up methods, primary and secondary data sources, building financial models, testing assumptions and the behavioural traps that undermine good research. Questions are mostly conceptual but often framed as practical situations.

Last updated 19 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Fundamental analysis

Fundamental analysis estimates a security's intrinsic value from the economy, industry and company: earnings, cash flows, balance sheet strength, management and competitive position. If the market price is below estimated value, the analyst may recommend buying, expecting the gap to close over time.

2. Technical analysis

Technical analysis studies price and volume patterns, trends, support and resistance and indicators such as moving averages and RSI to predict price movements. It assumes prices reflect all information and that patterns repeat. It is used mostly for timing rather than valuation.

3. Quantitative analysis

Quantitative analysts use statistical models and large data sets to identify factors such as value, momentum, quality and low volatility that explain returns. Quant research is systematic and rule-based, which reduces emotional bias but depends on the robustness of historical relationships.

4. Top-down vs bottom-up

Top-down research starts with the economy, moves to sectors likely to benefit, then picks the best companies within them. Bottom-up research starts with individual companies' strengths regardless of the macro view. Many analysts combine both to check consistency.

5. Primary data

Primary data is collected first-hand for the research: management meetings, plant visits, channel checks with dealers and distributors, customer surveys and expert interviews. It provides insight not yet in published numbers, but must not cross into unpublished price-sensitive information.

6. Secondary data

Secondary data comes from existing sources: annual reports, quarterly results, exchange filings, industry association data, government statistics, RBI publications and databases. It is cheaper and verifiable but may be dated or not tailored to the question.

7. Building a model

A financial model links revenue drivers (volume, price, market share) to costs, margins, capital expenditure, working capital and financing, producing forecast income statements, balance sheets and cash flows. Good models are transparent, internally consistent and easy to update.

8. Testing assumptions

Sensitivity analysis changes one input at a time, such as raw material cost, to see the effect on earnings or value. Scenario analysis changes several inputs together to build bull, base and bear cases. Both show how robust the thesis is.

9. Behavioural pitfalls

Analysts are prone to confirmation bias (seeking evidence that supports their view), anchoring (sticking to an initial estimate), overconfidence, herding with consensus and the sunk-cost effect after a wrong call. Checklists, peer review and pre-defined exit criteria help counter these.

Common traps in the exam

  • Treating technical analysis as a valuation tool. It is mainly used for timing based on price and volume patterns.
  • Confusing top-down with bottom-up. Top-down starts with the economy; bottom-up starts with the company.
  • Thinking channel checks allow use of insider information. Primary research must not involve UPSI.
  • Mixing sensitivity and scenario analysis. Sensitivity changes one variable; scenarios change several together.

Quick recap

Fundamental (value), technical (timing) and quantitative (factors) approaches. Top-down starts with the economy, bottom-up with companies. Primary data is first-hand; secondary data is published. Models link drivers to statements. Use sensitivity and scenario analysis. Guard against confirmation, anchoring, overconfidence and herding.
