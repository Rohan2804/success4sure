Company Analysis – Financial Analysis: NISM Series XV revision notes

Financial statement analysis is the core quantitative skill for a research analyst and carries many marks. You need to compute ratios from raw data, interpret trends, break ROE into its drivers using DuPont, judge the quality of earnings and link numbers back to the business. Practise with real annual reports to make the ratios intuitive.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Three statements

The income statement shows revenue, expenses and profit for a period. The balance sheet shows assets, liabilities and equity at a point in time. The cash flow statement splits cash movements into operating, investing and financing activities. Profit can be manipulated more easily than cash, so analysts compare the two.

2. Profitability ratios

Gross margin, EBITDA margin and net profit margin measure profit per rupee of sales. Return on equity = net profit ÷ average equity; return on capital employed = EBIT ÷ capital employed. A company earning 25% ROCE in a business with 12% cost of capital creates value.

3. Efficiency ratios

Asset turnover = sales ÷ total assets. Inventory days, receivable days and payable days show how quickly working capital moves. A retailer with low margins can still earn high returns through high asset turnover.

4. Working capital cycle

Cash conversion cycle = inventory days + receivable days − payable days. A company with 60 inventory days, 45 receivable days and 30 payable days has a 75-day cycle, meaning cash is tied up for 75 days. Lengthening cycles absorb cash even when profits rise.

5. Liquidity ratios

Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities; quick ratio excludes inventory. Very low ratios signal stress; very high ratios may indicate idle cash or slow-moving inventory. Industry norms matter: FMCG firms often run negative working capital.

6. Leverage and coverage

Debt-to-equity = total debt ÷ shareholders' equity. Interest coverage = EBIT ÷ interest expense. A coverage of 1.5 leaves little room if profits dip. Net debt to EBITDA shows how many years of operating profit would repay debt.

7. DuPont analysis

ROE = net profit margin × asset turnover × equity multiplier (assets ÷ equity). Two companies with 18% ROE may differ: one with high margins, another with high leverage. Leverage-driven ROE is riskier, so DuPont shows the quality of returns.

8. Quality of earnings

Compare cash flow from operations with net profit over several years; persistent shortfalls suggest aggressive revenue recognition or rising receivables. Watch one-time gains, capitalised expenses, changes in depreciation policy and large other income. High-quality earnings are recurring and cash-backed.

9. Accounting red flags

Receivables growing faster than sales, frequent restatements, contingent liabilities that are large relative to net worth, complex subsidiaries and loans to related parties are warning signs. Read notes to accounts and the auditor's report, not just headline numbers.

10. Trends and peers

A single ratio means little alone. Analysts compare ratios over five or more years and against peers in the same industry, and link changes to business events such as a new plant, a price war or an acquisition.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • ROE = (net profit ÷ sales) × (sales ÷ assets) × (assets ÷ equity).
  • Cash conversion cycle = inventory days + receivable days − payable days.
  • Interest coverage = EBIT ÷ interest expense.

Common traps in the exam

  • Using year-end equity when average equity is required, or mixing profit before and after tax in ratios.
  • Treating rising profit with falling operating cash flow as healthy. It may indicate poor earnings quality.
  • Assuming high ROE is always good. DuPont may reveal it is driven by leverage.
  • Comparing ratios across unrelated industries. Benchmarks differ by business model.

Quick recap

Read all three statements; cash confirms profit. Profitability: margins, ROE, ROCE. Efficiency: turnover and working capital days; CCC = inventory + receivable − payable days. Liquidity: current and quick ratios. Leverage: D/E, interest coverage. DuPont splits ROE into margin, turnover and leverage. Look for trends, peers and red flags.
