Industry Analysis: NISM Series XV revision notes

Even a well-managed company struggles in a structurally poor industry. This chapter covers frameworks that analysts use to judge industry attractiveness: structure and concentration, Porter's five forces, life cycle stages, regulation, demand drivers and cost structures. Questions ask you to apply frameworks to scenarios or interpret concentration data.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Industry structure

Structures range from perfect competition (many sellers, no pricing power) through monopolistic competition and oligopoly (few large players) to monopoly. Indian telecom and cement have oligopolistic features; many textile segments are fragmented. Concentrated industries often have better pricing power and margins.

2. Measuring concentration

The concentration ratio CR4 adds the market shares of the four largest firms. The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index sums the squares of all firms' market shares. Four firms with 25% each give HHI = 4 × 625 = 2,500, indicating high concentration. A higher HHI implies less competition.

3. Porter's five forces

Industry profitability depends on threat of new entrants, bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of buyers, threat of substitutes and rivalry among existing firms. Strong forces erode profits. For example, airlines face intense rivalry, powerful suppliers (aircraft makers, fuel) and price-sensitive buyers.

4. Barriers to entry

High capital requirements, regulatory licences, economies of scale, brand loyalty, distribution networks and switching costs protect incumbents. Banking licences and spectrum in telecom are regulatory barriers; brand and distribution protect FMCG leaders.

5. Industry life cycle

Stages: pioneering (high growth, many failures), expansion or growth (rapid growth, improving profits), maturity or stabilisation (slower growth, consolidation, steady cash flows) and decline (shrinking demand). Valuation approaches differ: growth stage focuses on revenue scaling, mature stage on cash flows and dividends.

6. SWOT and PESTLE

SWOT lists internal strengths and weaknesses and external opportunities and threats. PESTLE scans political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental factors. Both are checklists to ensure nothing important is missed, not substitutes for numbers.

7. Cost structure and operating leverage

Industries with high fixed costs, such as steel, cement and telecom, have high operating leverage: profits rise sharply with volume growth and fall sharply in downturns. Industries with variable costs show more stable margins. Analysts track capacity utilisation for such industries.

8. Demand drivers and metrics

Each industry has key metrics: average revenue per user in telecom, same-store sales growth in retail, net interest margin in banking, realisation per tonne in cement and order book in capital goods. Knowing the metrics helps identify what really drives earnings.

9. Regulation

Regulated industries such as power, banking, telecom and pharmaceuticals face price controls, licensing and compliance costs, and policy risk. Regulatory changes can quickly alter industry economics, so analysts monitor policy announcements and regulators' consultation papers.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • HHI = Σ (market share of each firm in %)²; e.g. shares of 40, 30, 20, 10 give 1,600 + 900 + 400 + 100 = 3,000.

Common traps in the exam

  • Adding market shares for HHI. HHI uses the sum of squared market shares.
  • Assuming a fast-growing industry is always attractive. Intense rivalry or low entry barriers can destroy returns.
  • Confusing SWOT's internal and external elements. Strengths and weaknesses are internal; opportunities and threats are external.
  • Ignoring operating leverage. High fixed-cost industries have volatile profits.

Quick recap

Assess industry structure and concentration (CR4, HHI as sum of squared shares). Porter's five forces: entrants, suppliers, buyers, substitutes, rivalry. Life cycle: pioneering, growth, maturity, decline. SWOT is internal plus external; PESTLE scans the environment. High fixed costs mean high operating leverage. Learn industry metrics.
