Qualities of a Good Research Report: NISM Series XV revision notes

A research report is only useful if readers can understand it, trust it and act on it. This chapter covers the essential sections of a report, what makes the thesis credible, mandatory disclosures and presentation standards. Questions often ask what must be included, or which statement in a report would be misleading or non-compliant.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Clear investment thesis

The report should state upfront why the stock is a buy, sell or hold, in a few key arguments backed by evidence. For example: 'Market share gains in two-wheelers and margin recovery from lower steel costs should lift EPS by 25% over two years'. Readers should not have to hunt for the conclusion.

2. Accurate, sourced data

Data must be accurate, current and sourced: company filings, industry data, government statistics or the analyst's own estimates clearly labelled as such. Errors in basic numbers destroy credibility, and misleading data can violate regulations.

3. Sound valuation

The report should explain the valuation method, key assumptions (growth, margins, discount rate) and how the target price is derived. Sensitivity of the target to key assumptions helps readers judge reliability. Valuation should be consistent with the thesis and the forecasts.

4. Rating, target price and time frame

A clear rating with a target price and the time frame (for example, 12 months) must be stated, along with the rating definitions used by the firm. Ratings should be consistent: a 'buy' with a target below the current price would confuse readers.

5. Key risks

Every report must explain the main risks to the thesis, such as raw material price spikes, regulatory changes, execution delays or competitive pressure. Highlighting risks shows balance and helps readers judge the recommendation.

6. Mandatory disclosures

SEBI rules require disclosure of the analyst's and firm's financial interest in the company, holdings of 1% or more, material conflicts of interest, compensation received from the subject company in the past twelve months, whether the firm managed an issue for it, and whether the analyst served as an officer or director. Disclosures must be prominent, not hidden.

7. Fact vs opinion

Reports must clearly distinguish facts (reported revenue, regulatory decisions) from opinions and forecasts (expected growth, target price). Presenting opinions as certainties, or implying assured returns, is misleading.

8. Plain language and structure

A good report uses simple, precise language, avoids unnecessary jargon, puts key messages first and uses charts and tables to support points. Consistent formatting across reports helps readers find information quickly.

9. Timeliness and consistency

Research should be updated promptly after results, major events or rating changes. Contradictions between a firm's reports, or between written and verbal views, damage trust. Reports should be distributed fairly to all clients simultaneously.

Common traps in the exam

  • Hiding disclosures in tiny print or omitting them. Disclosures are mandatory and must be clear.
  • Giving a target price without a time frame or method. Both are expected.
  • Presenting forecasts as facts. Opinions must be identified as such.
  • Leaving out risks to appear confident. Key risks are an essential section.

Quick recap

A good report has a clear thesis, accurate sourced data, transparent valuation, a rating with target price and time frame, key risks and mandatory disclosures (holdings ≥1%, conflicts, compensation, past issue management). Separate fact from opinion, write plainly, update promptly and distribute fairly.
