Corporate Valuation: NISM Series XV revision notes

Valuation turns analysis into a target price. This chapter covers absolute methods (discounted cash flow and dividend discount models), relative valuation using multiples, sum-of-the-parts, cost of capital and terminal value. Questions are numerical and conceptual: compute a value, or identify which method suits which company and why a multiple is high or low.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Free cash flow to firm

FCFF = EBIT × (1 − tax rate) + depreciation − capital expenditure − increase in working capital. Discounting FCFF at WACC gives enterprise value; subtracting net debt gives equity value. FCFF is preferred when leverage is expected to change.

2. Free cash flow to equity

FCFE = FCFF − interest × (1 − tax rate) + net borrowing. Discounting FCFE at the cost of equity gives equity value directly. It suits companies with stable leverage, such as many mature businesses.

3. Cost of capital

Cost of equity is usually estimated with CAPM: risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium. WACC = weight of equity × cost of equity + weight of debt × cost of debt × (1 − tax rate). For example, 60% equity at 13% and 40% debt at 9% pre-tax with 25% tax gives WACC = 7.8% + 2.7% = 10.5%.

4. Terminal value

Most DCF value often comes from the terminal value. Using the Gordon growth formula, TV = FCF in year n+1 ÷ (discount rate − long-term growth). Small changes in growth or discount rate swing value sharply, so analysts run sensitivity tables and keep long-term growth below nominal GDP growth.

5. Dividend discount model

For stable dividend payers, value = D1 ÷ (r − g). If next year's dividend is ₹10, required return 12% and growth 7%, value = 10 ÷ 0.05 = ₹200. The model fails when growth is close to or above the required return, and suits utilities or mature banks better than growth companies.

6. P/E and P/B

P/E compares price with earnings and suits companies with stable positive profits. P/B compares price with book value and suits banks and financial companies whose assets are marked close to market. A justified P/B rises with ROE relative to cost of equity.

7. EV/EBITDA and EV/Sales

EV/EBITDA neutralises differences in capital structure and depreciation policies, making it useful for capital-intensive sectors such as telecom, cement and power. EV/Sales is used for early-stage or loss-making companies, with caution, since sales do not guarantee profits.

8. PEG ratio

PEG = P/E ÷ expected earnings growth rate (in %). A stock with P/E 30 and 20% growth has PEG 1.5. PEG helps compare growth stocks, but depends heavily on the growth estimate.

9. Sum of the parts

For conglomerates and holding companies, analysts value each business separately, using the most suitable method for each, add listed investments at market value (often with a holding company discount), and subtract net debt at the parent. This is common for Indian diversified groups.

10. What drives multiples

Higher growth, higher return on capital and lower risk justify higher multiples. A company growing fast but earning below its cost of capital destroys value as it grows, so a high multiple is not justified.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • FCFF = EBIT(1 − t) + D&A − capex − ΔWC; FCFE = FCFF − interest(1 − t) + net borrowing.
  • WACC = We × Ke + Wd × Kd × (1 − t).
  • Terminal value = FCF(n+1) ÷ (r − g); value per share (DDM) = D1 ÷ (r − g).

Common traps in the exam

  • Discounting FCFF at the cost of equity. FCFF uses WACC; FCFE uses cost of equity.
  • Applying DDM when growth exceeds the discount rate. The formula breaks down.
  • Using P/E for loss-making companies. Use EV/Sales or other measures instead.
  • Forgetting to subtract net debt when moving from enterprise value to equity value.

Quick recap

FCFF at WACC gives EV; FCFE at cost of equity gives equity value. WACC uses after-tax cost of debt. Terminal value dominates DCF, so test sensitivity. DDM: D1 ÷ (r − g). P/B for banks, EV/EBITDA for capital-intensive firms, PEG for growth stocks, SOTP for conglomerates. Growth, ROCE and risk drive multiples.
