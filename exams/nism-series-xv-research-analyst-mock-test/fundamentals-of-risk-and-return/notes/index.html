Fundamentals of Risk and Return: NISM Series XV revision notes

Analysts must measure returns correctly and understand which risks investors are paid to bear. This chapter covers return calculations, expected return from scenarios, variance and standard deviation, covariance and correlation, beta, diversification, CAPM and risk-adjusted performance measures. Most questions are calculations, so learn the formulas well.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Holding period return

HPR = (ending price − beginning price + income) ÷ beginning price. Buying at ₹200, receiving a ₹5 dividend and selling at ₹230 gives HPR = 35 ÷ 200 = 17.5%. For multi-year periods, annualise using CAGR = (ending ÷ beginning)^(1/n) − 1.

2. Expected return

Expected return is the probability-weighted average of possible outcomes. If a stock has 30% chance of 20%, 50% chance of 10% and 20% chance of −10%, expected return = 6% + 5% − 2% = 9%.

3. Variance and standard deviation

Variance measures dispersion of returns around the mean; standard deviation is its square root and is expressed in the same units as returns. A higher standard deviation means a wider range of likely outcomes and higher total risk.

4. Covariance and correlation

Covariance shows whether two assets' returns move together. Correlation standardises covariance to a range of −1 to +1. Combining assets with low or negative correlation reduces portfolio risk without proportionately reducing return; this is the basis of diversification.

5. Systematic vs unsystematic risk

Unsystematic risk is specific to a company or industry, such as a plant fire or management change, and can be diversified away. Systematic risk arises from market-wide factors such as interest rates and economic cycles and cannot be diversified away.

6. Beta

Beta measures a stock's sensitivity to market movements: covariance of stock with market ÷ variance of market. A beta of 1.3 means the stock tends to move 1.3% for a 1% market move. Defensive stocks have betas below 1; cyclical stocks often above 1.

7. CAPM

Expected return = risk-free rate + beta × (market return − risk-free rate). With a risk-free rate of 7%, market return of 12% and beta 1.2, expected return = 7% + 1.2 × 5% = 13%. CAPM says only systematic risk is rewarded because unsystematic risk can be diversified.

8. Sharpe and Treynor ratios

Sharpe ratio = (portfolio return − risk-free rate) ÷ standard deviation, measuring excess return per unit of total risk. Treynor ratio = (portfolio return − risk-free rate) ÷ beta, measuring excess return per unit of systematic risk. Sharpe suits undiversified portfolios; Treynor suits well-diversified ones.

9. Jensen's alpha

Alpha = actual return − CAPM expected return. A portfolio returning 15% when CAPM predicts 13% has alpha of 2%, suggesting value added by the manager after adjusting for market risk.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • CAPM: E(R) = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf).
  • Beta = Cov(stock, market) ÷ Var(market).
  • Two-asset variance = w1²σ1² + w2²σ2² + 2w1w2ρσ1σ2.

Common traps in the exam

  • Thinking diversification removes all risk. It removes only unsystematic risk.
  • Using standard deviation in Treynor. Treynor uses beta; Sharpe uses standard deviation.
  • Adding variances of two assets to get portfolio variance. Covariance terms must be included.
  • Assuming a beta of 0 means no risk. It means no market risk; the stock may still be volatile.

Quick recap

HPR includes income; annualise with CAGR. Expected return is probability-weighted. SD measures total risk; correlation drives diversification. Only systematic risk (beta) is priced under CAPM: Rf + β(Rm − Rf). Sharpe uses SD, Treynor uses beta, Jensen's alpha is actual minus CAPM return.
