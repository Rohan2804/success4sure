Company Analysis – Business & Governance: NISM Series XV revision notes

Numbers tell what a company has done; qualitative analysis helps judge whether it can keep doing it. This chapter covers business models, competitive advantages, management quality, capital allocation and corporate governance, including promoter behaviour and ESG. Expect scenario questions in which you must spot a governance red flag despite attractive financials.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Business model

Understand how the company makes money: what it sells, to whom, at what price, through which channels and with what cost structure. A subscription software firm with recurring revenue has very different economics from a project-based engineering firm with lumpy order inflows.

2. Competitive advantages or moats

Durable advantages include brand strength (pricing power in FMCG), network effects (exchanges, payment platforms), switching costs (enterprise software, banking relationships), cost advantages (scale in cement or steel) and regulatory licences. Moats allow returns on capital to stay above the cost of capital for long periods.

3. Management quality

Assess track record in executing plans, honesty in communication, consistency between guidance and results, depth of the second line of management and succession planning. Management that blames external factors for every miss or frequently changes strategy deserves caution.

4. Capital allocation

Good managements reinvest in high-return projects, avoid value-destroying acquisitions, return surplus cash through dividends or buybacks and keep leverage sensible. Repeated equity dilution at low prices or diversification into unrelated businesses are warning signs.

5. Board and independence

An effective board has genuinely independent directors with relevant expertise, an independent audit committee and transparent remuneration. SEBI's LODR rules set minimum independence requirements, but analysts look beyond compliance to substance.

6. Related-party transactions

Large or unusual dealings with promoter-group entities, such as purchases at above-market prices, loans to group firms or royalty payments to a promoter entity, can transfer value away from minority shareholders. Analysts read RPT disclosures and shareholder approvals carefully.

7. Promoter pledging

When promoters pledge a large share of their holdings for loans, a fall in share price can trigger margin calls and forced selling, leading to sharp price declines and even change of control. Rising pledge levels are a red flag about promoter finances.

8. Other governance red flags

Frequent auditor resignations, qualified audit opinions, delayed results, complex group structures, aggressive accounting, high management turnover and regulatory actions all signal risk. An attractive valuation does not compensate for poor governance.

9. ESG considerations

Environmental, social and governance factors affect long-term risk and cost of capital. Top listed companies publish a Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report. Analysts look at emissions, safety records, labour practices, data privacy and governance, especially in sectors exposed to regulation or reputational risk.

Common traps in the exam

  • Valuing a company with weak governance at the same multiple as peers. Governance risk warrants a discount or avoidance.
  • Treating high growth as proof of a moat. Moats show up in sustained high returns on capital.
  • Ignoring rising promoter pledging. It can force selling and price crashes.
  • Assuming legal compliance means good governance. Substance matters beyond minimum rules.

Quick recap

Understand the business model and moat (brand, network, switching costs, cost, licences). Judge management by track record, candour and capital allocation. Watch boards, related-party transactions, pledging, auditor changes and accounting quality. ESG affects risk and cost of capital. Good financials do not cancel governance red flags.
