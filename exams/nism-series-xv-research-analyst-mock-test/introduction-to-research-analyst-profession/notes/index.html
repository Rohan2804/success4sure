Introduction to Research Analyst Profession: NISM Series XV revision notes

The Research Analyst exam opens by describing what analysts do and why their independence matters. Questions test the different types of analysts, who uses research, the steps of the research process, the skills required and the ethical foundations of the profession. These ideas recur in the regulatory chapter, so build a clear picture early.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. What an analyst does

A research analyst studies economies, industries and companies, builds forecasts and valuations, and communicates conclusions as recommendations or opinions about securities. The output helps investors decide whether to buy, sell or hold, and helps markets price securities more efficiently.

2. Sell-side analysts

Sell-side analysts work for brokers and investment banks and publish research for clients, often with buy, sell or hold ratings and target prices. Their research supports the broker's business, which creates potential conflicts with investment banking and trading desks that regulation seeks to control.

3. Buy-side analysts

Buy-side analysts work for mutual funds, insurers, PMS and other asset managers, and their research is used internally to make investment decisions. Their work is usually not published. Success is measured by the performance of the portfolio rather than by publicity.

4. Independent analysts

Independent research firms and individual analysts sell research directly to investors or institutions without investment banking ties. Their independence is a selling point, but they still need SEBI registration and must follow disclosure and conflict rules.

5. Users of research

Research is used by retail and institutional investors, fund managers, company managements benchmarking peers, lenders evaluating credit, and regulators and policymakers watching sectors. Different users need different depth: a retail investor wants a clear thesis; a fund manager wants detailed models.

6. Research process

Typical steps: define the question, collect data, analyse the economy, industry and company, build financial models and forecasts, value the security, form a recommendation, write the report and monitor after publication. Updating views when facts change is part of the process.

7. Skills required

Analysts need accounting and finance knowledge, quantitative and modelling skills, industry understanding, scepticism, communication and writing skills, and the discipline to separate facts from opinions. Interpersonal skills help in management meetings and channel checks, but must never compromise objectivity.

8. Independence and objectivity

Recommendations must be based on research, not on pressure from issuers, bankers, sales teams or personal holdings. Analysts should not let investment banking deals, brokerage revenue or their own trades influence ratings. Regulation enforces this through separation, disclosure and trading restrictions.

9. Confidentiality and fair dealing

Analysts often access sensitive information. They must not use or pass on unpublished price-sensitive information, must treat all clients fairly when releasing research, and must not selectively give advance notice of rating changes to favoured clients.

Common traps in the exam

  • Assuming buy-side research is published. It is usually used internally by the asset manager.
  • Thinking independent analysts need no registration. Anyone providing research for consideration needs SEBI registration.
  • Treating management access as a reason to soften ratings. Objectivity must not depend on relationships.
  • Believing the process ends at publication. Analysts must monitor and update their views.

Quick recap

Analysts turn data into recommendations. Sell-side publishes for broker clients; buy-side serves its own fund; independents sell research directly. Process: question, data, analysis, model, valuation, report, monitor. Independence, objectivity, confidentiality and fair dealing are the ethical core.
