Terminology in Equity and Debt Markets: NISM Series XV revision notes

This chapter is about the vocabulary and simple formulas analysts use every day. You must be able to compute and interpret each measure, see how corporate actions change per-share figures, and understand how bond prices, coupons and yields relate. Many questions are quick calculations, so precision matters more than speed.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Face value, book value and market price

Face value is the nominal value printed on a share, such as ₹10. Book value per share is shareholders' equity divided by shares outstanding. Market price is what the share trades at. A share with ₹10 face value, ₹150 book value and ₹600 price trades at four times book.

2. EPS and P/E

Earnings per share = net profit attributable to equity shareholders ÷ weighted average shares. P/E = market price ÷ EPS. If profit is ₹200 crore and there are 10 crore shares, EPS is ₹20; at a price of ₹400, P/E is 20. Earnings yield is the inverse: EPS ÷ price = 5%.

3. Dividends

Dividend per share is the cash dividend declared per share. Dividend yield = DPS ÷ market price. Payout ratio = DPS ÷ EPS; retention ratio = 1 − payout. A company with EPS ₹20 paying ₹6 has a 30% payout and retains 70%. Dividends are declared as a percentage of face value in India, which confuses beginners.

4. Market capitalisation and EV

Market cap = shares outstanding × market price. Enterprise value = market cap + debt + minority interest + preference capital − cash and equivalents. EV represents the value of the whole business to all capital providers and is used with EBITDA.

5. Corporate actions and per-share data

A 1:1 bonus doubles shares and halves EPS, price and book value per share, leaving totals unchanged. A split from ₹10 to ₹2 face value multiplies shares by five. Analysts must adjust historical per-share data for such actions to keep comparisons valid.

6. Bond basics

Face value is the amount repaid at maturity; coupon is the annual interest rate on face value; current yield = annual coupon ÷ market price. A ₹100 bond with 8% coupon priced at ₹95 has current yield of 8.42%. YTM is the internal rate of return if held to maturity.

7. Price-yield relationship

Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions. When market yields rise above the coupon, the bond trades at a discount; when they fall below, at a premium. A bond trades at par only when its YTM equals its coupon.

8. Duration

Modified duration estimates the percentage price change for a 1% change in yield. A bond with modified duration 5 falls about 5% if yields rise by 1%. Longer maturity and lower coupons increase duration and interest rate risk.

9. Credit ratings and spreads

Ratings from agencies like CRISIL, ICRA and CARE indicate default risk, from AAA (highest safety) down to D (default). The credit spread is the extra yield over a G-sec of similar maturity. Spreads widen when credit risk rises or markets become risk-averse.

Formulas and rules of thumb

  • % bond price change ≈ − modified duration × change in yield (in % points).
  • P/B = market price ÷ book value per share; earnings yield = EPS ÷ price.

Common traps in the exam

  • Confusing payout ratio with dividend yield. Payout uses EPS; yield uses market price.
  • Forgetting to adjust EPS history for bonus issues and splits.
  • Assuming a high coupon bond always trades at a premium. It trades at a premium only if market yield is below the coupon.
  • Computing current yield on face value. Current yield uses the market price.

Quick recap

EPS = profit ÷ shares; P/E = price ÷ EPS; earnings yield is its inverse. Payout = DPS ÷ EPS; dividend yield = DPS ÷ price. EV = market cap + debt − cash. Bonus and splits change per-share data, not totals. Bond prices move inversely to yields; current yield = coupon ÷ price; modified duration estimates price sensitivity.
