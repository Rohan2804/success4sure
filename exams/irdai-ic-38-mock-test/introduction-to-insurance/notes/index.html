Introduction to Insurance: IRDAI IC-38 revision notes

The opening chapter of the IC-38 syllabus explains what insurance is, how it works through pooling of risk, and how the industry developed in India. Questions test basic definitions, key historical milestones, methods of managing risk and terms like peril, hazard and reinsurance. Most questions are direct and quick, so clarity on vocabulary earns easy marks in the 50-question paper.

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Pooling of risk

Insurance collects small premiums from many people exposed to similar risks and pays the few who suffer a loss. If 1,000 houses worth ₹20 lakh each face a 1 in 1,000 chance of fire in a year, a premium of a little over ₹2,000 each can fund one total loss. The law of large numbers makes losses predictable for the group.

2. Peril and hazard

A peril is the cause of loss, such as fire, flood, theft or death. A hazard increases the chance or severity of loss. Physical hazards are tangible (storing fuel near a kitchen); moral hazard comes from dishonesty or carelessness of the insured, such as overinsuring and then causing a loss.

3. Managing risk

Risks can be avoided (not doing the activity), retained (bearing small losses yourself), reduced (fire extinguishers, safe driving), or transferred (insurance). Insurance suits risks with low probability but high severity. Small, frequent losses are usually better retained.

4. Insurable risks

An insurable risk involves a large number of similar exposures, an accidental and measurable loss, a premium that is affordable, and a loss that is not catastrophic for the insurer as a whole. Speculative risks, where gain is possible (like betting), are not insurable; only pure risks are.

5. History in India

Oriental Life Insurance Company was set up in Kolkata in 1818. Life insurance was nationalised in 1956, forming LIC. General insurance was nationalised in 1972 under GIC and four subsidiaries. The Malhotra Committee (1993) recommended opening the sector, and the IRDA Act, 1999 created the regulator and allowed private insurers.

6. Role of IRDAI

The Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India registers insurers and intermediaries, protects policyholders' interests, frames regulations on products, solvency and conduct, and promotes orderly growth of the industry. It is a statutory body under the IRDA Act, 1999.

7. Reinsurance

Reinsurance is insurance for insurers: an insurer passes part of a large risk to a reinsurer to protect its own solvency. GIC Re is India's national reinsurer, and foreign reinsurers operate through branches. Policyholders deal only with the original insurer.

8. Insurance as a social good

Insurance protects families from financial ruin, supports lending (banks need collateral insured), encourages enterprise, and channels long-term savings into infrastructure and government securities. Life insurers are among the largest investors in Indian government bonds.

Common traps in the exam

  • Peril is the cause of loss; hazard is a condition that increases it. Fire is a peril; storing petrol is a hazard.
  • Only pure risks (loss or no loss) are insurable, not speculative risks.
  • Insurance transfers risk; it does not prevent the loss from happening.
  • LIC was formed in 1956; general insurance was nationalised later, in 1972.

Quick recap

Insurance pools premiums from many to pay the few who suffer losses, relying on the law of large numbers. Peril causes loss; hazard increases it; moral hazard comes from behaviour. Manage risk by avoiding, retaining, reducing or transferring it. Only pure risks are insurable. Key dates: 1818 Oriental Life, 1956 LIC, 1972 GIC, 1999 IRDA Act. Reinsurance protects insurers.
