Health Insurance: IRDAI IC-38 revision notes

Health insurance is a major part of IC-38 because agents of life, general and health insurers all deal with it. This chapter covers the types of health products, key policy terms, waiting periods, claim processes through TPAs and cashless networks, standard products and tax benefits. Questions often test definitions such as day care, co-payment, sub-limit and portability.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Hospitalisation policy

The basic indemnity health policy pays medical expenses for hospitalisation of at least 24 hours, up to the sum insured. It typically covers room rent, ICU, doctors' fees, medicines and tests, plus pre-hospitalisation (often 30 days) and post-hospitalisation (often 60 days) expenses related to the illness.

2. Day care and domiciliary

Day care procedures such as cataract surgery or chemotherapy need less than 24 hours of hospitalisation due to technology, and are covered as listed in the policy. Domiciliary treatment covers treatment at home when the patient cannot be moved or a hospital bed is unavailable, subject to conditions.

3. Individual and floater

An individual policy has a separate sum insured for each person. A family floater shares one sum insured across family members, which is cheaper but can be exhausted by one member's large claim. Senior parents are often better on separate policies.

4. Waiting periods

Most policies have an initial waiting period (often 30 days, except accidents), specific waiting periods for listed diseases, and a waiting period for pre-existing diseases. IRDAI capped the pre-existing disease waiting period at three years and the moratorium period at five years in 2024; after the moratorium, claims cannot be contested except for fraud.

5. Co-payment and sub-limits

Co-payment means the insured pays a fixed percentage of each claim. Sub-limits cap amounts for room rent or specific procedures. A policy with a 1% room rent limit on ₹3 lakh cover allows ₹3,000 a day; a costlier room can lead to proportionate deduction of other charges.

6. Benefit and other products

Critical illness policies pay a fixed lump sum on diagnosis of listed illnesses regardless of actual expenses. Hospital cash pays a daily amount. Top-up and super top-up policies pay above a deductible. Standard products such as Arogya Sanjeevani have uniform features across insurers.

7. Claims, TPAs and cashless

Third party administrators process claims and manage hospital networks for insurers. In cashless claims, the hospital seeks pre-authorisation and the insurer pays directly. In reimbursement claims, the insured pays first and submits bills. IRDAI has pushed for faster cashless authorisation and discharge approval.

8. Portability and tax

Portability lets a policyholder move to another insurer with credit for waiting periods already served, if applied for before renewal. Premiums qualify for deduction under Section 80D within limits that are higher for senior citizens (old tax regime). Preventive health check-ups are included within the limit.

Common traps in the exam

  • Critical illness is a benefit policy paying a fixed sum; hospitalisation policies are indemnity.
  • Day care procedures are covered even though hospitalisation is under 24 hours.
  • A floater shares one sum insured; one large claim reduces cover for all members.
  • Portability transfers waiting period credit, not unused sum insured.

Quick recap

Basic health policies indemnify hospitalisation of 24+ hours plus pre and post expenses; day care and domiciliary are special cases. Individual vs floater sum insured. Waiting periods: initial, specific, pre-existing (max three years); five-year moratorium. Co-pay and sub-limits reduce claims. Critical illness pays a lump sum. TPAs handle cashless and reimbursement. Portability keeps waiting credit; 80D gives tax benefit.
