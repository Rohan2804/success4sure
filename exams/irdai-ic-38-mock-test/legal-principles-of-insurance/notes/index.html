Legal Principles of Insurance: IRDAI IC-38 revision notes

Insurance policies are legal contracts, and IC-38 tests the principles that govern them. This chapter covers the elements of a valid contract, the special principles of insurance such as utmost good faith and insurable interest, the indemnity principles used in general insurance, and Section 45 of the Insurance Act. Questions often describe a situation and ask which principle applies.

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Valid contract elements

Under the Indian Contract Act, a valid contract needs offer and acceptance, lawful consideration, competent parties (adult, sound mind, not disqualified), free consent (no coercion, fraud, misrepresentation) and a lawful object. In insurance, the proposal is the offer and the issue of the policy is acceptance; premium is consideration.

2. Utmost good faith

Insurance contracts require uberrimae fidei: both parties must disclose all material facts voluntarily and truthfully. The proposer knows more about their health, habits and property, so non-disclosure of a heart condition or a previous fire can make the policy voidable.

3. Material facts

A material fact is one that would influence the insurer's decision to accept the risk or fix the premium: health history, occupation, smoking, other policies, previous claims. Facts that reduce risk, or that the insurer already knows, need not be disclosed.

4. Insurable interest

The insured must stand to suffer a financial loss from the event. A person has insurable interest in their own life and their spouse's; an employer in a key employee; a creditor in a debtor up to the debt. In life insurance it must exist at the start; in marine insurance at the time of loss; in other general insurance at both times.

5. Indemnity

General insurance aims to restore the insured to the same financial position as before the loss, not to allow profit. If a ₹5 lakh car is damaged by ₹80,000, the insurer pays up to the loss (subject to deductibles), not the sum insured. Life insurance is not a contract of indemnity because life cannot be valued.

6. Subrogation and contribution

Subrogation lets the insurer, after paying a claim, step into the insured's shoes and recover from a third party responsible for the loss. Contribution applies when the same property is insured with more than one insurer: each pays its rateable share, so the insured does not recover more than the loss.

7. Proximate cause

The insurer pays only if the proximate cause, the dominant and effective cause, is an insured peril. If a fire (insured) causes a wall to collapse later, the fire is the proximate cause. If the main cause is an excluded peril, the claim may be rejected.

8. Section 45

Under Section 45 of the Insurance Act, no life policy can be called in question on any ground after three years from the date of issue, risk commencement, revival or rider, whichever is later. Within three years, the insurer may repudiate for fraud or non-disclosure of material facts, with reasons in writing.

Common traps in the exam

  • Utmost good faith binds both parties, not only the proposer.
  • Life insurance is not a contract of indemnity; subrogation and contribution do not apply to it.
  • Insurable interest in marine insurance must exist at the time of loss, not necessarily at the start.
  • After three years, a life policy cannot be questioned even for fraud under Section 45.

Quick recap

Valid contract: offer, acceptance, consideration, competent parties, free consent, lawful object. Insurance adds utmost good faith (disclose material facts), insurable interest (timing differs for life, marine, general), indemnity (no profit from loss), subrogation, contribution and proximate cause. Life insurance is not indemnity. Section 45: no questioning a life policy after three years.
