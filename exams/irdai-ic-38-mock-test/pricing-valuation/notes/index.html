Pricing & Valuation: IRDAI IC-38 revision notes

This chapter explains, without heavy maths, how life insurers set premiums and value their liabilities. It covers the elements of premium, the difference between net and office premium, rebates and loadings, how surplus arises and is shared as bonuses, and the role of the appointed actuary. Questions are conceptual: identify which factor raises or lowers premium, or what a bonus type means.

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Elements of premium

A life insurance premium is built from mortality (the expected cost of claims based on the probability of death at each age), interest (premiums are invested, so expected investment earnings reduce the premium), expenses (commission, administration, medicals) and a margin for contingencies and, in participating plans, bonus loading.

2. Mortality tables

Insurers use mortality tables showing the probability of death at each age, based on the experience of insured lives in India (for example the IALM tables). Higher age means higher mortality and so higher premium for the same cover. Women are often charged lower rates because of longer life expectancy.

3. Interest assumption

If the insurer assumes a higher interest rate on invested premiums, the premium can be lower. Insurers use conservative interest assumptions so that they can meet guarantees even if returns fall.

4. Net and office premium

Net premium covers only mortality and interest. Office (gross) premium adds loadings for expenses, contingencies and bonuses. The policyholder pays the office premium. A participating policy has a higher office premium than an equivalent non-participating one because of bonus loading.

5. Rebates and loadings

Rebates are given for higher sum assured (lower expense per ₹1,000 of cover) and for annual or half-yearly modes instead of monthly. Extra premium is loaded for substandard lives such as those with health conditions or hazardous occupations. Online purchase may also carry a lower premium.

6. Surplus

Surplus arises when actual experience is better than assumed: fewer deaths than expected, higher investment returns or lower expenses. The appointed actuary determines surplus through valuation, and a large share of surplus from participating business is distributed to participating policyholders.

7. Types of bonus

Simple reversionary bonus is declared as a rate per ₹1,000 sum assured and added each year; compound reversionary bonus is calculated on the sum assured plus existing bonuses. Terminal bonus is paid once at maturity or death for long-held policies. Interim bonus covers the period between the last valuation and the claim.

8. Valuation and solvency

The appointed actuary values liabilities periodically using prudent assumptions to check that assets are enough to meet future claims. IRDAI requires insurers to maintain a minimum solvency ratio (currently 150%), meaning available solvency margin must exceed the required margin with a buffer.

Common traps in the exam

  • A higher interest assumption lowers the premium; higher mortality or expense raises it.
  • Net premium excludes expense loading; office premium is what the customer pays.
  • Compound reversionary bonus is calculated on sum assured plus attached bonuses; simple is on sum assured only.
  • Surplus comes from experience better than assumptions, not from simply charging more.

Quick recap

Premium elements: mortality, interest, expenses, contingency and bonus loading. Net premium = mortality and interest; office premium adds loadings. Rebates for high sum assured and annual mode; extra premium for substandard lives. Surplus arises from better-than-expected experience and funds bonuses: simple or compound reversionary, terminal, interim. Appointed actuary values liabilities; solvency ratio 150%.
