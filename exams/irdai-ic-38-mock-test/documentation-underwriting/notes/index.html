Documentation & Underwriting: IRDAI IC-38 revision notes

This chapter carries a large share of IC-38 marks. It covers the documents used from prospecting to policy issue, and how insurers decide whether to accept a risk and on what terms. Questions test the purpose of each document, the agent's responsibilities, and underwriting outcomes such as standard acceptance, extra premium, postponement and decline.

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Prospectus and sales literature

The prospectus or product brochure describes the plan's benefits, conditions, exclusions and charges, and must be approved under IRDAI rules. Agents must not modify or make claims beyond it. A benefit illustration shows projected benefits at assumed rates, which are not guaranteed.

2. Proposal form

The proposal form is the basis of the contract. It records personal details, occupation, income, health, habits, family history, other insurance and nominee details. The proposer must answer truthfully and sign. If the agent fills it, the agent must read it back to the proposer and record a declaration.

3. KYC and age proof

Insurers verify identity, address and age through officially valid documents under PMLA requirements. Standard age proofs include birth certificates, school certificates and passports; non-standard proofs may lead to an extra premium or restrictions. Age misstatement can change benefits.

4. First premium receipt

When the proposal is accepted and the first premium is received, the insurer issues a first premium receipt, which is evidence that the contract has commenced (subject to any conditions). Renewal premium receipts are issued for later premiums.

5. Policy document

The policy document is evidence of the contract. It contains the policy schedule (names, dates, sum assured, premium, term, nominee), standard provisions, special conditions and exclusions. It must be read with the proposal form, which is part of the contract.

6. Endorsements

Changes to a policy after issue, such as change of address, nominee, assignment or correction of age, are made by endorsement. The endorsement becomes part of the policy and overrides earlier terms where they conflict.

7. Underwriting process

Underwriting assesses the risk to decide acceptance and terms. Field underwriting by agents provides first-hand information. Medical underwriting looks at health, tests and history; financial underwriting checks that cover is reasonable compared with income, to prevent over-insurance and moral hazard.

8. Underwriting outcomes

A proposal can be accepted at standard rates, accepted with an extra premium, accepted with a lien (reduced benefit in early years), accepted with reduced sum assured or restricted terms, postponed (for example after recent surgery), or declined. Substandard lives are those with higher than normal risk.

9. Non-medical underwriting

For lower sums assured and younger ages, insurers may issue policies without medical examination, relying on the declaration of good health. Higher sums and older ages trigger medical tests. Honest disclosure is crucial because claims can be contested within three years.

Common traps in the exam

  • The proposal form is part of the policy contract; misstatements in it affect claims.
  • Benefit illustrations show projections, not guaranteed amounts.
  • Postponement is a temporary decision, not a decline.
  • Financial underwriting guards against over-insurance relative to income, not just health risk.

Quick recap

Prospectus and benefit illustration explain the product; the proposal form, signed by the proposer, is the basis of the contract. KYC and age proof are mandatory. First premium receipt evidences commencement; the policy document holds the schedule, conditions and exclusions; endorsements record later changes. Underwriting is medical, financial and field-based; outcomes include standard, extra premium, lien, postponement and decline.
