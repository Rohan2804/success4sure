SEBI (KYC Registration Agency) Regulations, 2011: NISM Series III-A revision notes

The KRA Regulations, 2011 created a single-KYC system for the securities market. An investor completes KYC once with any SEBI intermediary, and the record is uploaded to a KYC Registration Agency so that other intermediaries can rely on it. Questions test the roles of intermediary and KRA, eligibility to become a KRA, confidentiality and the meaning of KYC statuses.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Why KRAs exist

Before 2011 an investor had to repeat KYC with every broker, DP and mutual fund. KRAs centralise KYC records so that an investor who completed KYC with a broker in Chennai can open a mutual fund folio in Delhi without resubmitting documents. This reduces duplication and inconsistency across intermediaries.

2. Who can be a KRA

A KRA must be registered with SEBI and must meet net worth and infrastructure requirements. Depositories and their subsidiaries and other entities meeting SEBI's criteria have operated KRAs; examples include CDSL Ventures (CVL) and NSDL's KRA. Registration follows the common process under the Intermediaries Regulations.

3. Intermediary's duties

The intermediary performs initial KYC: collects documents, does in-person verification (or approved video/digital verification), and uploads the details and scanned documents to the KRA system within the time SEBI specifies. It remains responsible for the accuracy of what it uploads and must keep original records.

4. KRA's duties

The KRA stores KYC records securely, allows intermediaries to access them, sends confirmation to the investor on upload and on modification, and validates key attributes such as PAN, name, address and mobile or email against official sources. It must maintain confidentiality and audit trails of every access.

5. Reliance by other intermediaries

A second intermediary can download the record from the KRA and need not repeat full KYC, though it may perform additional due diligence and must still do its own risk categorisation and in-person verification where required. It cannot use the KRA data for any purpose other than KYC.

6. KYC statuses

Records can show statuses such as registered, validated, on hold or rejected. A record goes on hold when, for example, PAN is not linked with Aadhaar or the email or mobile could not be verified, and the investor cannot transact in new accounts until it is fixed. Status names and conditions are updated by SEBI periodically.

7. Modification of details

Changes in address, contact details or name are submitted to any intermediary, which updates the KRA. The KRA then notifies the other intermediaries holding that client, so records stay consistent. The client should not have to inform each intermediary separately.

8. Confidentiality and data protection

KRAs and intermediaries must not share KYC data with anyone except as permitted by law. They must have systems to prevent unauthorised access, maintain logs and keep records for the period specified. Using KYC data for marketing is not permitted.

Common traps in the exam

  • The intermediary, not the KRA, performs the initial KYC and in-person verification.
  • Relying on a KRA record does not remove the second intermediary's own risk-based due diligence obligations.
  • A KYC record on hold blocks new transactions until deficiencies are cured; it is not the same as rejection.
  • KRAs must notify all linked intermediaries of changes; the investor need not update each one.

Quick recap

KRAs, registered with SEBI, hold a central KYC record for securities market investors. The intermediary collects documents and verifies in person, then uploads to the KRA; the KRA validates, stores, confirms to the investor and shares with other intermediaries. Others can rely on it but still do their own risk checks. Data is confidential and changes flow to all linked intermediaries.
