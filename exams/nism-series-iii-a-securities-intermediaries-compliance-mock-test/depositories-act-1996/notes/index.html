Depositories Act, 1996: NISM Series III-A revision notes

The Depositories Act, 1996 made it possible to hold and transfer securities in electronic form. It defines the relationship between the investor, the depository participant, the depository and the issuer, and changes how ownership is recorded. Exams test the registered-versus-beneficial-owner concept, fungibility, pledge procedures and the immunity from stamp duty and Companies Act transfer rules for demat transfers.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Need for the Act

Paper certificates caused bad deliveries, forgery, loss in transit and long transfer delays. The Act allowed securities to be dematerialised and transferred by book entry. NSDL began operations in 1996 and CDSL in 1999. Since April 2019, transfer of listed securities is permitted only in demat form, except for transmission and transposition.

2. Key parties

The depository holds securities electronically and maintains records; the depository participant is the agent through which investors open accounts and give instructions; the beneficial owner is the investor; the issuer is the company whose securities are held. Each depository must be registered with SEBI and be a company formed and registered under company law.

3. Registered owner versus beneficial owner

Section 10 deems the depository to be the registered owner of securities held in demat form for the purpose of effecting transfers, but it has no voting rights or economic interest. The beneficial owner enjoys all rights and liabilities, including dividends, bonuses and voting. The issuer's register of members shows the depository; the depository's records show the beneficial owner.

4. Fungibility

Section 9 makes demat securities fungible: they have no distinctive numbers, so any 100 shares of a company in demat form are identical to any other 100 shares. This is why demat holdings show quantity, not certificate numbers.

5. Option to hold in demat

Section 8 gives the investor the option to hold securities in physical or demat form, though SEBI rules now require demat for transfer of listed securities and for public issues. An investor who wishes to dematerialise surrenders certificates through the DP; the issuer cancels them and credits the depository.

6. Pledge and hypothecation

Section 12 lets a beneficial owner pledge or hypothecate demat securities with the depository's approval. The pledgor initiates through its DP, the pledgee accepts, and the depository records the pledge. On default the pledgee can invoke the pledge and get the securities transferred. Margin pledges for brokers follow this mechanism.

7. Transfer simplified

Transfers between beneficial owners happen by book entry on instruction to the DP. No transfer deed or stamp duty under the old physical procedure is needed for transfers within the depository, though stamp duty applies on market transactions under the Indian Stamp Act as amended.

8. Liability and penalties

The depository indemnifies beneficial owners for loss caused by negligence of the depository or its participant, and may recover it from the DP. Penalties apply for failure to furnish information, failure to reconcile records and other violations. Appeals lie to SAT.

Common traps in the exam

  • The depository is the registered owner only for effecting transfer; voting rights stay with the beneficial owner.
  • Fungibility is Section 9; registered and beneficial ownership is Section 10. Don't swap them.
  • The option to hold physical shares still exists in the Act, but transfers of listed securities now must be in demat.
  • The depository compensates the investor first and then recovers from the negligent DP.

Quick recap

Depositories Act 1996 enables book-entry holding and transfer. Depository is registered owner (Section 10) but beneficial owner holds all rights. Demat securities are fungible (Section 9). Investors may choose demat (Section 8). Pledge and hypothecation via Section 12. Depository indemnifies investors for losses from its or its DP's negligence. NSDL 1996, CDSL 1999.
