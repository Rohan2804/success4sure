SEBI (Credit Rating Agencies) Regulations, 1999: NISM Series III-A revision notes

Credit rating agencies give opinions on the likelihood of timely repayment of debt. Since investors and regulators rely on these ratings, SEBI regulates who can set up a CRA, how ratings are assigned and reviewed, and how conflicts are handled. Exam questions focus on promoter eligibility, rating process steps, surveillance, restrictions on rating associates, and the nature of a rating.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. What a rating is

A credit rating is an opinion on the relative ability and willingness of an issuer to meet debt obligations on time. It is not a recommendation to buy, sell or hold, and does not comment on market price or suitability. AAA indicates the highest safety; ratings below BBB- are speculative grade.

2. Eligible promoters

A CRA must be promoted by a public financial institution, a scheduled commercial bank, a foreign bank operating in India, a foreign credit rating agency with at least five years' experience, or a company or body corporate with continuous net worth of at least ₹100 crore for the previous five years. This keeps the business with established institutions.

3. Net worth and registration

The CRA itself must be a company with minimum net worth of ₹25 crore and adequate professionals, and must be registered with SEBI. Its memorandum must include rating as a main object.

4. Restrictions on rating

A CRA cannot rate securities issued by its promoter, by entities that are its associates, or by entities in which its promoter holds a significant stake beyond the specified limit. It cannot rate an issuer if it has a conflict that impairs independence, such as consulting assignments for the same entity.

5. Rating process

The CRA signs a rating agreement, gathers information from the issuer and public sources, analysts meet management and prepare a rating note, and a rating committee assigns the rating. The issuer can appeal once with additional information. The rating, rationale and key drivers are published.

6. Surveillance

Ratings must be reviewed at least annually for the life of the instrument, and continuously monitored. If the issuer stops cooperating, the CRA continues with available information and flags the rating as 'issuer not cooperating'. Defaults must be recognised promptly as per SEBI's timelines.

7. Disclosures and rating shopping

CRAs disclose rating methodologies, default rates, transition matrices and rating history on their websites. Issuers must disclose all ratings obtained, including those not accepted, so they cannot hide unfavourable ratings by 'rating shopping'.

8. Code of conduct

CRAs must act independently, avoid conflicts, keep information confidential, separate rating from other business, and have rating committee members free from conflicts. Analysts' compensation should not depend on the revenue from the rated entity.

Common traps in the exam

  • A rating is an opinion on credit risk, not a recommendation to invest or a guarantee of repayment.
  • The ₹100 crore five-year net worth test applies to a company promoter; the CRA's own minimum net worth is ₹25 crore.
  • Non-accepted ratings must still be disclosed to stop rating shopping.
  • Ratings are reviewed at least annually, not only when the issuer requests.

Quick recap

CRAs give opinions on timely debt repayment, not buy or sell calls. Promoters: PFIs, scheduled banks, foreign banks, foreign CRAs with five years' experience, or companies with ₹100 crore net worth for five years. CRA net worth ₹25 crore. No rating of promoters or associates. Committee-based ratings, annual surveillance, disclosure of methodologies and non-accepted ratings.
