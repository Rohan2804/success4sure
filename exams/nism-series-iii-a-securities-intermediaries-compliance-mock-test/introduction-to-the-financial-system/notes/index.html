Introduction to the Financial System: NISM Series III-A revision notes

This opening chapter of NISM Series III-A sets the stage for every regulation that follows. It explains how savings travel from households to companies and government through markets, instruments and intermediaries. Questions are usually definitional: classify an instrument by market and maturity, identify whether money reaches the issuer, or pick which intermediary performs a given function. Expect three to five easy marks if the vocabulary is clear.

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Purpose of a financial system

A financial system links surplus units (mainly households) with deficit units (companies, government). It mobilises savings, allocates capital, provides payment services, spreads risk and produces prices. When a Pune salaried saver buys a corporate bond, her surplus funds a factory expansion she will never see; the system makes that transfer cheap and trusted.

2. Direct versus intermediated finance

In direct finance the saver buys the security issued by the user of funds, such as shares in an IPO. In intermediated finance an institution stands in between: a bank takes deposits and makes loans, carrying the credit risk itself. Mutual funds are a middle path: they pool money but the investor bears the market risk.

3. Primary and secondary markets

The primary market is where securities are created and sold for the first time; money flows to the issuer. The secondary market is where existing securities change hands between investors on exchanges; the issuer receives nothing. A healthy secondary market matters to issuers anyway, because investors pay more for securities they can easily sell.

4. Money market versus capital market

Money market instruments have original maturity up to one year: call money, treasury bills, commercial paper, certificates of deposit, repos. Capital market instruments fund long-term needs: equity shares, corporate bonds, government securities beyond one year. A 364-day T-bill is still money market; a 5-year NCD is capital market.

5. Main participants

Issuers (companies, governments, banks), investors (retail, HNIs, institutions, FPIs), intermediaries (stock brokers, depository participants, merchant bankers, registrars, custodians, credit rating agencies, debenture trustees), market infrastructure institutions (stock exchanges, clearing corporations, depositories) and regulators (SEBI, RBI, IRDAI, PFRDA). The rest of III-A regulates exactly these intermediaries.

6. Market infrastructure institutions

Stock exchanges provide the trading platform, clearing corporations guarantee settlement through novation and margins, and depositories hold securities in electronic form. Together they are called MIIs and are treated as systemically important, so SEBI holds them to higher net worth, governance and ownership standards than ordinary intermediaries.

7. Functions the system performs

Remember the classic list: pooling of funds, transfer of resources across time and space, risk management (insurance, derivatives, diversification), price discovery, liquidity, reduction of information and transaction costs, and a payment and settlement mechanism. Exam stems often describe one function in plain words and ask you to name it.

8. Financial instruments by claim

Equity is an ownership claim with residual, variable returns. Debt is a contractual claim with fixed or floating interest and priority over equity in liquidation. Hybrids such as convertible debentures and preference shares sit between. Derivatives derive value from an underlying asset and are used to hedge, speculate or arbitrage.

9. Why intermediaries need regulation

Intermediaries hold client money and securities and know more than their clients. That information gap and custody of assets create room for misuse, which is why each one needs SEBI registration, a capital cushion, a code of conduct, segregation of client assets and grievance redressal. This logic explains the structure of the whole III-A syllabus.

Common traps in the exam

  • An IPO and an offer for sale both happen in the primary market, but in a pure OFS the money goes to selling shareholders, not the company.
  • Original maturity decides money versus capital market: a 10-year bond with six months left to redemption is still a capital market instrument.
  • Mutual funds are intermediaries, but unlike banks they do not guarantee principal; the investor carries the market risk.
  • Clearing corporations, not stock exchanges, guarantee settlement of trades through novation.

Quick recap

Savings move from surplus to deficit units either directly through markets or through intermediaries. Primary market creates securities; secondary market trades them. Money market means up to one year original maturity; capital market is longer. Exchanges, clearing corporations and depositories are MIIs. Intermediaries are regulated because they hold client assets and information, which is the thread running through the rest of NISM III-A.
