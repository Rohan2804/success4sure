Introduction to Compliance: NISM Series III-A revision notes

Before the Act-by-Act chapters, NISM III-A explains what compliance means inside a securities intermediary: following laws, regulations, circulars and internal policies, and proving that you did. Questions test the compliance officer's role, independence, reporting lines, the difference between compliance and audit, and consequences of failure. Expect scenario stems where you choose the most appropriate compliance response.

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. What compliance means

Compliance is the process of making sure an organisation and its people follow applicable laws, regulations, exchange and depository bye-laws, circulars and internal codes. It covers both the letter (filing a report on time) and the spirit (not designing products to sidestep a rule). It is ongoing, not a one-time certification.

2. Compliance risk

Compliance risk is the risk of legal or regulatory sanctions, financial loss or loss of reputation from failing to comply. For a broker this could be a penalty for late margin reporting, suspension of trading terminals, or clients leaving after adverse media coverage. Reputation loss is often costlier than the fine.

3. The compliance officer

SEBI regulations require most intermediaries to appoint a compliance officer who monitors compliance with the Act, rules, regulations and circulars and reports non-compliance to the board or senior management and, where required, to SEBI. The officer is also usually responsible for investor grievance redressal.

4. Independence and authority

A compliance function works only if it is independent of business targets, has direct access to the board, adequate resources and the authority to see any record. A compliance head who reports to the sales head creates a conflict. Many regulators prefer a separate compliance reporting line to the board or its committee.

5. Compliance policy and manual

Good practice is a board-approved compliance policy, a manual setting out procedures, a calendar of regulatory filings, and periodic testing. New circulars should be tracked, impact-assessed and communicated to the staff who must act on them, with records showing that this was done.

6. Compliance versus internal audit

Compliance is the second line of defence: it advises, monitors and tests on a continuous basis. Internal audit is the third line: it independently reviews whether controls, including compliance, actually work. Business units are the first line and own their risks. Exams often swap these roles.

7. Training and culture

Rules are followed when staff understand them. Regular training, certification requirements (for example NISM certificates for associated persons), whistle-blower channels and visible senior management support create a compliance culture. A tick-box approach without culture tends to fail during market stress.

8. Consequences of non-compliance

SEBI and MIIs can issue warnings, levy monetary penalties, suspend or cancel registration, debar individuals and order disgorgement. Serious violations can also lead to prosecution. Repeated minor lapses can lead to a finding that the intermediary is no longer 'fit and proper'.

Common traps in the exam

  • The compliance officer advises and monitors; the board and senior management remain ultimately responsible for compliance.
  • Compliance is not the same as internal audit; audit independently checks the compliance function too.
  • Following only the letter of a circular while defeating its purpose can still be treated as a violation.
  • Fit and proper status is continuous; an intermediary can lose it after registration.

Quick recap

Compliance means following law, regulation, bye-laws and internal codes, and being able to prove it. Compliance risk covers penalties, losses and reputation. Every SEBI intermediary needs an independent compliance officer with board access, usually also handling grievances. Business is the first line, compliance the second, internal audit the third. Ultimate responsibility stays with the board.
