SEBI (Debenture Trustees) Regulations, 1993: NISM Series III-A revision notes

Debenture trustees protect the interests of debenture holders, who are often numerous and unable to monitor the issuer themselves. The Debenture Trustees Regulations, 1993 set eligibility, conflict rules and a detailed list of duties from before issue to redemption. Exam questions test who may be a DT, the restrictions on associates and what the trustee must do when the issuer defaults.

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Key points

1. Need for a trustee

When a company raises ₹500 crore through listed NCDs held by thousands of investors, no individual holder can verify that assets were charged or covenants followed. A debenture trustee acts as the single representative of all holders, monitors the issuer and enforces security on their behalf. Companies Act and SEBI rules require a DT for listed and public debt issues.

2. Who can be a DT

Scheduled banks carrying on commercial activity, public financial institutions, insurance companies and body corporates can register as debenture trustees. A body corporate needs minimum net worth as specified by SEBI (raised to ₹10 crore through amendments) and adequate infrastructure and professionals.

3. Conflict restrictions

A DT cannot act for an issue if it is an associate of the issuer, has lent money to the issuer in a way that creates conflict (unless permitted), or is a beneficial holder of the issuer's shares. Independence from the issuer is central to the role.

4. Due diligence before issue

Before the issue, the DT verifies that assets offered as security are free from encumbrances or that existing charge holders have consented, checks valuation and insurance, and confirms adequacy of security cover. It gives a due diligence certificate to be filed with the exchange and disclosed in the offer document.

5. Monitoring during the life

The DT calls for periodic reports on utilisation of funds, security cover and covenant compliance, ensures that the issuer creates the charge and executes the trust deed within the specified time, and checks that interest and principal are paid on schedule. It monitors the recovery expense fund created by issuers.

6. Action on default

On default, the DT informs debenture holders, convenes meetings, obtains their consent for enforcement, and may take steps such as enforcing security, filing under IBC or SARFAESI, or entering inter-creditor agreements. It must act with the required majority consent as specified by SEBI.

7. Duties to holders

The DT must act in the interest of holders, keep them informed, ensure that the issuer redresses complaints, and disclose relevant information on its website. It must exercise due diligence and is liable for breach of trust if it fails to act.

8. Records and compliance

DTs keep records of each issue, reports received, meetings held and actions taken, and submit periodic reports to SEBI and exchanges. A compliance officer is appointed to monitor adherence to regulations and circulars.

Common traps in the exam

  • An associate of the issuer cannot act as its debenture trustee.
  • The DT verifies and monitors security; it does not guarantee repayment to holders.
  • Enforcement on default generally needs approval of the required majority of holders.
  • Creation of charge and trust deed execution are monitored by the DT, but the obligation to create them is the issuer's.

Quick recap

Debenture trustees represent all debenture holders. Eligible: scheduled banks, PFIs, insurers and body corporates meeting net worth. No associates of the issuer. Before issue: due diligence on assets and security cover. During: monitor charge creation, covenants, fund use and payments. On default: inform holders, convene meetings, enforce security with consent. DTs do not guarantee repayment.
