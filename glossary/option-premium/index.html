What is Option Premium (Intrinsic and Time Value)?

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Option Premium (Intrinsic and Time Value): An option premium is the price paid by the buyer to the seller, made up of intrinsic value (what the option is worth if exercised now) and time value.

Intrinsic value is the amount by which an option is in the money. For a call, it is the greater of zero and (spot − strike); for a put, the greater of zero and (strike − spot). It can never be negative. At-the-money and out-of-the-money options have zero intrinsic value, so their entire premium is time value.

Time value equals premium minus intrinsic value. It reflects the chance that the option becomes more valuable before expiry. It is highest for at-the-money options and falls as expiry approaches — a process called time decay (measured by theta). At expiry, time value is zero and the premium equals intrinsic value.

Six factors drive premiums: price of the underlying, strike price, time to expiry, volatility, interest rates and expected dividends. Higher volatility increases both call and put premiums because a larger move becomes more likely. More time to expiry generally increases premiums. Rising interest rates slightly increase call premiums and reduce put premiums, while dividends do the reverse.

Many traders think a cheap option is a bargain. A low premium usually means the option is far out of the money with a small chance of paying off. Comparing premiums across strikes without looking at intrinsic and time value leads to poor decisions.

Example

Spot ₹2,150. A ₹2,100 call trades at ₹78: intrinsic value ₹50 (2,150 − 2,100), time value ₹28. A ₹2,200 call trades at ₹30: intrinsic value zero, time value ₹30. A ₹2,200 put trades at ₹76: intrinsic value ₹50 (2,200 − 2,150), time value ₹26. If nothing else changes, by expiry the ₹2,100 call will be worth only ₹50 and the ₹2,200 call nothing.

FAQs

Can an option trade below its intrinsic value?

For European options, slight deviations can occur, especially in illiquid strikes or for deep in-the-money puts when interest rates are high. In liquid markets, large gaps are quickly removed by arbitrage. For practical purposes, assume premium is at least close to intrinsic value.

Why do premiums rise before events like results or the Budget?

Uncertainty raises expected volatility. Traders anticipate larger price moves, so implied volatility rises and both call and put premiums increase. After the event, implied volatility often drops sharply, a fall sometimes called a volatility crush, which can hurt option buyers even if the price moves.
