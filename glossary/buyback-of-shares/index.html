What is Buyback of Shares?

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Buyback of Shares: A share buyback is when a company repurchases its own shares from existing shareholders, reducing the number of shares outstanding and returning cash to investors.

Buybacks are governed by Section 68 of the Companies Act, 2013 and the SEBI (Buy-Back of Securities) Regulations, 2018. A company may buy back up to 25% of its paid-up capital and free reserves, with a lower limit of 10% if approved only by the board. After the buyback, its debt-to-equity ratio must generally not exceed 2:1. Bought-back shares are extinguished.

The tender offer route is now the main method: the company announces a buyback price, usually at a premium to the market, and a record date. Shareholders tender shares through the exchange's window. SEBI has phased out the open market route through stock exchanges. In a tender offer, 15% of the buyback is reserved for small shareholders — those holding shares worth up to ₹2 lakh on the record date.

If more shares are tendered than the company wants to buy, acceptance is proportionate. The acceptance ratio for small shareholders is often higher than for others because their reserved quota is smaller relative to their total holding. Unaccepted shares are returned to the shareholder's demat account.

Companies use buybacks to return surplus cash, signal that management sees the share as undervalued, and improve earnings per share. Investors sometimes assume they will sell all their shares at the premium price — in reality only a fraction is usually accepted, and the remaining shares trade at the market price. Tax treatment of buyback proceeds has changed in recent years, so check the current income-tax rules.

Example

A company announces a tender buyback of 1 crore shares at ₹1,500 when the market price is ₹1,300. The small-shareholder quota is 15 lakh shares. If small shareholders tender 50 lakh eligible shares, their acceptance ratio is 30%. A small investor with 100 shares has 30 accepted at ₹1,500 (₹45,000) and gets 70 shares back.

FAQs

Is a buyback compulsory for shareholders?

No. Participation is voluntary. Shareholders who do not tender keep their shares and end up with a slightly higher percentage ownership because the total number of shares falls. Whether to tender depends on the premium offered, expected acceptance ratio and one's own investment view.

Who counts as a small shareholder in a buyback?

Under SEBI's buyback regulations, a small shareholder is one whose holding is worth up to ₹2 lakh on the record date, based on the closing price on the exchange with the highest trading volume. Fifteen percent of the buyback, or their entitlement if higher, is reserved for this category.
