What is Bid-Ask Spread?

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Bid-Ask Spread: The bid-ask spread is the difference between the highest price a buyer is willing to pay (bid) and the lowest price a seller is willing to accept (ask).

Every trading screen shows a market depth or order book. The best bid is the top buy order; the best ask (or offer) is the top sell order. A trade happens only when someone accepts the other side's price. The gap between the two is the spread, often expressed in rupees or as a percentage of the mid-price.

Spreads are a hidden transaction cost. If you buy at the ask and immediately sell at the bid, you lose the spread even if the 'price' has not moved. In highly liquid large-cap stocks, the spread may be just one tick (₹0.05 or ₹0.10). In small, rarely traded stocks, it can be 1–2% or more, which matters a lot for short-term traders.

Market makers and active traders earn by capturing the spread — buying at the bid and selling at the ask. Exchanges watch spreads as a liquidity measure, and index providers use the related concept of impact cost when selecting constituents.

A misconception is that the last traded price (LTP) is the price you can trade at. The LTP is history; what you can actually buy at is the best ask, and sell at is the best bid. Also note that the visible depth shows only the top few levels, and large orders may consume several levels.

Example

A stock's order book shows best bid ₹184.20 (500 shares) and best ask ₹184.80 (300 shares). The spread is ₹0.60. The mid-price is ₹184.50, so the spread is 0.60 ÷ 184.50 ≈ 0.33%. A trader who buys 300 shares at ₹184.80 and immediately sells them at ₹184.20 loses ₹180 before any brokerage or taxes, even though nothing has changed in the stock.

FAQs

Why is the spread wider in small-cap stocks?

Fewer buyers and sellers place orders, so the best bid and ask are further apart. Market participants also demand a larger margin for the risk of holding a less liquid stock. Wider spreads mean higher hidden costs, which is why limit orders are especially useful in such stocks.

Does the bid-ask spread apply to options too?

Yes, and often more sharply. Far out-of-the-money or illiquid option strikes can have spreads that are a large percentage of the premium. Option traders check the spread and open interest before trading, because entering and exiting can cost more than expected.
