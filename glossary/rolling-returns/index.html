What is Rolling returns?

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Rolling returns: Rolling returns are a series of returns calculated over a fixed holding period, such as three years, starting from every possible date in a longer history.

Most fund advertisements show point-to-point returns: from one date to another, such as 'five-year return as on 31 March'. These figures depend heavily on the start and end dates. A fund that looks excellent on 31 March after a market rally can look ordinary three months later.

Rolling returns fix this. Take a 10-year history and calculate the 3-year return starting from every month (or day) in that period: January 2014 to January 2017, February 2014 to February 2017, and so on. You get dozens of 3-year returns. Their average, minimum, maximum and the percentage of periods beating the benchmark tell you how consistent the fund has been.

Advisers use rolling returns to answer practical questions: in what share of 5-year periods did this fund give more than 10% a year? How often did it lose money over 3 years? This is more useful for setting expectations than a single trailing return.

Rolling returns still describe the past. They also need enough history; a fund with four years of data cannot show many 3-year rolling periods. And comparing funds requires using the same periods and the same plan (direct or regular).

Example

Using monthly data for 2014–2024, an analyst calculates 85 rolling 3-year CAGRs for a large cap fund and its Nifty 100 TRI benchmark. The fund's 3-year returns ranged from 2.1% to 24.6%, averaging 12.8%; it beat the benchmark in 58 of 85 periods (68%) and never had a negative 3-year return. A single '3-year return of 18%' quoted at a market high would have hidden this range.

FAQs

Why are rolling returns better than trailing returns?

Trailing returns depend on one start and end date, so a recent rally or crash can distort them. Rolling returns average performance across many overlapping periods, revealing consistency and the range of outcomes an investor might have experienced depending on when they entered.

What rolling period should I look at?

Match it roughly to your holding period. For equity funds, 3-year and 5-year rolling returns are common. For debt funds, 1-year rolling returns are often used. Longer windows smooth out short-term noise but need more history.
