What is Marginal Standing Facility (MSF)?

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Marginal Standing Facility (MSF): Marginal Standing Facility is an emergency window through which scheduled banks borrow overnight from RBI at a rate above repo, pledging government securities, including some held for SLR.

RBI introduced MSF in May 2011 as a safety valve. Normal repo funding is limited and offered through auctions, so a bank facing a sudden, unexpected cash shortage at the end of the day needs somewhere to go. Under MSF it can borrow overnight without waiting for an auction, but at a penalty rate, currently set at 25 basis points above the repo rate (check the latest RBI statement as of 2026).

The special feature is that banks may dip into their Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) holdings. Normally securities held for SLR cannot be pledged, but under MSF a bank can use them up to a limit set by RBI, expressed as a percentage of its net demand and time liabilities (NDTL). This limit has been 2% of NDTL for most of the facility's life and was temporarily raised during the pandemic.

The MSF rate is the ceiling of the liquidity adjustment facility corridor; the SDF rate is the floor and the repo rate sits in the middle. Since 2014 the Bank Rate has been aligned with the MSF rate, so the two always move together.

Exam traps: MSF is for scheduled commercial banks (and some others RBI permits), not NBFCs; it is overnight, not term borrowing; and it injects liquidity, unlike SDF. Heavy use of MSF by banks is read as a sign of tight liquidity in the system.

Example

A bank's NDTL is ₹1,00,000 crore. With a 2% dip-down limit it can pledge SLR securities worth up to ₹2,000 crore under MSF. If repo is 5.50%, MSF is 5.75%. Borrowing ₹1,500 crore overnight costs 1,500 × 5.75%/365 = about ₹0.236 crore, or ₹23.6 lakh, compared with about ₹22.6 lakh at repo. The extra ₹1 lakh is the price of emergency access.

FAQs

What is the difference between MSF and repo?

Repo funds are offered by RBI, often through auctions, at the policy rate, and banks must pledge securities in excess of their SLR requirement. MSF is available on demand at a higher rate, and banks can pledge securities from their SLR portfolio up to a set limit. MSF is the last-resort window; repo is routine funding.

Can a bank breach SLR by using MSF?

Technically its SLR holding falls below the required level, but RBI permits this within the MSF dip-down limit, so it is not treated as a default. Borrowing beyond the permitted dip-down would be a genuine SLR shortfall and attract penal interest under the Banking Regulation Act.
