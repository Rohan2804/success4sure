What is Crossed Cheque?

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Crossed Cheque: A crossed cheque has two parallel lines drawn across its face, instructing the paying bank to pay the amount only through a bank account and not in cash over the counter.

Crossing is covered in Sections 123 to 131 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881. Its purpose is safety: even if a crossed cheque is lost or stolen, the money can be traced because it must pass through a bank account.

General crossing (Section 123) is two parallel transverse lines, with or without words like '& Co.' or 'not negotiable'. The cheque can be paid to any bank, which then credits the payee's account. Special crossing (Section 124) names a particular bank between the lines, such as 'State Bank of India'; the paying bank will pay only to that named bank. Special crossing gives more protection than general crossing.

'Account payee' or 'A/c payee only' crossing is not defined in the Act but is recognised by banking practice and RBI directions. It tells the collecting bank to credit only the account of the named payee. Because of RBI directions, banks do not credit such cheques to any other account, even if endorsed. 'Not negotiable' crossing means the cheque remains transferable, but the transferee cannot get a better title than the transferor had.

Who can cross? The drawer or the holder can cross a cheque, convert general crossing to special, or add 'not negotiable'. A banker to whom a cheque is specially crossed can cross it again to another bank as its collecting agent. Only the drawer can cancel a crossing ('open' the cheque) with full signature.

Example

Arun issues a ₹60,000 cheque to Kavita, drawing two parallel lines and writing 'A/c payee'. The cheque is lost and found by Rakesh, who tries to deposit it in his own account. His bank refuses, because an account payee cheque can be credited only to Kavita's account. Had it been a bearer cheque without crossing, Rakesh could have encashed it at the counter, and Arun's ₹60,000 would have been lost.

FAQs

What is the difference between general and special crossing?

General crossing has only two parallel lines (with or without '& Co.') and allows payment through any bank. Special crossing names a specific bank, and the paying bank will pay only to that bank. Special crossing is safer because it limits the channel through which the cheque can be collected.

Can a crossed cheque be cashed at the counter?

No. A crossed cheque must be paid into a bank account, so the paying bank will not hand over cash. Only the drawer can cancel the crossing by writing 'pay cash' and signing in full, which turns it into an open cheque that can be encashed at the counter.
