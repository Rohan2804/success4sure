What is Power of compounding?

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Power of compounding: Compounding is the process by which an investment's returns are reinvested and themselves earn returns, so that wealth grows faster over time than with simple interest.

With simple interest, ₹1 lakh at 10% earns ₹10,000 every year. With compounding, the second year's 10% is earned on ₹1.1 lakh, the third year's on ₹1.21 lakh, and so on. The growth curve bends upward: most of the final value comes in the later years.

Three variables drive compounding: the amount invested, the rate of return and, above all, time. Because time sits in the exponent of the formula FV = P × (1 + r)^n, adding years has a far bigger effect than adding a little to the return or the amount. This is why advisers urge young earners to start a modest SIP early rather than a large one at 40.

Compounding works in reverse too. Costs, inflation and high-interest debt compound against you. A credit card balance at 36–42% a year doubles in about two years if unpaid, and a 1% higher expense ratio compounds into a large shortfall over decades.

Compounding requires staying invested and reinvesting returns. Withdrawing gains, choosing IDCW payouts and spending them, or stopping SIPs during market falls all break the chain. Returns in equity are not steady, so the smooth curves in brochures are illustrations, not promises.

Example

₹1,00,000 invested at 10% a year compounds to about ₹2.59 lakh in 10 years, ₹6.73 lakh in 20 years and ₹17.45 lakh in 30 years. The last 10 years add more than the first 20 combined. Similarly, ₹10,000 a month for 20 years at an assumed 12% (monthly compounding) grows to about ₹1 crore on an investment of ₹24 lakh.

FAQs

Why is starting early so important for compounding?

Because time is the exponent in the compounding formula. Someone who invests ₹5,000 a month from 25 to 35 and then stops may end up with a similar or larger amount at 60 than someone who invests ₹5,000 a month from 35 to 60, at the same return, despite investing far less money.

What is the difference between simple and compound interest?

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal. Compound interest is calculated on the principal plus previously earned interest. Over short periods the difference is small; over long periods compound interest produces dramatically larger amounts.
