What is Fiscal Deficit?

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Fiscal Deficit: Fiscal deficit is the amount by which the government's total expenditure exceeds its total receipts excluding borrowings, showing how much it must borrow in a year.

Formula: Fiscal deficit = Total expenditure − (Revenue receipts + Non-debt capital receipts). Revenue receipts are tax and non-tax revenue. Non-debt capital receipts include disinvestment proceeds and recovery of loans. Borrowings are excluded because the deficit measures exactly how much needs to be borrowed. It is usually stated as a percentage of GDP.

The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003 set targets to reduce the deficit, later amended to add a debt-to-GDP anchor. The Union Budget announces a target each year; for 2025-26 it was 4.4% of GDP, and the government has signalled a shift toward focusing on reducing debt as a share of GDP. Check the latest Union Budget for current targets (as of 2026). States have their own limits, broadly around 3% of state GDP with conditional relaxations.

Why it matters: a high fiscal deficit means more government borrowing, which can push up interest rates and crowd out private borrowers, add to inflation if financed by money creation, and increase future interest burdens. But deficits used for capital spending on roads, railways and power can raise long-term growth, so economists look at its quality as well as its size.

It is financed mainly through market borrowings (dated securities and treasury bills), small savings (via the NSSF) and external loans. Since 1997, direct monetisation through ad hoc treasury bills by RBI has ended.

Example

Suppose the Centre's total expenditure is ₹50 lakh crore, revenue receipts are ₹34 lakh crore, and non-debt capital receipts (disinvestment plus loan recoveries) are ₹0.8 lakh crore. Fiscal deficit = 50 − (34 + 0.8) = ₹15.2 lakh crore. If GDP is ₹350 lakh crore, the deficit is 15.2/350 = 4.34% of GDP. If interest payments are ₹12.5 lakh crore, the primary deficit is ₹2.7 lakh crore.

FAQs

Is a fiscal deficit always bad?

No. Governments often run deficits to fund infrastructure or support the economy in a slowdown, as India did during 2020-21. The concern is sustained high deficits used for consumption spending, which raise debt without boosting future growth. That is why the revenue deficit and the share of capital expenditure are watched closely.

What is the difference between fiscal deficit and budget deficit?

Budget deficit is an older, narrower term, roughly total expenditure minus total receipts including some borrowing like treasury bills. India stopped using it officially in 1997-98 and now reports fiscal, revenue, effective revenue and primary deficits. In most exams, fiscal deficit is the key measure.
