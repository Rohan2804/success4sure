What is Rebalancing?

Last updated 19 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Rebalancing: Rebalancing is the periodic process of bringing a portfolio's asset mix back to its target allocation after market movements have caused it to drift.

Suppose you target 60% equity and 40% debt. After a strong year for stocks, equity may become 68% of the portfolio. Your risk is now higher than you chose. Rebalancing means selling some equity and buying debt to restore 60:40. After a fall, it works the other way: you buy equity when it is cheaper.

There are two main methods. Calendar rebalancing happens at fixed intervals, such as each year on a chosen date. Threshold or band rebalancing happens only when a weight drifts beyond a set limit, such as 5 percentage points. Many advisers combine the two: review annually, act only if outside the band.

In India, rebalancing by selling units can trigger capital gains tax and exit loads. Using fresh SIP money or bonuses to buy the underweight asset, rather than selling, reduces these costs. Hybrid funds rebalance internally without creating a tax event for the investor.

Rebalancing feels uncomfortable because it means selling what has done well and buying what has done badly. That discomfort is the point: it is a disciplined counter to the herd instinct, not a return-maximising trick in every period.

Example

Leena's ₹10 lakh portfolio started at 60% equity (₹6 lakh) and 40% debt (₹4 lakh). Over a year equity rose 30% to ₹7.8 lakh and debt stayed flat at ₹4 lakh, making ₹11.8 lakh with equity at 66%. To restore 60:40 she needs ₹7.08 lakh in equity and ₹4.72 lakh in debt, so she moves ₹72,000 from equity funds to debt funds, preferably from units held over a year.

FAQs

How often should I rebalance my portfolio?

Common practice is to review once a year and rebalance if any asset class has drifted more than about 5 percentage points from target. More frequent rebalancing increases costs and taxes with little extra benefit for most individuals.

Can I rebalance without selling?

Yes. Direct new investments, such as SIPs, bonuses or maturing deposits, into the asset class that is underweight. This avoids capital gains tax and exit loads. It works well when drift is moderate and fresh money is substantial.
