What is Futures Contract?

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Futures Contract: A futures contract is a standardised, exchange-traded agreement to buy or sell an underlying asset at a set price on a future date, with daily settlement of gains and losses.

Futures standardise everything a forward leaves to negotiation: the underlying, the lot size, the expiry date and the settlement method. In India, futures trade on stocks, indices, currencies, interest rates and commodities. Equity futures expire on the scheduled expiry day of the month, and contracts for the near, next and far months trade simultaneously.

To take a position, both buyer and seller deposit an initial margin with their broker, calculated by the clearing corporation using SPAN plus exposure margin. Every day the position is marked to market: gains are credited and losses debited based on the daily settlement price. If losses reduce the margin below the required level, the trader must add funds.

Stock futures in India are physically settled at expiry, meaning open long positions must take delivery of shares and short positions must deliver them. Index futures are cash settled. Most traders close or roll over their positions before expiry rather than taking delivery.

The leverage is high: a 15–20% margin means a 5% price move can produce a 25–30% gain or loss on your capital. A common misunderstanding is that a futures buyer can lose only the margin. Losses are unlimited in theory; if the price keeps falling, the trader must keep paying mark-to-market losses.

Example

A stock trades at ₹1,500 and its one-month futures at ₹1,510. Suppose the lot size is 500 shares, so one contract is worth ₹7,55,000, and the initial margin is about 20%, or ₹1,51,000. If the futures price rises to ₹1,560, the buyer gains ₹50 × 500 = ₹25,000 — about 16.6% of the margin. A fall to ₹1,460 causes an equal ₹25,000 loss, collected through daily mark-to-market.

FAQs

What is rollover in futures?

Rollover means closing a position in the expiring contract and opening the same position in the next month's contract. It lets traders maintain exposure beyond expiry. The cost of rolling depends on the price difference between the two contracts, which reflects interest and expected dividends.

Do I need to pay the full value to buy futures?

No. You pay only the initial margin, a fraction of the contract value, and then settle daily gains or losses. This leverage is why futures need careful risk management: you are exposed to the full contract value even though you put up only part of it.
