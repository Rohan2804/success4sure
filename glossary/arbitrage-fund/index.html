What is Arbitrage fund?

Last updated 26 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Arbitrage fund: An arbitrage fund is a hybrid mutual fund that earns returns by simultaneously buying a stock in the cash market and selling its futures at a higher price, capturing the price difference.

In the stock market, a share's futures contract usually trades above its spot price; the gap reflects the cost of carry until expiry. An arbitrage fund buys the share and sells the futures in equal quantity. At expiry, both prices converge, and the fund locks in the gap regardless of whether the share rises or falls. It then rolls the position into the next month.

SEBI requires arbitrage funds to invest at least 65% in equity and equity-related instruments, almost all of it hedged. The rest sits in debt and money market securities. Because the equity is hedged, market risk is very small, and returns broadly track short-term interest rates.

Tax treatment is the main attraction. Since gross equity exceeds 65%, the fund is treated as equity-oriented, so gains held over a year are taxed as equity LTCG rather than at slab rate. This makes it popular with investors in high tax brackets for parking money for a few months or more.

Returns are not fixed. When markets are quiet and few traders want leveraged long positions, the futures premium shrinks and arbitrage returns fall, sometimes below liquid funds. There is also a typical exit load for very short holding, often around 30 days.

Example

A stock, call it ABC, trades at ₹2,000 in the cash market and its one-month future at ₹2,011. The fund buys 500 shares for ₹10,00,000 and sells the future for ₹10,05,500. At expiry, if the share closes at ₹1,900, the fund loses ₹50,000 on the shares but gains ₹55,500 on the futures, netting ₹5,500. If it closes at ₹2,100, it gains ₹50,000 and loses ₹44,500, again ₹5,500. That is 0.55% for the month, about 6.6% annualised, before costs.

FAQs

Are arbitrage funds risk-free?

No. Market risk is largely hedged, but returns vary with the futures premium and can drop when arbitrage opportunities narrow. There is also execution risk, small credit risk on the debt portion, and exit loads for short holding. They are low-volatility funds, not guaranteed-return products.

Why are arbitrage funds taxed as equity?

Tax law treats a scheme as equity-oriented if at least 65% of its assets are in domestic listed equity. Arbitrage funds meet this test through their hedged stock holdings, so they get equity capital gains treatment even though their risk resembles a short-term debt fund.
