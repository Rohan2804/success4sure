What is Stock Split?

Last updated 19 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Stock Split: A stock split divides each existing share into several shares of a lower face value, increasing the number of shares without changing the company's total capital.

In a split, a company with shares of face value ₹10 may split each into five shares of ₹2, or ten shares of ₹1. The share count multiplies, the face value falls by the same factor, and the market price adjusts proportionately on the ex-date. Total paid-up capital and reserves on the balance sheet are unaffected; only the denomination of shares changes.

Companies split shares mainly to improve liquidity and affordability. A share trading at ₹12,000 can feel out of reach for small investors and leads to wide bid-ask spreads. After a 1:10 split, the price is around ₹1,200, and more participants can buy in smaller amounts.

A split requires board and shareholder approval because it alters the memorandum's capital clause. A record date is fixed, and depositories credit the new shares under a new ISIN after the old ISIN is cancelled. Exchanges adjust open futures and options contracts — the strike price is divided and the lot size multiplied — so that the position value stays the same.

The reverse of a split is a consolidation (reverse split), which combines shares into fewer shares of higher face value; it is rare in India. People often think a split makes a share 'cheap'. Valuation ratios like price-to-earnings do not change because earnings per share fall by the same factor as price.

Example

An investor holds 30 shares of face value ₹10 trading at ₹4,500 (₹1,35,000). The company splits each share into 5 of face value ₹2. After the ex-date, she holds 150 shares, and the price is about ₹900 — still ₹1,35,000. EPS of ₹150 becomes ₹30, so the P/E stays at 30. A call option with strike ₹4,600 becomes strike ₹920, with the lot size five times larger.

FAQs

Is a stock split good for shareholders?

A split by itself does not create value; it only changes the number of shares and price per share. It can improve liquidity and widen the investor base, which some studies associate with short-term interest, but long-term returns depend on the company's business performance, not on the split.

What is the difference between a stock split and a bonus issue?

A split reduces face value and keeps reserves unchanged; a bonus keeps face value the same and converts reserves into share capital. In both cases the number of shares rises and the price adjusts, so the investor's holding value is unaffected at the time of the action.
