What is Vega (Option Greek)?

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Vega (Option Greek): Vega measures how much an option's premium changes for a one percentage point change in the implied volatility of the underlying.

Higher volatility makes large price moves more likely, which raises the value of both calls and puts. Long options therefore have positive vega, and short options have negative vega. A vega of 1.13 means the premium rises by about ₹1.13 if implied volatility increases from, say, 20% to 21%, and falls by the same if volatility drops to 19%.

Vega is largest for at-the-money options and for options with more time to expiry. A three-month option is much more sensitive to volatility changes than a one-week option, because uncertainty compounds over time. This is why traders who want to bet on volatility often use longer-dated options.

Vega becomes critical around scheduled events — company results, RBI policy announcements, the Union Budget or elections. Implied volatility usually rises before the event and drops sharply afterwards. An option buyer who correctly predicts the direction can still lose if the drop in volatility reduces the premium more than the price move adds.

Vega is not a Greek letter at all; it was borrowed into options jargon. In exams, remember that vega is the same sign for calls and puts (positive for buyers) and that volatility is the only option pricing input that is not directly observable — it must be estimated or implied from market prices.

Example

Using Black-Scholes, a 30-day ₹1,000 at-the-money call priced at 20% volatility is worth about ₹25.79. At 21% volatility it is worth about ₹26.92, so vega ≈ ₹1.13. Before a result announcement, IV rises from 20% to 28%: the call gains roughly 8 × ₹1.13 ≈ ₹9 even if the stock does not move. If IV falls back to 20% after results, that ₹9 disappears.

FAQs

Do calls and puts have the same vega?

For European options with the same strike, expiry and underlying, call and put vegas are equal under the Black-Scholes model. Both gain when implied volatility rises and lose when it falls. This is why straddles, which combine a call and a put, have high vega.

Why do option prices fall after results even when the stock moves?

Implied volatility usually rises ahead of results because of uncertainty and then drops after the announcement. The drop reduces premiums through vega. If the stock's actual move is smaller than what the options had priced in, the loss from falling volatility can exceed the gain from the move.
