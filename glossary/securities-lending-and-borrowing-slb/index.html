What is Securities Lending and Borrowing (SLB)?

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Securities Lending and Borrowing (SLB): Securities lending and borrowing (SLB) is an exchange-traded mechanism where shareholders lend their shares for a fee to borrowers, who must return them on a fixed date.

SLB in India runs on the stock exchange platform through approved intermediaries, with the clearing corporation acting as the counterparty to both sides. This guarantee is what makes it attractive: the lender does not depend on the borrower's creditworthiness, since the borrower must post margins with the clearing corporation.

Lenders are often long-term investors, mutual funds and insurers who earn an extra fee on idle holdings. Borrowers include traders who want to short sell beyond intraday, those who need shares to avoid short delivery, and arbitrageurs. Contracts can range from a day to twelve months, and borrowers can roll over or recall positions early under exchange rules.

Corporate actions are handled so that the lender is not disadvantaged. If a dividend is paid during the lending period, the borrower compensates the lender. For events such as bonus, split or rights, contracts are adjusted or foreclosed as per exchange rules. The lender, however, loses voting rights while the shares are lent.

The main misconception is that SLB transfers ownership permanently or exposes lenders to default risk. Ownership returns at the end of the contract, and the clearing corporation guarantees the return or cash compensation. Fees are market-driven and vary widely by stock and demand.

Example

Ramesh holds 1,000 shares of a stock trading at ₹800 and lends them for 30 days at an annualised fee of 6%. His fee ≈ ₹8,00,000 × 6% × 30/365 = ₹3,945. A trader who expects the price to fall borrows these shares, sells them at ₹800 and buys back at ₹760 before returning them, earning ₹40,000 before the fee and costs. If the price rises, the trader loses instead.

FAQs

Is lending shares through SLB safe?

The clearing corporation guarantees the transaction and holds margins from borrowers, so the lender's risk of not getting shares back is low. The lender still faces normal market risk on the shares and loses voting rights during the lending period. Fees and liquidity vary by stock.

Why would someone borrow shares instead of selling futures?

Some stocks have no futures contracts, and a borrower may need actual shares to meet a delivery obligation. SLB also allows short positions for a tenure that does not match futures expiry. Borrowing costs and availability decide which route is cheaper or practical.
