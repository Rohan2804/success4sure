What is Lot Size (Contract Size)?

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Lot Size (Contract Size): Lot size is the fixed number of units of the underlying in one futures or options contract, set by the exchange; it determines the contract's rupee value.

Exchange-traded derivatives are standardised, so you cannot buy 37 shares' worth of a stock future — you trade in multiples of the lot size. Contract value equals lot size × futures price (or, for options, lot size × strike or underlying price for exposure purposes; the premium paid is lot size × option price).

SEBI sets a minimum contract value for equity derivatives, and exchanges fix lot sizes to meet it. In late 2024, SEBI raised the minimum contract value for index derivatives to a range of about ₹15–20 lakh at the time of review, mainly to reduce participation by small, high-risk retail traders. Lot sizes are reviewed periodically and adjusted as prices move — always check the exchange's current lot size file.

Lot sizes also change after corporate actions. A stock split or bonus increases the lot size proportionately while adjusting the strike prices and futures prices, so the value of open positions does not change.

Beginners often look only at the option premium per unit, say ₹40, and underestimate the actual cash needed. With a lot of 75, that is ₹3,000; with a lot of 1,200, it is ₹48,000. Likewise, a ₹10 move in a futures price means very different profits or losses depending on lot size.

Example

A stock future trades at ₹620 with a lot size of 1,250, so contract value is ₹7,75,000. If the price rises to ₹1,250 over a year, the exchange's next review may reduce the lot size so the contract value stays in range — for instance, to 600, giving ₹7,50,000. A call option at a premium of ₹14.50 on the original lot costs ₹18,125.

FAQs

Can I trade less than one lot in futures and options?

No. Exchange-traded derivatives can be traded only in whole lots. If you want smaller exposure, you have to use the cash market or products such as ETFs. This standardisation is what makes exchange-traded contracts liquid and easy to clear.

Why did SEBI increase the contract size for index derivatives?

SEBI found that most individual traders in equity F&O were losing money. Raising the minimum contract value, along with measures like reducing weekly expiries, was meant to ensure that participants have adequate capital and risk capacity. Check SEBI's circulars for the current thresholds.
