What is Price Band (Stock Circuit Limit)?

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Price Band (Stock Circuit Limit): A price band is the maximum percentage a stock's price can rise or fall in a single trading day from its previous close, as set by the exchange.

Exchanges assign each stock a daily price band, commonly 2%, 5%, 10% or 20%, based on factors like volatility, liquidity and surveillance concerns. Orders priced outside the band are rejected. When a stock reaches the top of its band, it is said to hit the 'upper circuit'; at the bottom, the 'lower circuit'. Trading continues within the band, but at the limit price there may be only buyers or only sellers.

Stocks with derivatives contracts (F&O stocks) do not have a fixed daily price band. Instead, exchanges apply dynamic price bands (operating ranges), typically starting at 10% and relaxed in steps if the market moves toward the edge. This allows price discovery in highly traded stocks while still preventing wild erroneous trades.

Price bands are also a surveillance tool. Exchanges can tighten a stock's band, for example from 20% to 5% or 2%, if it shows abnormal movements or comes under additional surveillance measures (ASM/GSM). Newly listed IPO shares have special pre-open sessions and bands on listing day.

The risk for investors is liquidity. If a small-cap stock is at a 5% lower circuit with a large pile of sell orders and no buyers, you may be unable to exit for several days in a row. Seeing a stock 'locked' in upper circuit is not a sign of strength by itself; it can also reflect thin trading or manipulation.

Example

A stock closed at ₹240 and has a 5% price band. Today, orders can be placed only between ₹228 and ₹252. A buy order at ₹253 is rejected. If heavy buying pushes the price to ₹252, the stock is at upper circuit and buy orders pile up at ₹252 waiting for sellers. Tomorrow's band is calculated on the new closing price: if it closes at ₹252, tomorrow's range becomes ₹239.40–₹264.60.

FAQs

Why do F&O stocks not have a price band?

Stocks in the derivatives segment are usually large and liquid, and fixed bands would distort arbitrage between the cash and futures markets. Instead, exchanges apply dynamic price bands that can be widened during the day in a controlled way. This still blocks erroneous orders while allowing genuine price discovery.

Can I sell a stock stuck at lower circuit?

You can place a sell order at the lower band price, but it will execute only if a buyer is willing to buy at that price. If sell orders far exceed buy orders, many sellers remain unfilled. This is why stocks with frequent circuit hits carry high liquidity risk.
