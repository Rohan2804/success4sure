What is Politically Exposed Person (PEP)?

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Politically Exposed Person (PEP): A politically exposed person is an individual who holds or has held a prominent public function, such as a senior politician, judge or military official, and who therefore needs enhanced due diligence.

Under SEBI's anti-money laundering guidelines and PMLA rules, PEPs are individuals entrusted with prominent public functions by a foreign country or within India — for example heads of state or government, senior politicians, senior government or judicial or military officers, senior executives of state-owned corporations and important political party officials. The definition is broad, and intermediaries also apply similar care to family members and close associates.

PEPs are not presumed to be criminals. They are treated as higher risk because their position could be misused for bribery or corruption, and because laundering proceeds of corruption often involves such individuals. International standards set by the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) require extra checks on them.

Enhanced due diligence includes approval from senior management before opening the account (or continuing it if an existing client becomes a PEP), taking reasonable steps to establish the source of wealth and funds, and conducting closer ongoing monitoring of transactions. The client must be categorised as high risk.

A common mistake is thinking that PEP status ends the moment a person leaves office. Risk-based approaches often keep former PEPs under enhanced monitoring for a period, and status of relatives and close associates also needs attention.

Example

A retired senior civil servant opens a demat and trading account and wants to transfer ₹2 crore of shares from another DP. The broker's screening flags him as a PEP. The compliance team obtains senior management approval, asks for documents on the source of the shares and wealth (inheritance records and past salary statements), categorises him as high risk and sets tighter alert thresholds on his transactions.

FAQs

Can an intermediary refuse to open an account for a PEP?

PEP status alone is not a reason to refuse. Intermediaries must apply enhanced due diligence and take a risk-based decision. If they cannot satisfactorily establish the source of funds or the client refuses to cooperate, they may decline the relationship and, where appropriate, consider filing a suspicious transaction report.

Do family members of PEPs also need enhanced checks?

Yes. Regulators and FATF guidance expect intermediaries to apply similar enhanced due diligence to family members and close associates of PEPs, because they may be used to hold or move funds on the PEP's behalf. The extent of checks follows the intermediary's risk assessment.
