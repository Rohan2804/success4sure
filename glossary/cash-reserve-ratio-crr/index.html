What is Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)?

Last updated 19 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR): Cash Reserve Ratio is the percentage of a bank's net demand and time liabilities that it must keep as a cash balance with RBI, on which RBI pays no interest.

CRR is prescribed under Section 42 of the RBI Act, 1934 for scheduled banks. It is calculated on net demand and time liabilities (NDTL), which broadly means customer deposits (savings, current, fixed) plus certain other liabilities, minus specific exempted items such as inter-bank liabilities. Banks report NDTL fortnightly, and CRR is maintained on an average daily basis, with a minimum of 90% of the requirement on every single day.

Because the money sits idle with RBI, CRR is both a safety cushion and a monetary policy lever. Raising CRR locks up more cash and reduces what banks can lend, which drains liquidity. Cutting CRR frees money for lending. A CRR change affects liquidity directly and in large amounts, so RBI uses it sparingly compared with the repo rate. RBI cut CRR in phases during 2025 to 3% of NDTL; check the current figure in the latest monetary policy statement (as of 2026).

CRR differs from SLR in three ways that exams love: CRR is kept with RBI, SLR is kept by the bank itself; CRR is in cash only, SLR can be cash, gold or approved securities; and CRR earns nothing, while SLR securities earn interest.

A shortfall attracts penal interest linked to the bank rate. Because CRR earns nothing, it acts like a tax on deposits, which is one reason banks lobby for lower CRR.

Example

A bank's NDTL is ₹2,00,000 crore and CRR is 3%. Required CRR balance = 2,00,000 × 3% = ₹6,000 crore, kept with RBI. If RBI cuts CRR by 0.50 percentage point to 2.5%, the requirement falls to ₹5,000 crore, freeing ₹1,000 crore. At an 8% lending rate, that freed cash could earn the bank about ₹80 crore a year.

FAQs

Does RBI pay interest on CRR balances?

No. Since 2007 RBI pays no interest on CRR balances, after the RBI Act was amended to remove the floor and ceiling on CRR along with the interest obligation. This is why CRR is costly for banks and why any cut directly improves their earning capacity.

What is NDTL in CRR calculation?

Net demand and time liabilities is a bank's demand liabilities (current and savings deposits, demand drafts outstanding) plus time liabilities (fixed deposits, recurring deposits), plus other liabilities, minus specified exemptions like inter-bank liabilities. CRR and SLR are both calculated as a percentage of NDTL.
