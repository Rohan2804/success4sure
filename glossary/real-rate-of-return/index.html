What is Real rate of return?

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Real rate of return: The real rate of return is the return on an investment after adjusting for inflation, showing the actual increase in purchasing power.

A nominal return is what the investment statement shows, say 7% on a fixed deposit. If prices rise 5% over the same year, your money buys only about 1.9% more than before. That is the real return. The quick approximation is nominal return minus inflation; the exact formula is (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1.

For planning, the post-tax real return is what matters. Interest on FDs is taxed at slab rate, so a 7% FD for someone in the 30% slab (plus cess) yields about 4.8% after tax, which may be below inflation. In that case the investor is losing purchasing power even though the balance grows.

Financial planners use real returns to project goals in today's rupees and to choose asset classes. Over long periods, equity has historically delivered a positive real return in India, while many fixed-income products have hovered close to inflation after tax.

A common error is comparing returns from different periods without adjusting for the inflation of those periods. A 10% return when inflation was 9% is worse than 7% when inflation was 3%.

Example

Gopal has ₹10 lakh in a 7% FD and is in the 30% tax slab (31.2% with cess). Pre-tax real return at 5% inflation = 1.07 ÷ 1.05 − 1 ≈ 1.9%. After tax, his interest is 7% × (1 − 0.312) ≈ 4.82%. Real post-tax return = 1.0482 ÷ 1.05 − 1 ≈ −0.17%. His deposit grows in rupees but slightly shrinks in purchasing power.

FAQs

Can real return be negative?

Yes, whenever inflation is higher than the nominal return, or higher than the post-tax return. Savings accounts and some fixed deposits often show negative real returns after tax, which slowly erodes purchasing power even though the balance grows.

Which inflation figure should I use for real return?

For general use, the consumer price index (CPI) is the standard measure in India. For specific goals, use the relevant inflation, such as education or medical costs, which have often risen faster than CPI. Personal inflation also varies by lifestyle.
