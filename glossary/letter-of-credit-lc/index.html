What is Letter of Credit (LC)?

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Letter of Credit (LC): A letter of credit is a written undertaking by a bank, issued on behalf of a buyer, to pay the seller a stated amount on presenting documents that comply with the LC's terms.

LCs solve a trust problem in trade, especially international trade. The exporter does not want to ship goods without payment certainty, and the importer does not want to pay before goods are shipped. With an LC, a bank steps in: the seller ships, presents the required documents (invoice, bill of lading, insurance, certificate of origin) and gets paid by the bank if the documents match the LC terms exactly.

Parties: the applicant (buyer or importer), the beneficiary (seller or exporter), the issuing bank (buyer's bank), the advising bank (in the seller's country, which authenticates and passes on the LC), and sometimes a confirming bank, which adds its own payment undertaking, and a negotiating bank, which examines documents and pays the seller.

Types: a sight LC pays on presentation of documents; a usance or deferred payment LC pays after a set period, such as 90 days after the bill of lading date. LCs under UCP 600 are irrevocable by default. A confirmed LC adds a second bank's guarantee. A revolving LC covers repeated shipments; a back-to-back LC is opened by a trader on the strength of another LC; a standby LC works more like a guarantee.

International LCs are governed by the Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits (UCP 600) of the International Chamber of Commerce. The golden rule is that banks deal in documents, not goods: a small discrepancy in documents can allow the bank to refuse payment even if the goods are perfect.

Example

A Ludhiana textile exporter sells fabric worth $80,000 to a buyer in Dubai. The buyer's bank in Dubai issues an irrevocable 60-day usance LC; an Indian bank advises it. The exporter ships, presents compliant documents and the Indian bank negotiates them. At ₹88 per dollar, the bill value is ₹70.4 lakh. If the Indian bank discounts the bill at 7% a year for 60 days, the exporter gets about 70.4 lakh × (1 − 0.07 × 60/365) = ₹69.59 lakh immediately.

FAQs

What does 'banks deal in documents, not goods' mean?

Under UCP 600, the bank checks only whether the presented documents strictly comply with the LC's terms. It does not inspect the actual goods. If the documents comply, the bank must pay even if the buyer later finds a quality issue; if they don't, the bank can refuse even if the goods are fine.

What is a confirmed letter of credit?

A confirmed LC carries an additional undertaking from a second bank, usually in the exporter's country, to pay if documents comply. Exporters ask for confirmation when they doubt the issuing bank's strength or the buyer country's political or currency risk. The confirming bank charges a fee for taking on this risk.
