What is Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)?

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR): Liquidity coverage ratio requires a bank to hold enough high-quality liquid assets to cover its expected net cash outflows over a 30-day period of severe stress.

LCR is a Basel III liquidity standard. Formula: LCR = Stock of high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) ÷ Total net cash outflows over the next 30 calendar days × 100. The ratio must be at least 100%, meaning the bank can survive a month-long stress scenario using its own liquid assets. RBI phased it in from January 2015 and it reached 100% in 2019.

HQLA are assets that can be turned into cash quickly with little loss of value. Level 1 assets include cash, CRR balances in excess of the requirement, and government securities, counted at full value. In India, banks can also count part of their SLR securities as HQLA through the MSF dip-down and the Facility to Avail Liquidity for LCR (FALLCR). Level 2A and 2B assets, such as high-rated corporate bonds, are counted after haircuts and within caps.

Net cash outflows assume that a share of deposits will run off: a small percentage for stable retail deposits, more for less stable deposits and much more for wholesale corporate funding. Expected inflows, capped at 75% of outflows, are deducted. RBI revised run-off factors in 2024-25, including higher rates for deposits that can be withdrawn instantly through digital banking; check RBI for current factors (as of 2026).

LCR works alongside the Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR), which looks at a one-year horizon and stable funding of long-term assets. Exam tip: LCR = short-term (30 days), NSFR = longer-term (one year).

Example

A bank holds HQLA of ₹25,000 crore. Expected 30-day outflows under stress: retail deposits ₹1,50,000 crore × 7.5% run-off = ₹11,250 crore; corporate deposits ₹40,000 crore × 40% = ₹16,000 crore; other outflows ₹3,000 crore. Total = ₹30,250 crore. Expected inflows are ₹8,000 crore (below the 75% cap). Net outflows = ₹22,250 crore. LCR = 25,000/22,250 = 112.4%, above the 100% minimum.

FAQs

What is the difference between LCR and SLR?

SLR is a statutory requirement under the Banking Regulation Act to hold a fixed percentage of NDTL in liquid assets. LCR is a Basel III ratio based on a stress test of 30-day net outflows. SLR is a simple percentage; LCR depends on the bank's deposit mix. Some SLR securities count towards LCR.

Why did RBI raise run-off factors for digitally enabled deposits?

Mobile and internet banking let depositors move large sums instantly, as seen in some overseas bank runs. RBI therefore proposed higher run-off assumptions for retail deposits with internet and mobile banking access, so banks hold more liquid assets against the risk of faster withdrawals during stress.
