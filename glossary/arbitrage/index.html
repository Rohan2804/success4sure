What is Arbitrage?

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Arbitrage: Arbitrage is the practice of simultaneously buying and selling the same or equivalent assets in different markets to lock in a low-risk profit from a price difference.

In theory, arbitrage requires no capital and no risk; in practice, it needs capital, costs money to execute and carries small risks. The most common form in India is cash-futures arbitrage. If a stock's futures price is higher than the spot price plus cost of carry, an arbitrageur buys the shares and sells the futures, earning the difference at expiry when prices converge. This is called cash-and-carry arbitrage.

Reverse cash-and-carry is the opposite: when futures trade below fair value, arbitrageurs sell shares they hold (or borrow) and buy futures. Other forms include inter-exchange arbitrage (price differences between NSE and BSE), calendar spreads between months and options-based arbitrage using put-call parity.

Arbitrage keeps prices efficient. Because many players watch for mispricing, gaps close quickly. Arbitrage mutual funds in India use cash-futures strategies, earning returns broadly linked to short-term interest rates, with low equity market risk.

Costs and frictions are crucial: brokerage, STT, stamp duty, impact cost, margin funding and the risk that an arbitrage position must be unwound early. A price gap that looks profitable on paper may disappear after costs. Exam questions often ask what arbitrageurs do when futures are overpriced (buy spot, sell futures).

Example

A stock trades at ₹1,000 in the cash market and its one-month futures at ₹1,015. Fair value at 7% for 30 days is about ₹1,005.75. An arbitrageur buys 1,000 shares for ₹10,00,000 and sells one lot of 1,000 futures at ₹1,015. At expiry, both settle at the same price, so the gross profit is ₹15,000 — about 1.5% for the month, or roughly 18% annualised, before costs, which then attract more arbitrageurs until the gap narrows.

FAQs

Is arbitrage completely risk-free?

Not in practice. Execution risk (one leg filling at a worse price), changes in margin requirements, dividend surprises, early unwinding and transaction costs can reduce or eliminate profit. These risks are small compared with directional trading, which is why arbitrage returns are usually close to short-term interest rates.

Who are arbitrageurs in Indian markets?

Proprietary trading desks of brokers, banks, arbitrage mutual funds, some hedge-fund-style AIFs and high-frequency traders. Their activity keeps futures prices close to fair value and links the cash and derivatives markets. Individual traders rarely compete effectively because of costs and speed.
