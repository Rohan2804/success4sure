What is Behavioural finance?

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Behavioural finance: Behavioural finance studies how psychological biases and emotions lead investors to make decisions that depart from rational, purely financial reasoning.

Traditional finance assumes investors are rational and markets efficient. Behavioural finance, built on work by Daniel Kahneman, Amos Tversky and Richard Thaler, shows that real people use mental shortcuts that often lead them astray, especially under uncertainty and stress.

Key biases include: loss aversion, feeling losses about twice as strongly as equal gains, which leads to selling winners too early and holding losers too long; herd behaviour, buying what everyone else is buying, as in NFO and small cap frenzies; anchoring, fixating on a reference price such as the purchase price; overconfidence, overestimating one's ability to pick stocks or time markets; recency bias, assuming recent returns will continue; and mental accounting, treating money differently by its source, such as a bonus.

These biases explain why investor returns are often lower than fund returns: people invest after rallies and redeem after falls. Advisers counter them with written plans, automatic SIPs, pre-agreed rebalancing rules and education before markets fall, not during.

Biases are not only for beginners. Professionals suffer them too. Awareness helps but is not enough; systems and rules work better than willpower.

Example

In early 2020 Vivek's ₹5 lakh equity portfolio fell 35% to ₹3.25 lakh. Fearing further losses, he redeemed everything in April. Markets recovered strongly over the next year, and a similar portfolio broadly tracking the Nifty that stayed invested would have climbed back above ₹5 lakh by early 2021. His friend Sneha had a written plan with a SIP and an annual rebalancing rule; she kept investing and topped up equity during the fall. The same market gave them very different outcomes, purely from behaviour.

FAQs

What is herd behaviour in investing?

It is the tendency to follow what others are doing rather than one's own analysis, such as rushing into a hot sector or selling in a panic because everyone is. Herding can push prices far from fair value and often leads to buying high and selling low.

How can investors reduce behavioural biases?

Use rules decided in calm times: a written plan, automatic SIPs, a target asset allocation with rebalancing bands, and limits on checking portfolios daily. Working with a registered adviser can also add accountability during emotional market phases.
