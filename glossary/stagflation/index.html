What is Stagflation?

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Stagflation: Stagflation is a situation where an economy suffers high inflation, weak or stagnant growth and rising unemployment at the same time.

The word combines 'stagnation' and 'inflation'. Traditional thinking, illustrated by the Phillips curve, held that inflation and unemployment move in opposite directions: when the economy is booming, prices rise but jobs are plentiful. Stagflation breaks that pattern, which is why it surprised economists in the 1970s.

The classic cause is a supply shock. When oil prices quadrupled after 1973 and rose again in 1979, costs rose for almost every business. Firms raised prices and cut output, so inflation rose while growth fell. Other causes include poor harvests, wage-price spirals where workers demand higher pay and firms pass it on, and policy mistakes such as printing money while growth is weak.

Stagflation is a policy dilemma. If the central bank raises rates to fight inflation, growth weakens further. If it cuts rates to support growth, inflation can rise. Governments face similar trade-offs with spending. Usually the solution involves fixing the supply problem, anchoring inflation expectations, and accepting some short-term pain.

India faced stagflation-like conditions in the early 1970s and around 2011-13, when CPI inflation stayed near double digits while GDP growth slowed. Exam questions often ask which curve it contradicts (Phillips curve), what type of inflation is usually behind it (cost-push) and the main policy challenge.

Example

Suppose an economy grew 7% with 5% inflation last year. A crude oil shock lifts fuel costs 40%, transport and fertiliser costs follow, and inflation jumps to 8%. Factories cut shifts, so growth slows to 4% and unemployment rises from 7% to 8.5%. If the central bank raises its policy rate from 6% to 7% to tame prices, businesses face costlier loans and growth may slip further, showing the stagflation dilemma.

FAQs

What is the difference between stagflation and recession?

A recession is a significant decline in economic activity, often defined as two consecutive quarters of falling GDP, and usually comes with low or falling inflation. Stagflation combines weak growth, which may or may not be negative, with high inflation. The added inflation makes stagflation harder to treat with ordinary rate cuts.

What type of inflation causes stagflation?

Stagflation is usually driven by cost-push inflation, where higher input costs such as oil, food or wages push prices up and reduce output at the same time. Demand-pull inflation, by contrast, comes with strong growth, so it rarely produces stagflation on its own.
