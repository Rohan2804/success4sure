What is Inflation?

Last updated 19 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Inflation: Inflation is a sustained rise in the general level of prices of goods and services over time, which means each rupee buys less than before.

Inflation is measured as the percentage change in a price index over a period, usually year-on-year. In India the main measures are the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which tracks what households pay, and the Wholesale Price Index (WPI), which tracks bulk prices. RBI's Monetary Policy Committee targets CPI inflation of 4% within a 2% to 6% band.

Economists separate causes into two types. Demand-pull inflation happens when spending grows faster than the economy can produce, for example after a big rise in incomes or easy credit. Cost-push inflation comes from higher input costs, such as crude oil, wages or a poor monsoon raising food prices. In India, food and fuel shocks are frequent drivers, which is why policymakers also watch core inflation, which excludes food and fuel and reflects underlying demand pressure.

Inflation hurts people on fixed incomes and savers whose deposit interest does not keep pace. The real interest rate is roughly the nominal rate minus inflation. It can help borrowers, since loans are repaid in cheaper rupees. Moderate, stable inflation is considered healthy; very high or volatile inflation distorts decisions.

Related terms often confused: disinflation means inflation is falling but still positive (prices rise more slowly); deflation means prices actually fall; stagflation is high inflation with weak growth; and the base effect is when a low or high figure in the previous year makes this year's rate look unusually high or low.

Example

A thali cost ₹100 last year and ₹106 this year, so its price inflation is (106 − 100)/100 = 6%. If your fixed deposit earned 7%, your ₹10,000 grew to ₹10,700, but you now need ₹10,600 to buy what ₹10,000 bought before. Your real gain is only about ₹100, roughly 0.94% (1.07/1.06 − 1). At 8% inflation, the same deposit would lose purchasing power.

FAQs

What is the difference between headline and core inflation?

Headline inflation is the change in the full CPI basket, including food and fuel. Core inflation removes these volatile items to show the underlying trend driven by demand. If headline inflation jumps because of a vegetable price spike while core stays steady, the pressure may be temporary and supply-driven.

Is deflation good because things become cheaper?

Not usually. Persistent deflation makes people delay purchases, expecting lower prices, which reduces demand, profits and jobs. It also raises the real burden of debt. Central banks generally prefer low positive inflation to deflation, which is why India's target is 4% rather than zero.
