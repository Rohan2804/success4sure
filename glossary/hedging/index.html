What is Hedging (and Hedge Ratio)?

Last updated 19 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Hedging (and Hedge Ratio): Hedging is taking a position in a derivative that offsets the risk of an existing or expected position in the underlying asset, reducing the impact of adverse price moves.

Hedgers want protection, not profit. An investor holding shares can sell index futures or buy puts; an importer can buy dollar futures; a farmer can sell agricultural futures before harvest. If the price moves against the physical position, the derivative gains, and vice versa. The result is a more predictable outcome.

The hedge ratio decides how much derivative to use. For a stock portfolio hedged with index futures, a common approach uses beta: number of contracts = (portfolio value × portfolio beta) ÷ (futures price × lot size). A portfolio with beta 1.2 needs 20% more index futures than its rupee value to offset market movements.

Hedging has costs and limitations. Futures hedges remove upside as well as downside. Options hedges keep the upside but cost premium. Basis risk remains when the hedge instrument does not move exactly like the asset — for example, hedging a mid-cap portfolio with Nifty futures. Hedges also need to be rolled at expiry.

A misconception is that a hedged position cannot lose money. It can, through basis risk, imperfect ratios, timing mismatches and costs. Another is that a successful hedge is one that makes profit. A good hedge reduces variability; if the derivative leg loses money because the main position gained, the hedge did its job.

Example

A fund holds a ₹5 crore portfolio with a beta of 1.2. Index futures trade at 24,500 with an illustrative lot size of 75, so one contract is worth ₹18,37,500. Contracts needed = (₹5,00,00,000 × 1.2) ÷ ₹18,37,500 ≈ 32.7, so it sells about 33 lots. If the index falls 5%, the portfolio is expected to lose about 6% (₹30 lakh), while the short futures gain about ₹30.3 lakh.

FAQs

What is basis risk in hedging?

Basis risk is the risk that the hedge instrument and the asset being hedged do not move exactly together. For example, an investor hedging a pharma portfolio with Nifty futures is exposed if pharma stocks fall while the Nifty rises. Basis risk also arises when the hedge expires before or after the underlying exposure.

Is buying a put better than selling futures for hedging?

Each has trade-offs. Selling futures has no upfront cost but gives up gains if prices rise. Buying puts costs premium but keeps upside potential while limiting downside. The choice depends on cost tolerance, outlook and whether the hedger wants to keep participating in gains.
