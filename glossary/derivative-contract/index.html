What is Derivative?

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Derivative: A derivative is a financial contract whose value is derived from the price of an underlying asset, such as a share, index, currency, commodity or interest rate.

The four basic types are forwards, futures, options and swaps. Forwards and swaps are mostly traded over the counter (OTC) between two parties, while futures and options in India are exchange-traded on NSE, BSE, MCX and NCDEX with a clearing corporation guaranteeing settlement. The Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act includes derivatives in the definition of securities, which brings exchange-traded derivatives under SEBI.

Derivatives have three main uses. Hedgers use them to reduce risk — an exporter locks in a rupee-dollar rate, a portfolio manager protects against a market fall. Speculators use them to take leveraged views on price movements. Arbitrageurs exploit price differences between related markets, such as the cash and futures markets, which keeps prices aligned.

Leverage is the defining feature: you control a large exposure by paying only a margin (for futures) or a premium (for options). This makes derivatives efficient for hedging and also dangerous for speculation. SEBI studies have found that most individual traders in equity F&O lose money, which led to measures in 2024 such as larger contract sizes and fewer weekly expiries.

A key misconception is that derivatives are separate from the 'real' market. Because arbitrage links them to the underlying, heavy derivatives activity can influence cash market prices, especially near expiry. Another is that derivatives are only for speculation; many of the largest users are banks, exporters and insurers managing risk.

Example

A jeweller expects to buy 1 kg of gold in three months. Gold futures for that month trade at ₹72,000 per 10 grams. By buying futures equivalent to 1 kg, he fixes his cost at about ₹72 lakh. If gold rises to ₹76,000, his physical purchase costs ₹4 lakh more but his futures gain about ₹4 lakh; if gold falls to ₹69,000, the futures lose ₹3 lakh while his purchase is ₹3 lakh cheaper.

FAQs

Are derivatives securities in India?

Yes. The Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956 includes derivatives in the definition of securities, which is why exchange-traded equity, currency and commodity derivatives are regulated by SEBI. OTC derivatives involving banks, such as interest rate swaps and forex forwards, are regulated mainly by the RBI.

Why are derivatives considered risky?

Because of leverage. A small margin controls a large position, so a modest adverse move can wipe out the margin and create further losses. Options sellers can face very large losses, and option buyers often lose the entire premium. Understanding contract size, margins and risk is essential before trading.
