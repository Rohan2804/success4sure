What is Straddle and Strangle?

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Straddle and Strangle: A straddle combines a call and a put with the same strike and expiry; a strangle combines an out-of-the-money call and put with different strikes and the same expiry.

A long straddle means buying an at-the-money call and an at-the-money put. It profits from a large move in either direction and loses if the price stays near the strike. The maximum loss is the total premium paid, and there are two breakevens: strike plus total premium and strike minus total premium.

A long strangle uses an out-of-the-money call and an out-of-the-money put. It is cheaper than a straddle, but needs a larger move to become profitable, because the breakevens are further apart. Both are long-volatility strategies: they benefit if actual or implied volatility rises, and they suffer from time decay.

The reverse positions — short straddle and short strangle — involve selling the options. Sellers collect premium and profit if the price stays within the breakevens, but face large, theoretically unlimited losses on big moves. They are popular among option sellers in Indian index options, which is why SEBI's margin rules and expiry-day measures focus on short options.

Traders often buy straddles before events like results or the RBI policy, expecting a big move. The catch: option prices already include expected volatility, so implied volatility may fall after the event, and the move may not be large enough to cover the premium.

Example

Index at 24,000. Long straddle: buy 24,000 call at 210 and 24,000 put at 190 — total 400 points, ₹30,000 for a lot of 75. Breakevens: 23,600 and 24,400. If the index expires at 24,900, the call is worth 900 and profit is 500 × 75 = ₹37,500. Long strangle: buy 24,300 call at 90 and 23,700 put at 80 — total 170 points (₹12,750) with breakevens at 24,470 and 23,530.

FAQs

Which is better, a straddle or a strangle?

A straddle costs more but starts making money with a smaller move; a strangle is cheaper but needs a larger move. Choice depends on how big a move is expected and the price of options. For sellers, a short strangle gives a wider profit range but collects less premium than a short straddle.

What is the risk of a short straddle?

A short straddle profits only if the price stays within the two breakevens. A sharp move either way can cause large losses, theoretically unlimited on the upside. It also has high negative gamma, especially near expiry, so losses can grow quickly. Sellers often use stop-losses or buy far options to cap risk.
