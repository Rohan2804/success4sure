What is Black-Scholes Model?

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Black-Scholes Model: The Black-Scholes model is a mathematical formula that estimates the fair value of European options using the spot price, strike, time, interest rate and volatility.

Developed by Fischer Black and Myron Scholes in 1973, with contributions from Robert Merton, the model transformed option trading by providing a consistent way to price options. Scholes and Merton received the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1997. Indian exchanges and brokers use Black-Scholes-based calculations to show theoretical prices, implied volatility and Greeks.

The call value is C = S × N(d1) − K × e^(−rT) × N(d2), and the put value is P = K × e^(−rT) × N(−d2) − S × N(−d1). Here S is the spot price, K the strike, r the risk-free rate, T the time to expiry in years, σ the volatility, and N() the cumulative normal distribution. d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2)T] ÷ (σ√T), and d2 = d1 − σ√T. N(d1) is the call's delta.

Its assumptions include: European exercise; no dividends (in the basic version); constant volatility and interest rate; continuous trading with no transaction costs; and log-normally distributed prices. Real markets violate several of these — volatility changes, prices sometimes jump, and costs exist — which is why market prices show a volatility smile.

In exams, focus on the inputs and direction of effects rather than computing by hand: a higher spot raises calls and lowers puts; higher volatility raises both; more time generally raises both; a higher interest rate raises calls and lowers puts. The model's practical use is less about the 'right' price and more about a common language for volatility and risk.

Example

Spot ₹1,000, strike ₹1,000, 30 days (T = 0.0822), r = 7%, σ = 20%. Then d1 ≈ 0.129 and d2 ≈ 0.072, giving N(d1) ≈ 0.551 and N(d2) ≈ 0.529. Call ≈ 1,000 × 0.551 − 1,000 × e^(−0.00575) × 0.529 ≈ ₹25.79, and put ≈ ₹20.05. Check with put-call parity: 25.79 − 20.05 = 5.74 ≈ 1,000 − 1,000 × e^(−0.00575).

FAQs

Can Black-Scholes be used for Indian stock options?

Yes. Indian equity options are European style, which suits the model. Practitioners adjust for expected dividends and use implied volatility from the market rather than a single historical figure. Exchange and broker option calculators commonly use Black-Scholes or close variants.

What is the biggest limitation of Black-Scholes?

The assumption of constant volatility. In reality, volatility changes over time and differs across strikes, creating the volatility smile or skew. Sudden jumps in prices, for example after unexpected news, are also not captured well. Traders deal with this by using different implied volatilities for different strikes.
