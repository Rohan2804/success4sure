What is Loss aversion?

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Loss aversion: Loss aversion is the behavioural tendency to feel the pain of a loss more strongly than the pleasure of an equal gain, often by around twice as much.

Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory found that most people would reject a coin toss offering a ₹1,000 loss or a ₹1,500 gain, even though the expected value is positive. Losses loom larger than gains. This asymmetry shapes many investment mistakes.

One result is the disposition effect: investors sell winning holdings quickly to 'lock in' gains but hold losing ones for years, hoping to get back to the purchase price. Another is panic selling in market falls, when the pain of watching losses becomes unbearable. Loss aversion also makes people keep too much in fixed deposits and too little in equity for long-term goals, accepting a near-certain erosion by inflation to avoid visible fluctuations.

Advisers handle it by framing: showing how often equity has been negative over 1, 5 and 10 years rather than only daily moves; separating short-term money from long-term money so falls in the latter are less frightening; and agreeing in advance what to do in a crash.

Loss aversion is not the same as sensible risk aversion. Avoiding risk you cannot afford is prudent; avoiding all visible losses at the cost of long-term goals is the bias.

Example

Neeraj bought shares of Company P at ₹800 and Company Q at ₹500. P rises to ₹1,000 and Q falls to ₹350. Needing ₹50,000, he sells P because booking a 'profit' feels good and holding Q avoids 'accepting' a loss. Yet Q's prospects have weakened. Selling Q would also have produced a capital loss that could be set off against gains. His decision followed feelings, not analysis.

FAQs

How does loss aversion affect mutual fund investors?

It leads many to stop SIPs or redeem during market falls, exactly when units are cheapest, and to wait until markets recover before investing again. Over time this lowers their actual returns compared with the fund's own returns.

Is loss aversion always harmful?

Some caution is healthy and protects against reckless bets. It becomes harmful when it causes decisions that hurt long-term goals, such as holding failing investments to avoid booking a loss or keeping retirement money entirely in low-return assets.
