What is Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT)?

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT): Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT), developed by Harry Markowitz, shows how combining assets with less-than-perfect correlation can reduce portfolio risk for a given expected return.

Markowitz's 1952 insight was that an asset should not be judged in isolation. What matters is how it changes the risk and return of the whole portfolio. Risk is measured by standard deviation, and the key ingredient is correlation: how closely two assets move together.

If two assets are perfectly correlated, combining them does not reduce risk; portfolio risk is just the weighted average. If correlation is lower, the ups of one partly offset the downs of the other, and portfolio risk falls below the weighted average. This is the mathematical basis of diversification.

By testing all possible combinations, one can plot the efficient frontier: the set of portfolios giving the highest expected return for each level of risk. A rational investor should hold a portfolio on this frontier, choosing the point that matches their risk tolerance. Adding a risk-free asset leads to the capital market line and later to the capital asset pricing model.

MPT relies on estimates of returns, volatility and correlations from the past, which can change, especially in crises when correlations rise. It also assumes investors care only about mean and variance. Still, its core lesson, that correlation drives the benefit of diversification, underpins modern asset allocation.

Example

An investor splits money 50:50 between equity (expected return 12%, standard deviation 18%) and debt (7%, 6%). Expected portfolio return is 9.5% whatever the correlation. If correlation were +1, portfolio standard deviation would be 12%, the simple average. With correlation of 0.2 it falls to about 10.0%, and with −0.2 to about 8.9%. Same return, less risk, purely from combining assets that do not move together.

FAQs

What is the efficient frontier?

It is the curve showing portfolios that offer the highest expected return for each level of risk, or the lowest risk for each level of return. Portfolios below the curve are inefficient because another combination gives more return for the same risk.

Is modern portfolio theory still relevant?

Yes, as a framework. Its central idea, that combining assets with low correlation reduces risk, guides asset allocation everywhere. But practitioners know its inputs are uncertain and correlations shift in crises, so they use it with judgement rather than as a precise formula.
