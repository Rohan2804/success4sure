What is Rupee cost averaging?

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Rupee cost averaging: Rupee cost averaging is the effect of investing a fixed rupee amount at regular intervals, so more units are bought at low prices and fewer at high prices.

When you invest the same amount every period, the number of units you get is amount ÷ price. Low prices give more units and high prices give fewer. Your average cost per unit is the total invested divided by total units, which mathematically equals the harmonic mean of the prices paid. The harmonic mean is always at or below the simple average, so your average cost is lower than the average NAV over the period.

This does not mean you always make money. If a fund keeps falling, you keep buying at lower prices and your total value can still be below your investment. If a fund rises steadily, a lump sum at the start would have bought more units cheaply, and averaging gives a worse result.

The real benefit is behavioural. Investors who average do not need to time the market, and a fall stops being frightening because it buys more units. That is why the concept is taught in NISM mutual fund and adviser exams alongside SIPs.

Do not confuse rupee cost averaging with 'averaging down' in stocks, where someone buys more of a losing share hoping to reduce cost. Rupee cost averaging is a fixed, pre-decided schedule, not a reaction to losses.

Example

An investor puts ₹5,000 each month for five months into a fund whose NAV moves ₹50, ₹40, ₹25, ₹40, ₹50. Units bought: 100, 125, 200, 125 and 100, a total of 650 units for ₹25,000. Average cost = ₹25,000 ÷ 650 = ₹38.46. The simple average NAV is (50+40+25+40+50) ÷ 5 = ₹41. At the end NAV is back to ₹50, so the holding is worth ₹32,500, a gain of ₹7,500 even though NAV only returned to where it started.

FAQs

Does rupee cost averaging reduce risk?

It reduces timing risk, the risk of putting all your money in at a market peak. It does not remove market risk: if the underlying assets fall and stay down, a SIP investor still loses money. Think of it as smoothing the entry price, not protecting the investment.

Why is the average cost lower than the average NAV?

Because you buy more units when NAV is low. Those cheaper units carry more weight in your average. Mathematically your cost is the harmonic mean of prices, which is never higher than the arithmetic mean. The gap is wider when prices swing more.
