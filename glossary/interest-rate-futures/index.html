What is Interest Rate Futures (IRF)?

Last updated 17 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Interest Rate Futures (IRF): Interest rate futures are exchange-traded contracts whose value depends on the price of an interest-bearing instrument, such as a government bond or treasury bill.

In India, interest rate futures (IRFs) trade on NSE and BSE on underlyings such as 10-year Government of India bonds and 91-day treasury bills, with other tenors introduced from time to time. They are regulated by SEBI for exchange trading, within the framework set by the RBI. Bond futures are typically cash settled based on the weighted average price of the underlying bond.

The key relationship is inverse: when interest rates (yields) rise, bond prices fall, and so do bond futures. A bank holding government bonds can sell IRFs to protect against rising yields. A fund expecting to receive money and invest in bonds later can buy IRFs to lock in today's yields.

How much a bond price moves for a given change in yield is measured by modified duration. A bond with modified duration 7 falls about 7% if yields rise by 1 percentage point. Hedgers use duration to work out how many IRF contracts they need: hedge ratio = (portfolio value × portfolio duration) ÷ (futures value × futures duration).

The market is mostly used by banks, primary dealers, insurers and mutual funds. Liquidity in Indian IRFs has been uneven, which limits retail use. Students often get the direction wrong — remember: expectation of rising rates means sell bond futures; expectation of falling rates means buy them.

Example

A 10-year bond future trades at ₹100.00 per ₹100 face value, and the contract size is ₹2 lakh face value. A trader expecting yields to rise sells 10 contracts (₹20 lakh face value). Yields rise by 0.25 percentage point; with a modified duration of 7, the price falls about 1.75% to ₹98.25. Profit ≈ ₹20,00,000 × 1.75% = ₹35,000, before costs.

FAQs

Why do bond futures fall when interest rates rise?

Existing bonds pay fixed coupons. When new bonds offer higher yields, older bonds become less attractive, so their prices drop until their yield matches the market. Bond futures track these prices, so they fall too. The opposite happens when interest rates fall.

Who uses interest rate futures in India?

Banks, primary dealers, insurance companies, mutual funds and some corporates use them to hedge interest rate risk on bond portfolios or future borrowings. Traders and arbitrageurs also participate. Retail participation has been limited because of the specialised nature of the product and uneven liquidity.
