What is Sharpe ratio?

Last updated 23 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Sharpe ratio: The Sharpe ratio measures how much return a portfolio earned above the risk-free rate for each unit of total risk, measured by standard deviation.

Developed by William Sharpe, the ratio is (portfolio return − risk-free rate) ÷ standard deviation of portfolio returns. In India, a 91-day treasury bill or overnight rate is commonly used as the risk-free rate. A higher Sharpe ratio means the fund delivered more reward for the volatility it put investors through.

Its main use is comparing funds with similar objectives. Two large cap funds with the same 14% return are not equal if one got there with much bigger swings. Mutual fund factsheets show Sharpe ratio alongside standard deviation and beta, usually computed over three years of monthly data.

Advisers use it when constructing portfolios and reviewing whether a fund's extra return justifies its extra volatility. It appears in NISM investment adviser and research analyst syllabi.

Limitations are important. Sharpe assumes returns are roughly normally distributed and treats upside and downside volatility alike. It can look excellent for strategies that earn steady small gains and occasionally suffer large losses, until the loss happens. It also changes with the period chosen, and comparing a debt fund's Sharpe with an equity fund's says little about suitability.

Example

Over three years, Fund A returned 14% a year with a standard deviation of 16%; Fund B returned 12% with a standard deviation of 9%. The risk-free rate was 7%. Sharpe for A = (14 − 7) ÷ 16 = 0.44. Sharpe for B = (12 − 7) ÷ 9 = 0.56. Fund B earned less in absolute terms but delivered more excess return per unit of risk.

FAQs

What is a good Sharpe ratio for a mutual fund?

There is no universal threshold; it depends on the asset class and period. Use it to compare funds in the same category over the same period. A fund with a consistently higher Sharpe than its peers and benchmark has delivered better risk-adjusted returns historically.

What is the difference between Sharpe and Treynor ratio?

Sharpe divides excess return by total risk (standard deviation). Treynor divides excess return by beta, which captures only market-related risk. Treynor suits well-diversified portfolios where specific risk has been diversified away; Sharpe suits evaluating a portfolio on its own.
