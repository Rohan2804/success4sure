What is XIRR (Extended Internal Rate of Return)?

Last updated 25 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

XIRR (Extended Internal Rate of Return): XIRR, or extended internal rate of return, is the annualised return on a series of investments and withdrawals made on different dates, used especially for SIPs.

XIRR finds the single annual rate that, applied to every cash flow from its own date, makes the total present value equal to zero. Outflows (investments) are entered as negative numbers and inflows (current value or redemptions) as positive. Spreadsheets have a built-in XIRR function that takes the amounts and dates.

It solves a problem CAGR cannot. In a 12-month SIP, the first instalment was invested for a year but the last for only a month. Dividing total gain by total investment ignores this and understates the real annual return. XIRR weighs each instalment by the time it was invested.

Mutual fund statements, consolidated account statements and most investment apps report SIP returns as XIRR. Advisers use it to measure the actual return of a client's portfolio including additional purchases and withdrawals.

XIRR over very short periods can produce extreme annualised numbers, so treat a one-month XIRR with caution. It also reflects the investor's own timing, so two people in the same fund can have very different XIRRs.

Example

Amit invested ₹10,000 on the 5th of every month from January to December 2025, a total of ₹1,20,000. On 5 January 2026 his holding is worth ₹1,30,000. Absolute return is ₹10,000 ÷ ₹1,20,000 = 8.3%. But since the average rupee was invested for only about six and a half months, the XIRR works out to roughly 15.7% a year. In a spreadsheet: list the twelve dates with −10,000 and the final date with +1,30,000, then use =XIRR(amounts, dates).

FAQs

Why is XIRR higher than absolute return for SIPs?

Because absolute return treats all money as invested for the whole period. In a SIP, later instalments were invested for much less time. XIRR annualises the return based on how long each rupee was actually invested, so for short periods it is usually higher than the absolute percentage.

What is the difference between XIRR and IRR?

IRR assumes cash flows occur at regular intervals, such as every year. XIRR handles cash flows on any dates, which suits real-world investing where SIP dates, extra purchases and withdrawals are irregular. For evenly spaced flows, both give similar answers.
