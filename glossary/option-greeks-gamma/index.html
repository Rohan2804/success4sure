What is Gamma (Option Greek)?

Last updated 20 November 2026 · By the Success4Sure editorial team · Free

Gamma (Option Greek): Gamma measures how much an option's delta changes for a ₹1 change in the price of the underlying asset.

If delta is the speed at which an option's price changes, gamma is the acceleration. Both calls and puts have positive gamma for the buyer and negative gamma for the seller. A gamma of 0.004 means delta rises by 0.004 for every ₹1 rise in the underlying (and falls by the same for a ₹1 drop).

Gamma is highest for at-the-money options and lowest for deep in-the-money or far out-of-the-money options. It also rises sharply as expiry approaches for ATM options, because a small move can decide whether the option ends with value or worthless. This is why expiry-day option prices swing so wildly.

Long-gamma positions (bought options) benefit from big moves in either direction: delta grows in the favourable direction as the price moves. Short-gamma positions (sold options) suffer from big moves: the position becomes increasingly exposed in the wrong direction. Option sellers who hedge with delta must keep buying high and selling low as gamma pushes their delta around.

Students often think gamma only matters to professionals. In practice, it explains why a short straddle that looks safe in the morning can lose heavily by afternoon on expiry day. High gamma combined with leverage is the main reason expiry-day option selling is risky.

Example

A call has delta 0.50 and gamma 0.004 per ₹1. If the stock rises ₹20, delta becomes about 0.50 + 0.004 × 20 = 0.58. The premium rises not by 0.50 × 20 = ₹10 but by roughly the average delta × 20 = 0.54 × 20 = ₹10.80. On a lot of 1,000, that is ₹10,800 instead of ₹10,000 — the extra ₹800 is the gamma effect, which favours the buyer and hurts the seller.

FAQs

Is high gamma good or bad?

It depends on whether you are long or short options. Buyers have positive gamma, so large moves help them. Sellers have negative gamma, so large moves hurt them progressively. High gamma generally comes with high theta, so buyers pay for gamma through faster time decay.

Why does gamma increase near expiry?

With little time left, an at-the-money option's delta can swing from near 0 to near 1 with a small move, because the outcome of finishing in or out of the money becomes very sensitive to price. That rapid change in delta is high gamma.
