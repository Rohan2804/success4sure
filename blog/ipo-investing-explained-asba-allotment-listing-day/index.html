IPO Investing Explained: What Really Happens Between Applying and Listing Day

IPO investing in India has become a weekend sport: apply on Friday, check allotment on Tuesday, watch listing on Wednesday. But most applicants do not know why they missed allotment, what the money block means, or why a heavily subscribed issue can still list flat. Here is the full process, with the numbers, and an honest look at listing-day expectations.

By the Success4Sure editorial team · Updated 20 November 2026 · Editorial process

Key takeaways

  • Money is only blocked under ASBA; it is debited only if you get shares.
  • Approve the UPI mandate before the cut-off or your bid is invalid.
  • In oversubscribed issues, retail allotment is a lottery for one minimum lot; more lots do not raise your odds.
  • Shares list on T+3 after the issue closes under current SEBI rules.
  • Oversubscription and grey market premium do not ensure a listing gain.

What an IPO actually is

An initial public offering is the first time a company offers its shares to the public and lists them on a stock exchange. The offer can include a fresh issue, where new shares are created and the money goes to the company, and an offer for sale (OFS), where existing shareholders such as promoters or private equity funds sell part of their stake and the money goes to them, not the company.

That split matters. If most of an IPO is OFS, the company is not raising money to grow; early investors are cashing out. That is not automatically bad, since venture investors have to exit at some point, but you should know who is selling and why. The red herring prospectus (RHP) lists the objects of the issue and the selling shareholders in detail.

Price band, lot size and categories

Most Indian IPOs use book building: the company announces a price band, say ₹285 to ₹300, and investors bid within it. Applications are in lots. If one lot is 50 shares, a retail investor bidding at the upper end needs ₹15,000 per lot. Most retail applicants bid at the 'cut-off' price, which means they accept whatever final price is fixed within the band, and the block is calculated at the upper end.

The issue is divided into categories. Qualified institutional buyers (QIBs) such as mutual funds and insurers get one portion, with a part reserved for anchor investors who commit a day before the issue opens. Non-institutional investors (NIIs, often called HNIs) get another, and retail individual investors, who bid up to ₹2 lakh, get their own quota. The exact split depends on the type of company and the SEBI rules it falls under, so check the RHP.

ASBA and UPI: your money is blocked, not taken

Every public issue application must go through ASBA, Application Supported by Blocked Amount. When you apply through your bank's net banking or through your broker using UPI, the bid amount is blocked in your bank account but not debited. It keeps earning savings interest. Only if you receive an allotment is the relevant amount debited; the rest is unblocked.

With UPI, you approve a mandate request in your UPI app. If you do not approve it before the cut-off time, your application is not valid, a mistake that silently knocks out many first-time applicants. UPI-based bids are permitted for individual applications up to a limit (₹5 lakh as of 2026 — check SEBI's current circular), which covers all retail bids and smaller NII bids.

How retail allotment really works

When the retail portion is oversubscribed, SEBI rules require that as many retail applicants as possible receive at least the minimum lot. Allotment is done by a computerised lottery among valid applicants, with the registrar to the issue (RTA) running the process under exchange supervision.

Here is what that means in numbers. Suppose the retail quota is 10 lakh shares, the lot is 50 shares, so there are 20,000 lots available. If 4 lakh valid retail applications come in, only 20,000 applicants, about one in twenty, can receive a lot. Applying for 13 lots instead of one does not improve your odds of getting that one lot. It just blocks more money.

This is why families often apply from several PAN-linked accounts: each distinct PAN is a separate lottery ticket. Applying multiple times from the same PAN, however, gets all those applications rejected as duplicates.

The T+3 timeline

SEBI cut the listing timeline to T+3 from the issue closing date (T) for issues from December 2023. In practice: the issue closes on day T, allotment is finalised on T+1, shares are credited to demat accounts and unallotted money unblocked on T+2, and shares list on T+3. Check the specific dates in the RHP and on the exchange website for any issue, because holidays shift them.

Allotment status can be checked on the registrar's website or the exchange's IPO status page using your PAN or application number. If your money is not unblocked within the stated time, raise it first with your bank and then with the registrar.

Common reasons applications get rejected

Every large issue sees a slice of retail bids rejected for avoidable reasons. Knowing them saves you a wasted application and blocked money.

Mismatch between the PAN on the application and the PAN linked to the demat account is a frequent one, as is applying through a bank account that is not in the applicant's own name. Duplicate applications under the same PAN, across different brokers or banks, are all treated as invalid. Bids above the retail limit of ₹2 lakh are moved out of the retail category. And a UPI mandate left unapproved, or approved after the cut-off on the closing day, means the bid never counted at all. Approve mandates early, and confirm the bid status on the exchange website rather than assuming the broker's 'submitted' message is final.

Listing day: why oversubscription is not a promise

Listing day opens with a special pre-open call auction to discover a fair opening price, after which normal trading begins. Heavy subscription often leads to a listing above the issue price, because many applicants who missed allotment try to buy in the market. But it is not a rule. Issues that were subscribed many times have listed at a discount when overall market mood turned or when the valuation looked stretched.

The grey market premium (GMP) quoted in news and on social media is an unofficial, unregulated indicator. It is based on informal trades outside any exchange, has no investor protection and can be thinly traded or even manipulated. Treat it as gossip, not data.

Studies of Indian IPOs over the years have found that listing-day gains in hot markets do not reliably continue; many stocks drift below their listing price over the following year while others do very well. The point is that listing day answers one question (how excited is the market today) and not the important one (is this a good business at this price).

Questions to ask before applying

Treat an IPO like any other share purchase, with one handicap: the company has no listed price history. This post is for education only and is not investment advice.

  • How much of the issue is fresh money and how much is OFS, and who is selling?
  • What will the money be used for: debt repayment, capacity expansion, or general corporate purposes?
  • What is the valuation (P/E, P/B) at the upper price band compared with listed peers mentioned in the RHP?
  • What are the top risk factors the company itself discloses in the RHP?
  • Am I applying because I like the business, or only for listing gains?
  • Can I hold the shares calmly if they list below the issue price?

FAQs

Does applying for more lots increase my chance of IPO allotment?

In a heavily oversubscribed retail category, no. Allotment is decided by lottery among valid applicants for the minimum lot, so one lot and several lots have roughly the same chance. More lots only matter when the issue is undersubscribed or mildly oversubscribed, where applicants may receive more than the minimum.

What does cut-off price mean in an IPO application?

Bidding at cut-off means you agree to buy at whatever final price the company fixes within the price band. It is available to retail individual investors. Your bank blocks money at the upper end of the band, and if the final price is lower, the difference is released after allotment.

Is the grey market premium reliable for predicting listing price?

No. GMP comes from informal, unregulated deals outside stock exchanges, with no disclosure, settlement guarantee or investor protection. It can change sharply in a few days and has often been wrong. Rely on the RHP, the subscription data published by exchanges and your own view of the valuation instead.
