Why Most F&O Traders Lose Money, and What Risk Management Really Means

Ask why most F&O traders lose money and you will hear 'lack of discipline'. That is part of it, but SEBI's studies point to something more structural: costs, leverage, time decay and an uneven playing field work against the typical individual trader before psychology even enters. Here is what the data say, why the odds look the way they do, and what risk management actually means.

By the Success4Sure editorial team · Updated 23 November 2026 · Editorial process

Key takeaways

  • According to SEBI studies (check sebi.gov.in for the latest), a large majority of individual F&O traders lost money.
  • Costs act like a fee on every trade, so even breakeven skill loses money over time.
  • Leverage multiplies the cost of each mistake and shortens the learning runway.
  • Option buyers lose to time decay; option sellers face rare but large losses.
  • Real risk management sets an annual loss budget, per-trade and daily limits before trading.

What SEBI's studies found

SEBI has published studies of individual traders in the equity F&O segment, and the findings have been consistent: according to SEBI studies (check sebi.gov.in for the latest), a large majority of individual traders, roughly nine in ten, made net losses over the periods studied. Transaction costs added substantially to those losses, and a small minority made most of the profits. The studies also noted a rising share of young traders and traders from smaller cities.

Proprietary traders and foreign institutions, by contrast, made aggregate profits in the same periods. The money individuals lost did not disappear; much of it went to better-equipped participants and to transaction costs. Read the original SEBI reports on sebi.gov.in for the exact figures and methodology rather than relying on social-media summaries.

Reason 1: costs are a guaranteed loss on every trade

Every F&O trade pays brokerage, securities transaction tax (STT), exchange transaction charges, SEBI fees, stamp duty and GST. The STT on F&O was raised in recent years (check current rates as of 2026 on the income tax department or exchange websites). Each round trip may look small, but an active options trader doing several trades a day can rack up costs that run into lakhs of rupees a year on modest capital.

Think of it as playing a game in which you pay a fee every time you take a turn, win or lose. Even a trader with no skill disadvantage, whose trades are exactly coin flips, will slowly lose money to costs. To profit, you must be good enough not just to break even, but to overcome that fee on every trade.

Reason 2: leverage magnifies mistakes faster than skill

F&O lets you control large exposures with small margins. A trader with ₹2 lakh can hold a futures position worth many times that. A normal 1.5% adverse move in the index can then wipe out a large fraction of the account in a day. Leverage does not change your accuracy; it changes how much each mistake costs and how quickly you run out of capital to keep learning.

The cruel part is that early success with leverage teaches the wrong lesson. A beginner who doubles ₹50,000 in a month with aggressive positions often increases size, and the first ordinary bad week then takes far more than the original gain.

Reason 3: option buyers fight time decay, sellers fight tail risk

Most retail participants buy cheap, out-of-the-money options, especially near expiry, because the premium is small and the potential payoff looks huge. But such options expire worthless most of the time, and their value decays faster as expiry approaches. Buying a ₹20 option many times hoping for a ₹200 outcome is closer to buying lottery tickets than to trading.

Retail option sellers face the opposite problem: they win small amounts most of the time and then suffer a large loss on a sudden move. Neither side is safe. Each simply has a different way of losing money if the trader does not understand the payoff.

Reason 4: the playing field is not level

Individual traders compete with algorithmic systems that react in microseconds, proprietary desks with low costs and real-time data, and institutions that hedge large portfolios. When you place a market order on a weekly option at 2:45 pm, the person on the other side is often a machine designed to profit from exactly that kind of order. Information, speed and cost advantages are real, and they compound over thousands of trades.

SEBI has tightened rules to reduce excessive speculation, including larger minimum contract sizes for index derivatives, fewer weekly expiries, upfront collection of option premium and closer monitoring of position limits (as of 2026 — check SEBI circulars for the current framework). These measures change the economics but do not change the basic arithmetic described above.

Two traders, one year

Picture two people who each start the year with ₹3 lakh for F&O. Suresh buys weekly out-of-the-money options several times a day, never sizes trades in advance and adds money twice after big losses. Lata trades two or three times a week, risks 1% of capital per trade, stops after losing 3% in a month and journals every position.

Suresh may well have exciting months, but his costs alone run high, his options mostly expire worthless, and his top-ups mean he ends up losing more than his starting capital. Lata may also end the year with a loss; good process does not ensure profit. But her loss is limited by design, perhaps a fraction of her capital, and she has a hundred journaled trades that tell her whether to continue. The difference between them is not intelligence. It is whether risk was decided before trading or discovered during it.

What risk management really means

Risk management is often reduced to 'use a stop-loss'. Stop-losses help, but genuine risk management is about survival: making sure that no single trade, day or month can damage your finances or force you out of the game. It is decided before you trade, not during.

In concrete terms, it means deciding the total amount you are prepared to lose on trading this year, and treating that as a fee for learning, separate from your savings and investments. It means limiting the loss on any single trade to a small fraction of trading capital, often 1% or 2%, and having a daily loss limit after which you stop trading. And it means knowing the worst-case outcome of a position, including a gap move through your stop-loss, before you enter.

  • Annual risk budget: an amount whose total loss would not affect your rent, EMIs, goals or emergency fund.
  • Per-trade risk: a fixed small percentage of trading capital, calculated from entry and stop-loss.
  • Daily stop: after losing a set amount in a day, close the terminal.
  • No borrowed money: never trade F&O with loans, credit cards or money meant for family goals.
  • Review: a monthly look at net P&L after all charges and taxes, not just the winning trades.

A sober way to think about F&O

If you still want to learn derivatives, approach it as a professional would. Study the NISM equity derivatives syllabus, understand margins and the Greeks, paper-trade for a few months, then trade the smallest size possible while keeping a journal. Measure yourself after a hundred trades, net of all costs. If you are losing, the data are telling you something, and the most sensible response may be to stop.

There is nothing heroic about losing money slowly in a market that is statistically stacked against most individuals. Many who learn derivatives thoroughly end up using them only for occasional hedging, or for careers in broking, risk management and research where the knowledge pays without the personal risk. Nothing here is investment advice or a recommendation to trade.

FAQs

Is F&O trading the same as gambling?

Derivatives are legitimate regulated instruments used for hedging and price discovery. However, short-term speculation in them by individuals without an edge has outcomes that look similar to gambling: most participants lose, costs are high, and a few win. Whether it resembles gambling depends on how, why and with how much money you trade.

Do stop-losses prevent big losses in F&O?

Stop-losses help limit losses in normal conditions, but they do not guarantee an exit at your chosen price. Overnight gaps, sudden news and fast markets can cause execution far beyond the stop level. Options can also move sharply in illiquid strikes. Position sizing that accounts for gap risk is therefore as important as the stop-loss itself.

Where can I read SEBI's study on F&O trader losses?

SEBI publishes its research studies and press releases on sebi.gov.in under the reports and statistics sections. Search for studies on profit and loss of individual traders in the equity F&O segment. Reading the original report gives you the exact figures, time periods and methodology, which summaries on social media often distort.
