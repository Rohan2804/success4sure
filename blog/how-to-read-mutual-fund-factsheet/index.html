How to Read a Mutual Fund Factsheet: A Line-by-Line Guide for Beginners

Every AMC publishes a monthly factsheet for each scheme, packed with numbers most investors skip. Learning how to read a mutual fund factsheet takes about twenty minutes and changes how you look at funds. This guide walks through it section by section, explains the ratios in plain language, and flags the traps that catch beginners.

By the Success4Sure editorial team · Updated 23 November 2026 · Editorial process

Key takeaways

  • Check the factsheet date; holdings and ratios change monthly.
  • Read the objective, benchmark, TER, exit load and manager tenure first.
  • Top-10 concentration and market-cap split reveal risk better than the fund's name.
  • Compare performance with the benchmark TRI across several periods, not one year.
  • For debt funds, YTM minus TER and duration times rate change give a quick risk picture.

Where to find it and what it is

A factsheet is a monthly snapshot of a mutual fund scheme published by the AMC, usually as a PDF covering all its schemes. It summarises what the fund is trying to do, what it holds, how it has performed and how risky it has been. It is not the legal offer document; for that, you read the scheme information document (SID) and key information memorandum (KIM). But the factsheet is the most practical place to understand a fund month to month.

Factsheets are prepared as of a date, usually the last day of the previous month. Always check that date: holdings and ratios change, and a six-month-old factsheet can be misleading.

Section 1: The basics block

The top of each scheme page usually lists the fund's category, investment objective, benchmark, inception date, fund manager names with their tenure, AUM, minimum investment, exit load and the total expense ratio for both direct and regular plans. Read the objective in full. A fund described as investing 'predominantly in large-cap companies' should not suddenly look like a mid-cap fund in its holdings.

The exit load line tells you the cost of redeeming early, for example a 1% charge if units are redeemed within a year of purchase (an illustrative structure; each scheme sets its own). Fund manager tenure is worth noting: if the manager changed recently, the long-term track record may reflect someone else's decisions.

Section 2: Portfolio and concentration

The portfolio section lists holdings with their percentage weights, often grouped by sector. For equity funds, check three things. First, the top 10 holdings: if they make up 60% or more of the portfolio, the fund is concentrated and individual stocks matter a lot. Second, sector weights: a 35% weight in one sector is a big bet. Third, the market-cap split between large, mid and small caps, which tells you more about risk than the fund's name does.

Also note the cash or 'net current assets' line. A fund holding 10% cash is partly sitting out of the market, which can cushion falls but drags returns in rallies.

Section 3: Performance, read carefully

Performance tables show returns over periods such as one, three, five and ten years and since inception, alongside the benchmark (usually its total return index, TRI) and sometimes an additional benchmark such as Nifty 50 TRI. Returns over one year are shown as absolute or annualised; longer periods are usually CAGR.

Compare the fund with its benchmark, not with whichever fund topped last year's chart. A fund that beat its benchmark in five of the last seven calendar years tells you more than one with a spectacular single year. Remember the mandatory line about past performance: it may or may not be sustained in future. Also check whether the table shows the direct or regular plan, since their returns differ by the TER gap.

Section 4: Risk ratios for equity funds

Most factsheets list a few risk statistics, typically calculated over the last three years of monthly or daily returns. Here is what they mean in plain terms.

RatioWhat it tells youHow to read it
Standard deviationHow much returns swung around their averageHigher means bumpier; compare within the same category
BetaSensitivity to the benchmarkBeta of 0.9 means the fund moved about 90% as much as the market, on average
Sharpe ratioReturn earned per unit of total risk, above the risk-free rateHigher is better, but only compare funds in the same category and period
Portfolio turnoverHow often the portfolio is tradedHigh turnover suggests an active trading style and higher hidden costs

What these ratios cannot tell you

Ratios are backward-looking. A low standard deviation over three calm years says little about how a fund will behave in a sharp crash. Different AMCs may also use slightly different calculation methods and risk-free rates, so comparing a Sharpe ratio across factsheets from two AMCs is not perfectly apples to apples. Use ratios to ask better questions, not to rank funds mechanically.

A common beginner mistake is comparing ratios across categories: a liquid fund will always show a lower standard deviation than a small-cap fund. That says nothing about which is better; they do different jobs.

Reading the dividend and NAV details

Factsheets list NAVs for each plan and option, such as direct growth, regular growth and IDCW (income distribution cum capital withdrawal). In the IDCW option, payouts come out of the fund's own value, so the NAV falls by roughly the amount paid; it is not extra income on top of returns. Comparing NAVs across funds tells you nothing about which is cheaper or better, because a fund's NAV simply reflects when it started and how it has grown.

Section 5: Debt fund metrics

Debt fund pages carry a different set of numbers, and these are arguably more important than the equity ratios because they directly describe the risks you take.

  • Yield to maturity (YTM): the approximate annual return the portfolio would earn if all bonds were held to maturity at current prices, before expenses. It is not a promised return; prices and holdings change.
  • Modified duration: sensitivity to interest rate changes. A modified duration of 4 means a 1 percentage point rise in yields would cause roughly a 4% fall in the portfolio's value, and vice versa.
  • Macaulay duration and average maturity: related measures of how long the money is locked in bonds; SEBI's debt fund categories are defined using Macaulay duration bands.
  • Credit quality: the share in AAA, AA, sovereign and lower-rated paper. Lower ratings mean higher yield but higher default risk.

A worked reading of a debt fund page

Imagine a short duration fund showing a YTM of 7.4%, a modified duration of 2.5 and 85% in AAA and sovereign paper. Subtract a TER of, say, 0.4% for the direct plan, and the rough indicative yield is around 7%. If interest rates rise by 0.5 percentage points, the fund could lose around 1.25% of value in the short term (2.5 × 0.5), which the higher yield then gradually earns back. The 15% in lower-rated paper explains part of the yield and is where credit risk sits.

This kind of quick reading, YTM minus expenses, duration times rate change, credit mix, is the most useful skill a factsheet teaches for debt funds. It turns abstract terms into rupee consequences.

The riskometer and final checks

Each scheme carries a riskometer, a SEBI-mandated label ranging from low to very high risk, based on the portfolio and updated periodically. Check it against your own comfort with losses. A 'very high' label on a fund you thought was safe is a signal to read more carefully.

Before finishing, compare the current factsheet with one from six or twelve months ago. Changes in fund manager, a big jump in cash, a new sector bet or a rise in lower-rated debt holdings tell you more about where a fund is heading than any single month's return. FAQs

Where can I find the factsheet of a mutual fund?

Each AMC publishes monthly factsheets on its website, usually under a downloads or literature section. Some cover all schemes in one PDF. AMFI's website also provides scheme data such as NAVs and TERs. Make sure you are reading the latest month's factsheet, as holdings and ratios change regularly.

What is a good Sharpe ratio for a mutual fund?

There is no universal good number. The Sharpe ratio is useful mainly for comparing funds in the same category over the same period, using the same calculation method. A higher figure means more return per unit of risk in that period. It is backward-looking, so treat it as one input, not a ranking.

Is YTM the return I will get from a debt fund?

No. YTM is an indication of the portfolio's current yield if bonds were held to maturity at today's prices, before expenses. Actual returns depend on interest rate movements, changes in holdings, credit events and costs. Subtract the TER for a rough idea, and remember that it is not a promised return.
