Margin, Leverage and Margin Calls: How Borrowed Exposure Can Turn Against You

Margin and leverage are what let a trader with ₹1 lakh take a position worth ₹5 lakh or more. They are also the most common reason small losses turn into account-ending ones. This post explains how margins work across F&O, intraday and margin trading facility in India, how a margin call unfolds, and the rupee maths that shows why leverage cuts both ways.

By the Success4Sure editorial team · Updated 19 November 2026 · Editorial process

Key takeaways

  • Margin is a deposit; leverage is exposure divided by margin.
  • At 5x leverage a 2% adverse move costs 10% of your capital.
  • Unmet margin calls lead to forced square-offs at bad prices, plus possible penalties.
  • MTF magnifies gains and losses and charges interest every day.
  • Size by full exposure, not margin, and keep a buffer above the minimum.

Margin is a deposit, leverage is the ratio

Margin is the money you must keep with your broker to hold a position. It is a security deposit, not the price of the position. Leverage is the ratio between the exposure you control and the margin you put up. If you control ₹10 lakh of exposure with ₹2 lakh of margin, your leverage is 5 times.

Leverage does not change the percentage move in the underlying; it changes what that move does to your capital. At 5x leverage, a 2% move against you becomes a 10% loss of your margin. At 10x, the same move costs 20%. The market does not care how much you borrowed.

How F&O margins are set

For futures and option selling, the clearing corporation computes margins using a risk-based system. Historically this has been called SPAN margin, covering the worst likely one-day loss on the portfolio, plus an exposure margin as an extra buffer. Margins rise when volatility rises and can be increased further for specific contracts or around expiry. Option buyers pay the full premium upfront instead of margin.

Take an illustrative Nifty futures lot of 75 units at 25,000, an exposure of ₹18.75 lakh. If the total margin is about ₹2.5 lakh, leverage is roughly 7.5 times. A 1% move in Nifty, 250 points, changes your P&L by ₹18,750, which is 7.5% of your margin. Three bad days in a row can take a large bite out of the account. Margin rates and lot sizes change, so check NSE Clearing and your broker for current numbers (as of 2026).

Intraday leverage in the cash segment

After SEBI's peak margin framework was fully implemented, brokers must collect a minimum upfront margin for intraday trades in the cash segment, which caps the leverage they can offer (check SEBI and your broker for current limits). Before these rules, some brokers offered very high intraday leverage, which magnified retail losses.

Even within today's limits, intraday leverage can be substantial. If you buy ₹2.5 lakh of a stock intraday with ₹50,000 of margin, a 2% fall costs ₹5,000, or 10% of your money, in a few minutes. Brokers automatically square off intraday positions near the close, and they can close them earlier if your losses eat into the required margin.

Margin trading facility: leverage that charges interest

Margin trading facility (MTF) lets you buy shares for delivery by paying a part of the value while the broker funds the rest, with the shares pledged as collateral. Only approved stocks qualify, and the broker charges interest on the funded amount for every day you hold.

Here is how it can go wrong. Vikram puts in ₹1 lakh and borrows ₹1 lakh under MTF to buy ₹2 lakh of shares. If the shares rise 15%, his position is worth ₹2.3 lakh; after repaying ₹1 lakh plus interest, his gain is about ₹30,000 minus interest, roughly 30% on his own money. If they fall 20% instead, the position is worth ₹1.6 lakh, and after repaying ₹1 lakh plus interest he has less than ₹60,000 left, a loss of over 40%. And the interest keeps running whether the trade works or not.

How a margin call unfolds

A margin call is your broker's demand for more money when the value of your account falls below the margin required. In F&O, mark-to-market losses are settled daily, so a losing position drains your free margin every evening. If your balance falls below the requirement, the broker asks you to add funds, often with a deadline of the next morning or even within the same session in volatile markets.

If you cannot or do not add funds, the broker has the right to square off your positions, fully or partly, at whatever price the market offers. This often happens at the worst moment, during a sharp move, locking in the loss. Exchanges also levy penalties on short collection of margin, which brokers pass on to clients. With MTF and pledged shares, a fall in the collateral value can trigger the same process, and the broker may sell your pledged shares.

  • Step 1: the position moves against you; MTM loss reduces available margin.
  • Step 2: the account dips below the required margin; the broker sends an alert.
  • Step 3: you add funds, reduce the position, or do nothing.
  • Step 4: if you do nothing, the broker squares off positions; you bear the loss and any penalty.

Pledging shares for margin: useful but not free

Many investors pledge their long-term holdings or mutual fund units to get margin for trading. The pledged securities stay in your demat account, but a haircut is applied, so ₹1 lakh of shares might provide noticeably less than ₹1 lakh of margin. Exchanges also require that a portion of F&O margin be in cash or cash equivalents (check current rules).

The hidden risk is linkage. If the market falls sharply, your trading positions lose money at the same time that the value of your pledged shares, and therefore your margin, shrinks. A market crash can thus hit you twice and force the sale of investments you never intended to sell. That is how a short-term trading mistake can damage long-term savings.

A margin call, day by day

Put the pieces together with an example. Deepa holds ₹3 lakh in her trading account and buys one illustrative Nifty futures lot requiring ₹2.5 lakh of margin, leaving ₹50,000 spare. On day one Nifty falls 1%, about 250 points, and ₹18,750 is debited as mark-to-market loss. Her balance is ₹2,81,250, still above the requirement.

On day two volatility rises, the exchange increases margin requirements by a little, and Nifty falls another 1.2%. A further ₹22,500 is debited, leaving ₹2,58,750 against a higher requirement of, say, ₹2.65 lakh. Her broker sends a margin call. She has no spare cash that morning, so the broker squares off the lot at the open, after a further dip. In two and a half days, Deepa has lost roughly ₹50,000, a sixth of her account, on an index move of under 3%, and she is no longer in the trade if the market recovers. Had she held a single lot with ₹6 lakh in the account, she could have stayed in; had she not used leverage at all, a 3% dip would have been a minor fluctuation.

Using leverage responsibly, if at all

The safest rule is to size positions by full exposure, not margin. If you would not hold ₹18.75 lakh of Nifty in the cash market, a futures lot of the same exposure is too large for you, however small the margin. Keep a buffer well above the minimum margin so that normal volatility does not trigger forced exits. Avoid MTF for speculative trades, and never pledge savings meant for goals. Nothing here is investment advice or a recommendation to trade.

  • Calculate exposure, not margin, before every trade.
  • Keep spare cash above the margin requirement to absorb ordinary swings.
  • Know your broker's square-off rules and timings.
  • Treat interest on MTF as a certain cost against an uncertain gain.
  • Do not pledge long-term investments to fund short-term trades.

FAQs

What happens if I do not meet a margin call?

Your broker can square off your positions, fully or partly, without further notice to bring the account back within margin requirements. This locks in the loss at prevailing prices, which may be unfavourable in a volatile market. You may also be charged penalties for margin shortfall that the exchange levies on the broker.

Is MTF the same as a loan against shares?

Both involve borrowing with securities as collateral, but MTF is a broker facility to buy specific approved shares with partial payment, with those shares pledged. A loan against shares is usually from a bank or NBFC against shares you already own, for any purpose. Both charge interest and carry the risk of forced sale if prices fall.

Do option buyers face margin calls?

An option buyer pays the full premium upfront, so there is no margin call on a simple bought option; the maximum loss is the premium. Option sellers, futures traders and those holding combined strategies with short legs do face margin requirements and can receive margin calls when positions move against them.
