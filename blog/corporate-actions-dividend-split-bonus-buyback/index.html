Corporate Actions Decoded: What Dividends, Splits, Bonus Issues and Buybacks Do to Your Holding

When a company announces a split, bonus or buyback, the app shows your holding jumping or the price crashing overnight, and beginners panic or celebrate for no reason. Corporate actions mostly rearrange value rather than create it. Here we work through each one with a single holding of 100 shares so you can see exactly what changes and what does not.

By the Success4Sure editorial team · Updated 25 November 2026 · Editorial process

Key takeaways

  • Splits and bonus issues change share count and price, not the value of your holding.
  • Dividends reduce the share price by about the dividend amount and are taxed at your slab rate.
  • Bonus shares have zero cost and a fresh holding period, which matters for capital gains tax.
  • Since October 2024, buyback proceeds are taxed as dividend, with the share cost allowed as a capital loss.
  • Always confirm record date, ex-date and ratios in the exchange announcement.

The starting point: one holding, four events

Assume Arjun holds 100 shares of a hypothetical company bought at ₹1,000 each, so his cost is ₹1,00,000. The share currently trades at ₹1,200, so his holding is worth ₹1,20,000. We will apply each corporate action separately to this starting position and check three things each time: number of shares, value of the holding, and anything that affects tax.

Two dates appear in every announcement. The record date is when the company checks its register (through the depositories) to see who is entitled. The ex-date is the first trading day on which a buyer no longer gets the benefit. With T+1 settlement, the ex-date and record date are usually the same day for most actions; to be entitled, you must have bought before the ex-date. Always confirm dates in the exchange announcement.

Dividends: cash out, price adjusts

Suppose the company declares a dividend of ₹20 per share. Arjun receives ₹2,000. On the ex-date, the share price typically opens lower by roughly the dividend amount, other things being equal, because ₹20 of cash per share has left the company. His holding moves from ₹1,20,000 of shares to about ₹1,18,000 of shares plus ₹2,000 cash. Total value is the same on day one.

Since April 2020, dividends are taxed in the shareholder's hands at their income tax slab rate, and companies deduct TDS above a threshold amount per year (₹10,000 for resident individuals from April 2025, as of 2026 — check the income tax department). So someone in the 30% slab effectively keeps less of each rupee of dividend than someone in a lower slab. A high-dividend stock is not free money; it is part of the company's value being paid out, then taxed.

Dividends are still meaningful. A steady dividend backed by steady profits tells you the earnings are real cash, not just accounting. The useful question is whether the payout is sustainable, not how high the yield looks.

One practical point: do not buy a share just before the ex-date only to capture a dividend. The price drops by roughly the dividend, you pay tax on the dividend at your slab, and you pay transaction costs, so the strategy usually leaves you worse off than doing nothing.

Stock splits: more slices, same pizza

In a split, the face value of each share is divided. If the company splits shares of face value ₹10 into face value ₹2, each old share becomes five new ones. Arjun's 100 shares become 500, and the price adjusts from ₹1,200 to about ₹240. Holding value: 500 × ₹240 = ₹1,20,000. Nothing has changed except the number of slices.

For tax, his cost is spread across the new shares: ₹1,00,000 ÷ 500 = ₹200 per share, and the holding period continues from the original purchase date. Companies split shares mainly to make the price per share more accessible and improve trading liquidity. Any price rise after a split comes from sentiment or business performance, not the split itself.

Bonus issues: shares from reserves

A bonus issue gives existing shareholders free additional shares by converting part of the company's reserves into share capital. In a 1:1 bonus, Arjun receives one new share for each share held. He now has 200 shares, and the price adjusts from ₹1,200 to about ₹600. Holding value: ₹1,20,000. Again, no value is created on the day.

The key difference from a split is accounting: a bonus moves money from reserves to share capital while face value stays the same; a split changes the face value without touching reserves. For you, the economic effect is nearly identical. Tax treatment differs, though. The original 100 shares keep their cost of ₹1,000 each, while the 100 bonus shares have a cost of zero and a holding period that starts from their allotment date. If Arjun sells the bonus shares within twelve months, the whole sale value is a short-term capital gain. This trips up many investors at tax time.

Buybacks: the company buys its own shares

In a buyback, the company uses its cash to purchase its own shares, usually at a premium to the market price, through a tender offer or open-market purchases. In a tender offer at ₹1,400, Arjun can offer his shares. Because more shareholders usually tender than the company wants to buy, only a part is accepted. A separate reservation exists for small shareholders, which often gives them a better acceptance ratio. Suppose 20 of his 100 shares are accepted: he receives ₹28,000 and keeps 80 shares.

Tax rules for buybacks changed significantly from 1 October 2024. The entire buyback amount is now treated as a dividend in the shareholder's hands and taxed at slab rates, while the cost of the shares tendered is treated as a capital loss that can be set off against other capital gains (as of 2026 — check the income tax department for the current position). For Arjun, the ₹28,000 is taxable income, and his ₹20,000 cost of those 20 shares becomes a capital loss. For investors in higher tax slabs, this has made tendering much less attractive than before.

Why do companies buy back shares? It returns surplus cash, reduces the share count so that each remaining share owns a slightly bigger slice of future profit, and can signal that management thinks the shares are undervalued. Whether it helps continuing shareholders depends on the price paid: buying back expensive shares destroys value.

Other corporate actions you will meet

Rights issues let existing shareholders buy new shares, usually at a discount, in proportion to their holding; if you ignore them, your stake gets diluted, though rights entitlements can often be sold on the exchange. Mergers and demergers change which company's shares you hold, with ratios set in the scheme. Delisting offers let promoters take a company private through a price discovery process. This post is for education only and is not investment advice.

  • Read the exchange announcement, not just the app notification, for ratios and dates.
  • Your broker's average price may not adjust correctly for bonus or demerger; check your tax computation independently.
  • Charts from data providers adjust past prices for splits and bonus, so do not mistake an unadjusted chart for a crash.
  • Corporate actions rarely create value on the day; the business does that over years.

FAQs

Do I need to apply to receive bonus shares or split shares?

No. If you hold the shares in your demat account as of the record date, the depository credits the additional or split shares automatically, usually within a few days. You only need to act for optional events such as buyback tender offers, rights issues or open offers, where you must apply within the specified window.

Is a stock split a good sign for investors?

A split by itself does not change the company's value or your share of it. It reduces the price per share, which may improve liquidity and attract small investors. Companies often split after a strong price rise, so it can reflect past success, but future returns depend on the business, not on the split.

Why did my share price fall after the dividend was paid?

On the ex-dividend date the price usually drops by roughly the dividend amount because the cash has left the company and new buyers no longer receive it. Your total wealth is unchanged at that moment: lower share value plus the dividend cash. Market movement on that day can make the drop larger or smaller.
