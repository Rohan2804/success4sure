Is an MBA Worth It in India? How to Work Out Your Own ROI Before You Apply

Whether an MBA is worth it in India depends far less on the degree and far more on three numbers: what it really costs you, what your career would look like without it, and how long the gap takes to repay. Here is a simple framework, worked through with rupee figures, that you can apply to any college on your list.

By the Success4Sure editorial team · Updated 20 November 2026 · Editorial process

Key takeaways

  • Count opportunity cost: lost salary is often the largest part of an MBA's cost.
  • Compare likely post-MBA pay with where you would be anyway, not with today's salary.
  • Use median, not average, placement figures from official reports.
  • Run pessimistic, realistic and optimistic cases before deciding.
  • A career switch that is hard laterally is the strongest non-financial reason.

The question behind the question

People ask whether an MBA is worth it as if there were one answer. There isn't. A two-year programme at a well-placed school can transform one person's career and barely move another's. The useful question is narrower: compared with what I would otherwise do for the next five years, does this specific programme leave me better off?

That comparison has a financial side, which you can estimate, and a non-financial side (career switch, network, confidence, location), which you have to weigh honestly. Let us do the financial part first.

Step 1: Count the full cost, including the salary you give up

Fees are only part of the cost. For a two-year full-time programme, you also give up two years of salary and usually pay for hostel, food, laptop and travel. The salary you forgo, called opportunity cost, is often the largest single number.

Take an illustrative case. Priya is an analyst earning ₹8 lakh a year. Her target college's fees plus living costs come to, say, ₹22 lakh over two years (an assumed figure; fees vary enormously, so use the college's official fee schedule). Her opportunity cost is about ₹16 lakh of salary. Total economic cost: roughly ₹38 lakh. If she funds the fees with an education loan, add interest on top.

Step 2: Estimate the salary gap, not the headline package

Placement brochures tend to highlight averages and highest packages. What matters to you is the likely gap between your post-MBA salary and the salary you would have reached anyway. Priya at ₹8 lakh with normal increments might reach about ₹11 lakh in two years without the MBA.

Suppose, after reading the college's audited placement report and speaking to recent alumni, she judges a realistic post-MBA offer for someone like her to be around ₹18 lakh. Her annual gap is ₹7 lakh before tax, perhaps ₹5 lakh after tax. Use the median or a conservative figure rather than the average, which can be pulled up by a few large offers.

Step 3: Read the payback period sensibly

A payback of seven to eight years looks long, but it ignores two things. First, the gap often widens over time if the MBA moves you onto a faster track. Second, the loan interest and inflation push the other way. A rough rule many people use: if the realistic payback is under five years, the financial case is strong; if it is over ten years, you need strong non-financial reasons.

Run three versions: pessimistic, realistic and optimistic. If the decision only works in the optimistic case, that tells you something. If even the pessimistic case looks fine, you can apply with confidence.

Step 4: Value the things a spreadsheet misses

Some benefits of an MBA are real but hard to price. A career switch is the biggest: moving from, say, IT services into product management, consulting or corporate finance can be very hard laterally and much easier through campus placements. Location matters too, as does an alumni network in your target industry.

Equally, be honest about costs a spreadsheet misses: two years away from earning during your late twenties, stress, and the risk that the job market is weak in your placement year. Placement outcomes depend on the economy, the college and the individual; nobody can promise them.

  • Strong reasons: you want a career switch that is hard laterally; your industry values the credential; you have a clear specialisation goal.
  • Weak reasons: everyone in your peer group is doing it; you are unhappy with your current manager; you want to delay a career decision.

A second example: when the numbers say no

Consider Arjun, a software engineer earning ₹18 lakh a year with five years of experience. His opportunity cost for a two-year programme is about ₹38 lakh in salary alone, plus fees. If a realistic post-MBA offer for his profile is ₹24 lakh, against the ₹22 lakh he would likely earn anyway, the annual gap after tax may be little more than ₹1–1.5 lakh. On pure numbers, the payback runs into decades.

That does not mean Arjun should never do an MBA. It means his case rests entirely on non-financial reasons, such as switching from engineering into strategy or product roles, or on choosing a different format: a one-year programme, a part-time executive MBA, or a targeted certification. The framework did its job by showing which question he really needs to answer.

Notice the pattern. The higher your current salary, the larger your opportunity cost and the more the programme must deliver. Freshers and people early in their careers face a much lower opportunity cost, which is one reason the two-year route is popular with them, though they may find interviews harder without work stories to tell.

Alternatives worth comparing

Before committing, compare the MBA with alternatives that cost less. A one-year executive programme after five or more years of experience changes the opportunity-cost maths entirely. Professional certifications such as NISM modules, CFA levels or JAIIB and CAIIB for bankers can move a finance career meaningfully at a fraction of the cost. Internal moves within your company can also deliver a role change without a gap.

None of these is automatically better. The point is to compare like with like: what each route costs and what each route realistically leads to in five years.

Questions to ask any B-school before you pay

Ask for the full placement report, ideally audited or following a recognised reporting standard, and look at median salaries and the percentage of students placed. Ask how many students came from backgrounds similar to yours and where they went. Speak to at least three alumni from the last two batches, including someone who did not get a top offer. Check approvals and accreditation on official portals. These conversations will tell you more than any ranking.

Also ask practical questions that affect your ROI directly: what share of fees is refundable if you withdraw, whether the college has tie-ups for education loans and at what terms, how many students in your intended specialisation were placed in that function, and how the institute supports students who are not placed through campus. A college that answers these clearly and in writing is usually one that respects its students. Evasive answers are themselves a data point worth noting before you commit lakhs of rupees and two years.

FAQs

Is an MBA from a lesser-known college worth it?

It can be, but the maths is harder. Fees may be lower, yet the salary gap may also be small. Check verifiable placement data, alumni outcomes and accreditation, and compare against alternatives like certifications or internal promotions. If the realistic payback looks longer than ten years, think carefully before borrowing.

How much work experience is ideal before an MBA in India?

Many full-time programmes admit freshers, while one-year executive programmes expect several years of experience. Two to four years often helps in interviews and placements because you can speak from real work. More experience raises your opportunity cost, though, so weigh the trade-off for your own salary level.

Should I take an education loan for an MBA?

An education loan can make sense if your realistic post-MBA salary comfortably covers the EMI. Use an EMI calculator with the actual rate and tenure offered, and test whether you could still pay if placement takes longer or pays less than expected. This is general guidance, not financial advice.
