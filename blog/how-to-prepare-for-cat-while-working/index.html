How to Prepare for CAT While Working: A Realistic Plan for 9-to-7 Jobs

Thousands of people prepare for CAT while working, and the ones who do well rarely have more free time than you. They have a smaller, sharper plan: fewer hours used better, mocks treated as study material, and a schedule built around energy rather than wishful thinking. Here is that plan, with the trade-offs spelt out.

By the Success4Sure editorial team · Updated 20 November 2026 · Editorial process

Key takeaways

  • Plan for the 10–12 hours a week you actually have, not the 25 you wish you had.
  • Keep weekday tasks small and low-setup; let weekends carry mocks and analysis.
  • Every mock needs analysis time at least equal to the mock itself.
  • The skipped-but-solvable bucket is where most percentile gains hide.
  • Arithmetic and consistent daily reading give working candidates the best return on time.

Start with an honest hours audit

Most working aspirants plan for the week they wish they had. Before buying any material, track one ordinary work week: when you leave home, when you get back, how tired you are at 10 pm, and how much of Sunday disappears into chores and family. A typical private-sector employee in Mumbai or Bengaluru with a 60–90 minute commute usually finds 60–75 minutes on weekdays and 5–6 hours across the weekend that are genuinely usable.

That adds up to roughly 10–12 hours a week. Over a nine-month runway (say January to November) that is about 400 hours, which is enough for a serious attempt if the hours are spent well. Planning for 25 hours a week and managing 8 is far worse than planning for 11 and hitting 11, because missed targets quietly kill motivation by March.

Split the year into three phases

Working aspirants do better with phases than with a giant syllabus checklist. Each phase has one job, and you know when to move on.

  • Phase 1, foundations (roughly months 1–4): arithmetic (percentages, ratios, time-speed-distance, time and work), basic algebra, reading habits, and the core DILR set types. One sectional test a week, no full mocks yet.
  • Phase 2, application (months 5–7): one full mock every 10–14 days, plus a detailed analysis session. Fill gaps in geometry, modern maths and harder DILR sets. Reading comprehension practice becomes daily.
  • Phase 3, mock-driven (months 8–10): one to two mocks a week, each followed by analysis that takes at least as long as the mock itself. Very little new theory; mostly selection skill and stamina.

A weekly template that survives a bad week

The plan below assumes about 11 hours. The key design choice is that weekdays carry small, low-setup tasks and weekends carry the heavy ones. On a day when work runs late, you drop the weekday slot without guilt; the weekend still holds the week together.

DayTimeWhat to do
Mon–Thu60 min eachOne RC passage + 15–20 quant questions on the current topic, or one DILR set
FridayOff or 30 minLight vocabulary or para-jumbles; protect sleep
Saturday3 hoursSectional test or full mock (Phase 2–3)
Sunday2.5–3 hoursMock analysis, error log review, one weak topic

Use your commute and lunch break, but only for the right tasks

Commute time is useful but low quality. Reading long editorials or opinion essays on your phone builds the reading speed CAT's VARC section rewards, and it needs no pen. Flashcards for quant formulas also work. What does not work on a crowded local train is DILR: a set needs a notebook, quiet and 25 focused minutes.

A 20-minute lunch break can hold two para-summary or odd-sentence questions. Small blocks like these add roughly 2–3 hours a week without eating into evenings, which is often the difference between finishing the quant syllabus by May or by August.

Treat mocks as the syllabus, not the exam

The most common mistake among working candidates is taking mocks as score checks. With limited hours, every mock must become a lesson. After each one, sort every question into four buckets: attempted and correct, attempted and wrong, skipped but solvable, and skipped correctly. The third bucket is where your percentile improvement hides.

Suppose in a mock you attempt 18 quant questions, get 13 right, and later find that 6 of the skipped ones were easy. At +3 and −1, your score was 13×3 − 5×1 = 34. Had you picked those 6 easy questions instead of 5 tricky wrong ones, the score would have been 19×3 = 57. Same knowledge, better selection. Mock analysis trains exactly this, and it needs no extra theory hours.

Keep an error log: one line per mistake, noting the topic, why you got it wrong (concept, calculation, misread, time) and the fix. Review it every Sunday. By September the log becomes your personal revision book.

Section-wise priorities for people short on time

VARC rewards consistency more than cramming. Thirty minutes of daily reading across economics, philosophy, science and culture moves your accuracy over months; a weekend binge does not. For verbal ability, para-summary and odd-sentence-out questions have patterns you can learn.

DILR is the section where working professionals often have an edge: analysing messy information is part of many jobs. The skill to build is set selection. In the exam you might see four or five sets and solving two cleanly can beat touching all of them. Practise spending the first few minutes scanning before committing.

For quant, arithmetic carries a large share of questions and is the best return on time. Get percentages, ratio, averages, mixtures and time-speed-distance solid before heavy geometry or number theory. Many working candidates score well by being very accurate in arithmetic and selective elsewhere.

Protecting energy, job and relationships

Burnout, not lack of intelligence, ends most working-professional attempts. Sleep below six hours damages the reading accuracy that VARC depends on. Tell your manager early only if you trust the relationship; otherwise simply plan leave around the exam date and the last two weekends before it.

Be explicit with family about your Saturday mock slot. Three protected hours that everyone knows about are better than six hours constantly interrupted. And keep one evening a week completely free; preparation that runs for nine months needs a pressure valve.

Traps that quietly derail working aspirants

The first trap is material hoarding. Downloading five sets of notes and three video courses feels productive, but with ten hours a week you can finish one good source properly. Pick one core resource per section and stick to it until the end of Phase 1.

The second is comparing yourself with full-time students on social media. A college student posting eight-hour study days is not your benchmark; your own mock trend over six weeks is. The third is skipping analysis when a mock goes badly. Bad mocks are the most valuable ones to analyse, because they show exactly which topics or habits collapse under pressure.

Finally, watch out for the March slump. Enthusiasm fades after the New Year resolution energy wears off, and appraisal season often lands at the same time. Pre-plan a lighter fortnight around your appraisal so a dip does not turn into quitting.

What to do in the final eight weeks

Stop starting new topics around early September unless a topic keeps appearing in your mock errors. Shift to full mocks at the same time of day as your exam slot, so your brain is used to peaking then. Revise your error log and formula sheet rather than reading new material.

In the last fortnight, reduce mock frequency to protect confidence and take a day or two of leave before the exam. Also start thinking about which other exams you will write: many working aspirants also register for XAT, CMAT or NMAT as a backup, and their patterns differ enough that a few targeted practice sessions help.

FAQs

Is it possible to crack CAT while working a full-time job?

Yes, many candidates take CAT while working, and work experience can help later at the interview stage. What matters is consistent, focused hours and serious mock analysis rather than total hours. No plan guarantees a particular percentile, but a realistic weekly schedule followed for eight to ten months gives you a fair shot.

Should I quit my job to prepare for CAT?

For most people, no. Quitting adds financial pressure and creates a gap you will have to explain in interviews. It can make sense only if your job leaves almost no usable time and you have savings to cover a year. Try a structured plan with your job first and judge after three months of honest tracking.

How many mocks should a working professional take for CAT?

Quality matters more than count. Around 20–30 well-analysed full mocks across the year, plus sectional tests, is a reasonable range for many candidates. Ten mocks analysed properly will teach you more than forty taken back to back without reviewing why you got questions wrong or skipped them.
