How Mutual Funds Are Taxed in India in 2026, With a Worked Example

How mutual funds are taxed depends on three things: what the fund holds, how long you held each unit, and whether you took gains or IDCW payouts. The rules changed several times between 2023 and 2026, and the new Income-tax Act now applies. This guide sets out the current framework and works through a full example, with a reminder to check official sources.

By the Success4Sure editorial team · Updated 20 November 2026 · Editorial process

Key takeaways

  • Tax depends on fund type, holding period of each unit and whether you take IDCW.
  • Equity funds: 20% short-term; 12.5% long-term above ₹1.25 lakh a year (as of 2026).
  • Debt-oriented funds bought from April 2023: gains taxed at slab rate regardless of holding period.
  • Every SIP instalment has its own holding period; redemptions follow first-in, first-out.
  • Switches count as redemptions; check incometax.gov.in for current rates before acting.

First, a note on the new law

The Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the Income-tax Act, 1961 from 1 April 2026. It renumbers sections and replaces the old 'previous year' and 'assessment year' with a single 'tax year', but the capital gains rates for mutual funds described below were broadly carried over from the rules in force after the July 2024 Budget. Because section numbers and some details changed, and Finance Acts can amend rates every year, treat every rate here as as of 2026 — check the income tax department (incometax.gov.in) for current rules.

Throughout, the figures exclude surcharge and include health and education cess of 4% where we calculate tax. This is general education, not investment or tax advice for your situation.

The three tax buckets

Mutual funds fall into three broad buckets for capital gains, based on the fund's portfolio, not on its name.

Equity-oriented funds in detail

If you redeem equity fund units within twelve months of buying them, the gain is short-term capital gain taxed at 20%. Beyond twelve months, it is long-term, and the first ₹1.25 lakh of total long-term gains from listed equity shares and equity-oriented funds in a tax year is exempt; the rest is taxed at 12.5%. The exemption is per person per year and is shared across all your equity holdings, not per fund.

Securities transaction tax of a tiny percentage is deducted on redemption of equity fund units, and stamp duty applies on purchases. Units bought before 1 February 2018 have a grandfathering rule that uses the 31 January 2018 value as the cost if it is higher, which matters only for old holdings.

Debt and other funds in detail

Funds that invest more than 65% in debt and money market instruments are treated as 'specified mutual funds'. For units bought on or after 1 April 2023, all gains are deemed short-term and added to your income, taxed at your slab rate, regardless of how long you hold. The old benefit of indexation for debt funds held over three years no longer applies to these units. Units bought before April 2023 and held over 24 months are taxed at 12.5% without indexation under the current rules.

Funds that are neither equity-oriented nor specified, such as some hybrid funds with moderate equity, gold funds and international fund of funds, follow the general rules for other capital assets: long-term after 24 months for unlisted units (12 months for listed units such as ETFs), taxed at 12.5% without indexation; short-term gains taxed at slab rates. Classification depends on the fund's actual portfolio limits, so check the scheme documents or the fund house's tax note.

SIPs: every instalment is its own purchase

With a SIP, each monthly instalment buys units on a different date, so each lot has its own holding period. When you redeem, units are treated as sold on a first-in, first-out basis. If you started an equity fund SIP 14 months ago and redeem everything today, the first two instalments are long-term and the remaining twelve are short-term, taxed at 20%.

This catches many investors who think 'I started my SIP more than a year ago, so it is all long-term'. If you plan a redemption, check the purchase dates in your consolidated account statement; waiting a few months can move more units into the long-term bucket.

A worked example

Karan, a salaried professional in the 30% slab under the regime he has chosen, makes three redemptions in tax year 2026-27. All figures are illustrative.

First, equity index fund units bought three years ago for ₹4,00,000, redeemed for ₹6,10,000. Long-term gain = ₹2,10,000. After the ₹1,25,000 exemption, taxable gain = ₹85,000. Tax at 12.5% = ₹10,625; with 4% cess, ₹11,050.

Second, flexi-cap fund units bought eight months ago for ₹1,50,000, redeemed for ₹1,80,000. Short-term gain = ₹30,000. Tax at 20% = ₹6,000; with cess, ₹6,240.

Third, short-duration debt fund units bought in 2024 for ₹2,00,000, redeemed for ₹2,28,000. Gain = ₹28,000, taxed at his 30% slab = ₹8,400; with cess, ₹8,736. Total tax on ₹2,68,000 of gains: ₹26,026. Notice that the debt fund gain, though the smallest, carries the highest rate.

Common mistakes at tax time

The most common error is assuming a fund's name decides its tax treatment. A 'balanced' or 'multi-asset' fund may be taxed as equity, as debt or as an 'other' fund depending on its portfolio limits; the fund house's annual tax note and the scheme information document state the treatment. A second error is ignoring small redemptions, such as a few thousand rupees taken from a liquid fund. Every redemption with a gain must be reported, and your registrar's capital gains statement makes this straightforward.

A third mistake is redeeming equity units a few weeks before they turn long-term. On a ₹60,000 gain, the difference between paying 20% short-term tax (₹12,480 with cess) and paying nothing because the long-term gain sits within the ₹1.25 lakh exemption can be significant. Checking purchase dates before redeeming costs nothing. Finally, investors sometimes forget that IDCW payouts and redemptions appear in the annual information statement (AIS) shared with the tax department, so mismatches in the return can lead to notices.

IDCW, switches and losses

Income distributions under the IDCW option (earlier called dividends) are added to your income and taxed at your slab rate. The fund house deducts TDS when payouts from a fund house exceed a threshold in a year (₹10,000 from April 2025, as of 2026 — check the income tax department (incometax.gov.in) for current rules). For investors in higher slabs, IDCW is often less tax-efficient than the growth option, where tax arises only on redemption.

Switching from one scheme to another, or from a regular plan to a direct plan of the same scheme, is treated as a redemption and a fresh purchase, so it can trigger capital gains tax. Capital losses can be set off: short-term losses against short- or long-term gains, long-term losses only against long-term gains, with unabsorbed losses carried forward for up to eight years if your return is filed on time.

Some investors redeem and reinvest equity units each year to use the ₹1.25 lakh long-term exemption, sometimes called gains harvesting. It can reduce tax over time but involves exit loads, a brief time out of the market and record-keeping, so weigh it carefully.

  • Rebates available under the new regime may not apply against tax on special-rate capital gains; check current rules.
  • Capital gains statements from registrars help you report each lot correctly.
  • Gains from mutual funds must be reported in the capital gains schedule of your return, even when no tax is due.

FAQs

Is the ₹1.25 lakh exemption available separately for each mutual fund?

No. The exemption applies to your total long-term capital gains from listed equity shares and equity-oriented mutual fund units in a tax year, combined. If you have ₹80,000 of such gains from shares and ₹1,00,000 from equity funds, only ₹1.25 lakh in total is exempt and the remaining ₹55,000 is taxable at the long-term rate.

Are debt mutual funds still better than fixed deposits for tax?

For units bought on or after 1 April 2023, gains from debt-oriented funds are taxed at your slab rate, much like FD interest. The difference is timing: FD interest is taxed every year as it accrues, while debt fund gains are taxed only on redemption, which can defer tax. Returns and risks also differ, so tax is only one factor.

Do I pay tax on a SIP if I have not redeemed anything?

No. Capital gains tax arises only when you redeem or switch units. Simply holding a growth option fund, even as its NAV rises, does not create taxable income. If you have chosen an IDCW option, however, any payouts during the year are taxable at your slab rate, even without a redemption.
