Index Funds and ETFs Explained: Same Index, Different Plumbing

Index funds and ETFs both copy an index such as the Nifty 50, so they often get treated as the same thing. The investment inside is similar; the plumbing is not. How you buy, what price you pay, what it costs and how closely each follows the index can differ meaningfully. Here is a practical guide to both, with the numbers that actually separate a good passive fund from a poor one.

By the Success4Sure editorial team · Updated 23 November 2026 · Editorial process

Key takeaways

  • Index funds and ETFs both copy an index; the difference is how you buy, hold and pay.
  • Index funds transact at NAV with no demat needed; ETFs trade at market prices on the exchange.
  • An illiquid ETF can trade at a premium or discount to NAV that outweighs its low cost.
  • Tracking difference hits returns; tracking error measures how steadily a fund follows the index.
  • Choosing which index to track matters more than choosing the wrapper.

What passive funds try to do

An index fund or exchange-traded fund (ETF) holds the same securities as its benchmark index in the same proportions, aiming to deliver the index's return minus costs. The fund manager makes no attempt to pick better stocks. If the Nifty 50 rises 12% in a year including dividends, a well-run Nifty 50 fund should deliver slightly less than 12%, with the gap explained mainly by its expenses.

That simplicity is the appeal. You know what you own, costs are generally lower than actively managed funds, and there is no risk that a manager's bets go wrong. You still carry full market risk: when the index falls 25%, so does your fund.

Index funds: mutual fund units at NAV

An index fund is an open-ended mutual fund scheme. You buy and redeem units directly with the fund house (or through a platform) at the day's NAV, determined by the cut-off time rules. No demat account is needed, though you can hold units in demat if you choose. SIPs, STPs and SWPs are easy to set up, and you can invest any amount above the scheme minimum, including fractional units.

Because the fund must handle daily inflows and redemptions, it keeps a small amount of cash and trades frequently to match flows, which adds a little to tracking error. Expense ratios on index funds are typically low compared with active funds, but they vary across fund houses tracking the same index, so compare them.

ETFs: units traded on the stock exchange

An ETF is also a mutual fund scheme, but its units are listed and traded on NSE or BSE like shares. You need a demat and trading account, you buy at the market price during trading hours, and you pay brokerage and other transaction charges. Large investors and designated market makers can create or redeem ETF units with the fund house directly in big blocks; individual investors usually trade on the exchange.

The key practical issue is that the market price of an ETF can differ from its NAV. Fund houses publish an indicative NAV (iNAV) during market hours. In a liquid ETF with active market makers, the price usually stays close to iNAV and the bid-ask spread is narrow. In a thinly traded ETF, you may pay a premium when buying or accept a discount when selling, which can wipe out the cost advantage of a low expense ratio.

Tracking error vs tracking difference

These two terms are often mixed up, and the difference matters. Tracking difference is the gap between the fund's return and the index's total return over a period. If the Nifty 50 Total Return Index gained 12.0% and the fund gained 11.7%, the tracking difference is 0.3 percentage points. It mostly reflects the expense ratio plus trading costs and cash drag.

Tracking error is the standard deviation (variability) of that gap over time, usually calculated from daily returns. A low tracking error means the fund follows the index closely day to day; a high one means its returns wander around the index. SEBI requires passive funds to disclose tracking error, and AMFI publishes data you can compare.

For a long-term investor, tracking difference is usually what hits your wallet. On ₹10 lakh held for 15 years with the index compounding at an illustrative 11% a year, a fund with 0.2% tracking difference would grow to roughly ₹46.6 lakh, while one with 1.0% would reach about ₹41.8 lakh. That gap of nearly ₹5 lakh comes from a difference that looks tiny in a single year. The 11% is only an assumption for the arithmetic, not an expected return.

Costs beyond the expense ratio

The expense ratio is deducted daily from the fund's NAV, so you never see it as a separate charge. For index funds, there are usually no other costs except an exit load if you redeem within a short period, which some schemes charge, and stamp duty on purchases. For ETFs, the visible costs add up differently: brokerage on each trade, the bid-ask spread, DP charges when you sell, and any premium you pay over iNAV.

Consider Pooja, who invests ₹5,000 every month. If her broker charges a flat fee per ETF order and her ETF trades with a spread of a few paise on a ₹250 unit, her costs per purchase can be a noticeable fraction of each small instalment, and she also ends up with uninvested cash because she can only buy whole units. The same ₹5,000 into an index fund is fully invested at NAV. For a ₹5 lakh lump sum, the comparison often tilts the other way, because fixed charges become negligible and the ETF's lower expense ratio matters more over the years.

Choosing the index matters more than the wrapper

Many investors spend hours on index fund versus ETF and minutes on which index to track. The index is the bigger decision. A Nifty 50 fund owns India's largest companies; a Nifty Next 50 fund owns the next 50, which have historically been more volatile; a Nifty 500 or total market fund spreads across large, mid and small companies; a midcap or smallcap index adds more risk.

There are also 'smart beta' or factor indices, such as momentum, low volatility, quality or equal-weight. These follow fixed rules, so they are passive in execution, but they make an active bet on a factor. They can outperform or underperform the broad market for long stretches. Sectoral and thematic index funds concentrate risk further. Understand the index methodology, published by the index provider, before investing.

Which wrapper suits whom

If you invest a fixed amount every month through a SIP, do not have or want a demat account, and value simplicity, an index fund is usually more convenient. If you already have a demat account, invest larger lump sums occasionally, and choose an ETF with good trading volume and a tight spread, an ETF can be slightly cheaper.

Some fund houses also offer fund of funds that invest in their own ETFs, which give the convenience of an index fund with the ETF inside; check the combined expense ratio. Whatever you choose, compare expense ratio, tracking difference over several years, and for ETFs, volume and spread. This is general education, not investment or tax advice for your situation.

  • Check the fund's tracking difference over one, three and five years against the total return index.
  • For ETFs, look at average daily traded value and compare the price with iNAV before placing an order.
  • Use limit orders for ETFs to avoid paying an unexpectedly wide spread.
  • Avoid trading ETFs in the first and last few minutes of the session, when spreads are often wider.

FAQs

Is an ETF cheaper than an index fund?

ETFs often have lower expense ratios, but you also pay brokerage, the bid-ask spread and possibly a premium to NAV when buying. For small monthly investments, these costs can outweigh the expense ratio saving. For larger lump sums in a liquid ETF, the total cost can be lower. Compare all costs, not just the expense ratio.

What is iNAV in an ETF?

iNAV, or indicative NAV, is an estimate of the ETF's per-unit value calculated during market hours from the live prices of its underlying holdings. It is published by the fund house or exchange. Comparing an ETF's market price with its iNAV shows whether you are paying a premium or receiving a discount when you trade.

Do index funds have zero risk because they are passive?

No. Passive only means the fund does not pick stocks; it still holds the same securities as the index. If the index falls sharply, the fund falls by about the same amount. Index funds remove the risk of a manager's choices underperforming, but not market risk, concentration in the index or tracking errors.
