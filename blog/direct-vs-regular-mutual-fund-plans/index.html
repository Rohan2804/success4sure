Direct vs Regular Plans: What the Expense Ratio Really Costs You, in Rupees

Every mutual fund scheme comes in two versions, direct and regular, holding the same portfolio but charging different expense ratios. The gap looks tiny, often under one percentage point. Worked through over 20 years in rupees, it becomes surprisingly large. Here is how the direct vs regular mutual fund choice really works, and when paying more can still make sense.

By the Success4Sure editorial team · Updated 26 November 2026 · Editorial process

Key takeaways

  • Direct and regular plans hold the same portfolio; only the cost differs.
  • The TER is deducted daily from the NAV, so you never see a separate bill.
  • A 0.8-point TER gap can grow into lakhs of rupees over 20 years.
  • A regular plan can be worth it if you genuinely receive good service and guidance.
  • Switching plans is a redemption: check exit loads and tax first.

Same portfolio, two price tags

Since January 2013, SEBI has required mutual funds to offer a direct plan alongside the regular plan for each scheme. Both plans invest in exactly the same securities, managed by the same fund manager. The only difference is cost. A regular plan includes a commission paid by the AMC to the distributor who sold it to you; a direct plan has no distributor commission, so its expense ratio is lower.

Because the costs differ, the two plans have different NAVs. Over time, the direct plan's NAV grows slightly faster. That is why a direct plan's NAV is usually higher than the regular plan's for a scheme that has existed for a while.

What the expense ratio actually is

The total expense ratio (TER) is the annual cost of running the scheme, expressed as a percentage of its assets. It covers fund management fees, administration, registrar and custodian costs and, in regular plans, distribution commission. SEBI caps the maximum TER based on the type of scheme and its size, with lower caps for larger funds; the exact limits are revised from time to time, so check sebi.gov.in or the AMC's website for current figures as of 2026.

Crucially, you never see a separate bill. The TER is deducted from the scheme's assets daily, in tiny slices, and the NAV you see is already net of it. That invisibility is exactly why many investors underestimate it.

The ₹ example: one year

Take a ₹10 lakh investment in an equity scheme. Assume the regular plan has a TER of 1.5% and the direct plan 0.7%. (Illustrative figures; actual TERs vary by scheme.) In the first year, the regular plan costs roughly ₹15,000 and the direct plan roughly ₹7,000. The difference of about ₹8,000 is, broadly, what goes towards the distributor's commission and related costs.

₹8,000 on ₹10 lakh does not sound dramatic. The problem is that it repeats every year, on a growing balance, and the money lost also loses its own future growth.

The ₹ example: twenty years

Now assume the scheme's portfolio earns 12% a year before expenses, a purely hypothetical figure used only to show the arithmetic; actual returns will differ and may be negative in some years. After costs, the direct plan compounds at about 11.3% and the regular plan at about 10.5%.

Over 20 years, ₹10 lakh in the direct plan grows to about ₹85.1 lakh, while the regular plan grows to about ₹73.7 lakh. The gap is roughly ₹11.4 lakh, more than the original investment. For a SIP of ₹10,000 a month over the same 20 years under the same assumptions, the direct plan reaches about ₹84.5 lakh against about ₹76.8 lakh for the regular plan, a gap of around ₹7.7 lakh.

So is a regular plan always a bad idea?

Not necessarily. The extra cost pays for something: a distributor who helps with fund selection within your risk profile, paperwork, KYC, nomination, rebalancing reminders and, perhaps most valuable, talking you out of panic selling during a market crash. For an investor who would otherwise stop SIPs at the bottom or chase last year's top performer, good handholding can be worth far more than 0.8% a year.

The honest question is whether you are actually receiving that service. If your distributor has not contacted you in three years, you are paying for help you do not get. If you choose direct plans, you take on the responsibility for selection, monitoring and behaviour yourself.

Your three realistic options

Investors in India broadly choose between three routes, each with different costs and responsibilities.

  • Regular plan through a mutual fund distributor: you pay through the higher TER; the distributor provides service and must follow AMFI's code of conduct and SEBI rules.
  • Direct plan on your own: lowest cost; you handle selection, monitoring and discipline.
  • Direct plan with a SEBI-registered investment adviser (RIA): you pay the adviser a fee separately, the adviser has no commission from products, and recommends direct plans.

Why small percentages feel smaller than they are

Our brains compare percentages with returns, not with rupees. A 0.8% cost feels trivial next to a 12% return. But it is a cost on the whole corpus, every year, not on the gains alone. In a year when the fund returns only 3%, a 1.5% TER takes half of the gain. In a year when the fund falls 10%, the TER is still charged, deepening the loss slightly.

A better habit is to translate TER into rupees on your actual balance. If your mutual fund holdings are worth ₹25 lakh, every 0.1 percentage point of expense ratio is roughly ₹2,500 a year. Seen that way, it becomes easier to judge whether the service you receive is worth what you pay, whichever route you choose.

Remember also that TER is not the only cost. Exit loads, stamp duty on purchases and, for actively traded portfolios, the hidden trading costs reflected in portfolio turnover all reduce what you finally earn, though TER is usually the largest and most comparable one.

Switching from regular to direct

If you decide to move, remember that switching from a regular plan to the direct plan of the same scheme is treated as a redemption and a fresh purchase. That can trigger exit loads if units are within the exit-load period, and capital gains tax on the gains redeemed. Tax rates and holding-period rules have changed in recent years, so check the current position with the income tax department or a qualified tax professional before switching a large amount.

A practical approach many investors use is to start new SIPs in direct plans while letting older regular-plan units run until they are outside the exit-load period and the tax impact is acceptable. Your Consolidated Account Statement shows which of your holdings are in which plan.

How to check what you hold

Look at the scheme name on your account statement or CAS. Direct plans include the word 'Direct' in the plan name; if it is absent, you probably hold a regular plan. The factsheet and the AMC website list the TER of both plans, usually updated monthly. Compare them side by side to see the gap for your own funds. FAQs

Why is the NAV of a direct plan higher than the regular plan?

Both plans start with the same NAV when introduced, but the direct plan deducts lower expenses every day. Over time, this small daily difference compounds, so the direct plan's NAV grows slightly faster. A higher NAV does not mean it is more expensive; it reflects the lower costs.

How do I know the expense ratio of my mutual fund?

The total expense ratio for each plan is published on the AMC's website and in the monthly factsheet, and AMFI also publishes TER data. Check both the direct and regular plan figures for your scheme. TERs can change, so look at the current figure rather than an old document.

Is a direct plan right for every investor?

Not necessarily. Direct plans cost less, but you take on fund selection, monitoring and staying disciplined during market falls. If you are unsure, consider either a qualified distributor whose service you actually use, or a SEBI-registered investment adviser who charges a fee and recommends direct plans.
