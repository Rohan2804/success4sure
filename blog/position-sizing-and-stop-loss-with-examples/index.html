Position Sizing and Stop-Losses: The Rupee Maths That Keeps Traders in the Game

Ask a new trader how many shares they bought and the answer is usually 'whatever my money allowed'. Position sizing flips that around: you decide how much you are willing to lose first, place a stop-loss where your idea is proven wrong, and let those two numbers decide the quantity. Here is the rupee maths, plus why drawdowns are so hard to climb out of.

By the Success4Sure editorial team · Updated 20 November 2026 · Editorial process

Key takeaways

  • Decide the rupee risk first; quantity = risk per trade ÷ (entry − stop).
  • A wider, logical stop with fewer shares beats a tight, arbitrary stop with more shares.
  • Breakeven win rate = 1 ÷ (1 + reward-to-risk), before costs.
  • Recovery is asymmetric: a 50% loss needs a 100% gain.
  • Stop-losses can slip in gaps, so size overnight positions conservatively.

Start from the loss, not the profit

Every trade has two numbers that matter more than the target: where you will exit if you are wrong, and how much money that exit will cost. Beginners obsess over targets because they are pleasant to imagine. Professionals start from the loss because it is the only part of a trade they can control.

The fixed-fractional method is the simplest way to do this. You decide that no single trade will cost more than a fixed percentage of your trading capital, commonly 0.5% to 2%. That figure, in rupees, is your risk per trade. The distance between your entry and stop-loss is your risk per share. Divide one by the other, and you have your position size.

A worked example

Anjali has ₹4,00,000 of trading capital, kept separate from her savings. She decides to risk 1% per trade, which is ₹4,000. She wants to buy a stock at ₹820 because it has bounced from a level where it found buyers several times before. If it falls below ₹795, she believes the setup has failed, so she places her stop-loss there. Risk per share = ₹820 − ₹795 = ₹25.

Position size = ₹4,000 ÷ ₹25 = 160 shares. Position value = 160 × ₹820 = ₹1,31,200, about a third of her capital. If the stop is hit, she loses about ₹4,000 plus charges, exactly as planned. If the stock rises to her target of ₹880, she gains ₹60 × 160 = ₹9,600, a reward-to-risk ratio of 2.4.

What the table shows

The wider your stop, the fewer shares you buy, and the rupee risk stays the same. This frees you to put the stop where it makes sense on the chart or in your analysis, instead of squeezing it tight so you can buy more shares. A tight stop placed at an arbitrary level gets hit by normal noise; a logical stop with a correctly reduced quantity gives the trade room without risking more money.

Notice also that the position value can be large relative to capital when the stop is tight. If Anjali used a ₹10 stop, the formula would say 400 shares worth ₹3.28 lakh, almost her whole capital. Most traders add a second cap, such as no single position above 25% or 30% of capital, to avoid concentrating everything on one idea.

Where to place the stop-loss

A stop-loss belongs at the price that proves your reason for entering was wrong, not at a round number or a fixed percentage chosen for comfort. If you bought because support held at ₹800, the stop goes a little below that support, allowing for normal fluctuation. If you bought on a breakout above ₹850, a fall back below the breakout level suggests the breakout failed.

Remember that a stop-loss order is not a guarantee of price. A stop-loss market order executes at the best available price once triggered, which can be worse in a fast fall. A stop-loss limit order sets a worst price but may not execute at all in a gap. Overnight gaps can jump straight past your level, so if you hold positions overnight, size them as though the loss could be bigger than planned.

Win rate and reward-to-risk work together

A strategy does not need to win most of the time to make money. It needs the average win, multiplied by how often you win, to exceed the average loss multiplied by how often you lose, after costs. The breakeven win rate for a given reward-to-risk ratio R is 1 ÷ (1 + R).

At a reward-to-risk of 1, you need to win more than 50% of trades. At 2, you need just over 33%. At 3, 25%. That is why traders favour setups with larger potential rewards relative to the stop. But be honest: targets are often not reached, and a setup that 'should' give 3R may average 1.5R in practice. Measure your real averages from your journal rather than using planned ones.

Why small risk per trade matters: drawdown maths

Losing streaks are normal. Even a strategy that wins half the time will see runs of six, eight or ten losses in a row over a few hundred trades. With 1% risk per trade, ten straight losses reduce capital by about 9.6%. With 5% risk per trade, the same streak cuts capital by about 40%.

The deeper problem is that recovery is asymmetric. A 10% loss needs an 11.1% gain to get back to where you were. A 20% loss needs 25%. A 40% loss needs 66.7%, and a 50% loss needs 100%. Large risk per trade does not just cause bigger drawdowns; it makes them disproportionately hard to recover from, and it usually triggers emotional decisions such as doubling up to win back losses.

Common sizing mistakes

The most frequent mistake is sizing by conviction: putting more money into trades that feel certain. Feelings of certainty are poorly correlated with outcomes, and the biggest losses in many journals come from the 'sure shot' trades. A second mistake is not recalculating risk as capital changes. After a 20% drawdown, 1% of capital is a smaller rupee amount; keeping the old rupee risk means risking a larger percentage just when confidence is lowest.

A third is ignoring correlation. Three positions in three private banks are not three independent risks; one piece of sector news can hit all of them together. Treat highly correlated positions as one larger position when you add up total open risk. Finally, many traders forget costs. If your round-trip charges are ₹150 and your planned risk is ₹1,000, the real risk is ₹1,150, and on small trades that difference distorts every statistic in your journal.

Sizing rules worth writing down

Rules only work if they are decided in calm moments and followed in stressful ones. Write these in your trading journal before your next trade. Nothing here is investment advice or a recommendation to trade.

  • Risk per trade: a fixed percentage of trading capital, recalculated monthly as capital changes.
  • Maximum position size: a cap as a share of capital, regardless of how tight the stop is.
  • Maximum open risk: total risk across all open positions, for example no more than 4% to 5% at once.
  • Daily and monthly loss limits: stop trading for the day or month when they are hit.
  • Never move a stop-loss further away once the trade is live.
  • For F&O, size by the full contract exposure and gap risk, not by the margin required.

FAQs

Is risking 1% per trade too conservative?

It may feel slow, but it allows you to survive long losing streaks that every strategy experiences, and keeps drawdowns recoverable. Many experienced traders risk between 0.5% and 2% per trade. The right figure depends on your strategy's win rate and your tolerance for drawdowns, which you can only judge from your own journal data.

Should I use a percentage stop-loss like 5% on every trade?

A fixed percentage ignores the structure of each trade. A 5% stop may be too tight for a volatile small-cap stock and too loose for a stable large-cap. It is usually better to place the stop where your trade idea is invalidated and then reduce the quantity so that the rupee risk stays within your limit.

How do I size a position in futures?

Calculate the rupee loss per lot if your stop-loss is hit: points between entry and stop multiplied by the lot size. Divide your rupee risk per trade by that figure to get the number of lots. If even one lot exceeds your risk limit, the trade is too large for your capital and should be skipped.
