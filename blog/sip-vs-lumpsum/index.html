SIP vs Lumpsum: A Clear Way to Think About It, With Worked ₹ Examples

The SIP vs lumpsum debate is usually framed as a contest with a winner. It isn't. Each method suits a different situation: where your money comes from, how markets move after you invest, and how you will behave if they fall. Two worked rupee examples show what really happens, including the case where SIP loses.

By the Success4Sure editorial team · Updated 20 November 2026 · Editorial process

Key takeaways

  • For monthly salary income, a SIP simply matches how money arrives.
  • For a one-time sum, lumpsum has often done better historically, but not always.
  • SIPs win in dip-then-recovery periods; lumpsum wins in steadily rising ones.
  • The real value of staggering is behavioural: it reduces regret and panic selling.
  • An STP from a liquid fund is a sensible middle path for lumpsum money.

First, separate two different questions

Most people who ask about SIP vs lumpsum are actually asking one of two quite different questions. The first is: 'I earn a salary every month; should I invest monthly or save up and invest once a year?' The second is: 'I have ₹5 lakh sitting in my account today, from a bonus, an inheritance or a matured FD; should I invest it all now or spread it out?'

For the first question, a SIP is usually the natural answer because it matches how the money arrives. Waiting twelve months to accumulate a lumpsum just keeps money idle. The second question is the genuinely interesting one, and that is where most of this post focuses.

How a SIP works, in one paragraph

A systematic investment plan invests a fixed amount at regular intervals, usually monthly, into a mutual fund scheme. Each instalment buys units at that day's NAV. When the NAV is low, your fixed amount buys more units; when it is high, it buys fewer. This mechanical effect is called rupee cost averaging. It does not guarantee a profit or protect against loss; it simply means your average cost per unit ends up lower than the simple average of the NAVs you bought at.

Example 1: a market that falls, then recovers

Suppose you have ₹1,20,000. Option A invests it all as a lumpsum at a NAV of ₹100, buying 1,200 units. Option B invests ₹10,000 a month for 12 months. Over that year, the NAV drifts down to ₹80 in month five, then recovers to ₹115 by month twelve. (These NAVs are invented for illustration.)

The SIP buys 125 units at ₹80 and only about 87 units at ₹115. Across the year it accumulates about 1,245 units, at an average cost of about ₹96.37 per unit. At the final NAV of ₹115, the SIP is worth about ₹1,43,200 while the lumpsum is worth ₹1,38,000. In a dip-then-recovery year, the SIP comes out ahead because it bought heavily during the dip.

Example 2: a market that rises steadily

Now take a year where the NAV climbs steadily from ₹100 to ₹140. The lumpsum still buys 1,200 units at ₹100, worth ₹1,68,000 at the end. The SIP buys fewer and fewer units as the price rises: about 998 units at an average cost of ₹120.22, worth about ₹1,39,700.

Here the lumpsum is ahead by more than ₹28,000. This is not a quirk. Because equity markets have historically risen over long periods more often than they have fallen, investing a lumpsum immediately has often beaten spreading it out, simply because more money spends more time invested. That is a historical tendency, not a promise, and past performance may not be repeated.

One fairness point: in the SIP case, the money not yet invested would sit somewhere, perhaps in a savings account or liquid fund, earning a little. That narrows the gap slightly but rarely closes it in a strongly rising market.

So why do so many people prefer SIPs?

If lumpsum often wins on paper, why do advisers and distributors so often recommend staggering? Because investors are human. The biggest risk with a lumpsum is not the maths; it is regret. Someone who invests ₹5 lakh and watches it become ₹4 lakh three months later may panic and redeem at the bottom, turning a temporary fall into a permanent loss.

Spreading the investment reduces that regret. If markets fall, you are pleased that most of your money is still waiting to buy cheaper. If they rise, you are pleased that some of it is already invested. Behavioural comfort that keeps you invested is worth something real, even if it costs a little expected return.

The middle path: STP

A systematic transfer plan offers a compromise for lumpsum money. You place the full amount in a liquid or ultra-short duration fund of an AMC, then set up a monthly transfer into an equity fund of the same AMC over, say, 6 to 12 months. The waiting money earns debt-fund returns instead of sitting idle, and you still get the staggered entry.

Keep the STP period sensible. Spreading a lumpsum over three or four years mostly means keeping it out of the market for a long time, which defeats the purpose if your horizon is long. Remember too that each transfer is technically a redemption from the source fund, so check exit loads and the tax treatment of those redemptions; tax rules change, so verify the current position as of 2026 with the income tax department or a qualified professional.

What about waiting for a correction?

A third option many people quietly choose is to keep the lumpsum in a savings account and wait for the market to fall before investing. It sounds sensible but rarely works in practice. Nobody can reliably predict when a correction will come or how deep it will be, and markets can rise for long stretches before a fall that may still leave prices above where you started waiting. Meanwhile the money earns little. If timing worries you, a defined STP schedule is a more disciplined answer than open-ended waiting.

A simple decision framework

Rather than asking which method wins, ask these four questions about your own situation.

  • Where is the money coming from? Monthly income suits a SIP; a one-time sum opens up lumpsum or STP.
  • How long is the horizon? For goals ten or more years away, the entry method matters less than staying invested.
  • How would you react to an immediate 20% fall? If honestly badly, stagger the entry.
  • Is the money truly long-term? Equity is not the place for funds you may need in one to three years, however you invest.

Common confusions to clear up

A SIP is not a product; it is a way of investing in a mutual fund scheme. 'SIP returns' are the returns of the underlying scheme, measured over the dates of your instalments, usually using XIRR because the money goes in at different times. You can stop, pause or change most SIPs without penalty from the AMC, though exit loads may apply to units redeemed early.

Also, rupee cost averaging is not the same as buying at the lowest price. It reduces the impact of bad timing; it does not eliminate it. And neither SIP nor lumpsum changes the fund's underlying risk. A volatile small-cap fund remains volatile whichever way you enter. FAQs

Is SIP better than lumpsum for long-term investing?

Neither is always better. For regular income, SIPs are natural. For a lumpsum amount, investing at once has often done better historically when markets rose, while staggering helps in volatile or falling periods and reduces regret. Over long horizons, staying invested matters more than the entry method.

What is rupee cost averaging in simple terms?

When you invest a fixed amount regularly, you buy more units when prices are low and fewer when they are high. As a result, your average cost per unit is lower than the simple average of the prices on your purchase dates. It reduces the impact of poor timing but does not guarantee profits.

Should I stop my SIP when the market falls?

Many investors stop SIPs during falls, which means they stop buying exactly when units are cheaper. If your goal and horizon have not changed, continuing usually fits the logic of a SIP. If your financial situation has changed, review your plan with a registered adviser before deciding.
