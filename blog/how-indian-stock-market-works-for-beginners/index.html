How the Indian Stock Market Actually Works: Follow One ₹10,000 Order From Tap to Demat

Most beginners learn how the Indian stock market works as a list of acronyms: SEBI, NSE, BSE, NSDL, CDSL, T+1. This guide does it differently. We follow one ₹10,000 buy order from the moment you tap 'Buy' to the morning the shares appear in your demat account, and meet each institution exactly where it does its job.

By the Success4Sure editorial team · Updated 26 November 2026 · Editorial process

Key takeaways

  • Every trade passes through broker, exchange, clearing corporation and depository, with SEBI overseeing all of them.
  • Indian equity trades settle on T+1: shares and money move the next trading day.
  • The clearing corporation guarantees settlement, so retail investors rarely face counterparty risk.
  • Shares sit in your name with NSDL or CDSL, not with the broker.
  • SEBI protects against fraud and misconduct, not against a falling share price.

Start with the map, not the jargon

Think of the market as a relay race with five runners. You place an order with a stock broker. The broker sends it to a stock exchange, where it is matched with a seller. A clearing corporation steps in to guarantee that both sides settle. A depository moves the shares electronically into your demat account, held through a depository participant. Above all of them sits the regulator, the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI), which writes the rules and punishes those who break them.

Every single trade, whether it is ₹500 or ₹5 crore, passes through this same chain. Once you understand who holds the baton at each stage, terms that appear in news, broker emails and NISM exam questions start making sense on their own.

Step 1: you place the order with a broker

Say Priya, a 23-year-old analyst in Pune, wants to buy 20 shares of a company trading around ₹500. She logs into her broker's app and places a limit order: buy 20 shares at ₹498 or lower. A limit order sets the maximum she is willing to pay; a market order would accept whatever the best available price is at that moment.

Before the order leaves the broker, its risk system checks that Priya has at least ₹9,960 plus estimated charges in her trading account. Since SEBI's peak margin and upfront collection rules, brokers cannot let you buy first and pay later in the cash segment unless you have opted for a specific facility such as margin trading. That check is why the order is either accepted or rejected within a second.

Step 2: the exchange matches it

NSE and BSE run continuous electronic order books during market hours (normal trading runs from 9:15 am to 3:30 pm, with a pre-open session before it; check the exchange website for current timings). Every buy and sell order sits in the book ranked by price and then time. Priya's bid at ₹498 waits until a seller is willing to sell at ₹498 or below. When a match happens, the exchange generates a trade confirmation and her broker sends her a contract note by the end of the day.

Two things often surprise beginners here. First, you never know who sold you the shares; the exchange matches anonymous orders. Second, the same company can trade at slightly different prices on NSE and BSE at the same second, because each exchange has its own order book. Arbitrageurs quickly close any meaningful gap.

Step 3: clearing and T+1 settlement

Matching is a promise, not a delivery. Settlement is when money and shares actually change hands. India moved all equity trades to T+1 settlement in January 2023, meaning a trade done on Monday (T) settles on Tuesday (T+1), assuming Tuesday is a trading day. An optional same-day (T+0) settlement has also been introduced for a limited set of stocks; check SEBI and exchange circulars for its current scope.

The clearing corporation is the quiet hero. Through novation it becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. If the person who sold to Priya defaults, the clearing corporation still has to deliver, using its settlement guarantee fund and the auction mechanism. That is why retail investors in India rarely worry about counterparty risk on exchange trades.

  • Trade day (T): order matched, contract note issued.
  • T+1: funds debited and shares credited to the buyer's demat account; the seller receives money.
  • If a seller fails to deliver, the shares are bought in an auction and the defaulting seller bears the cost.

Step 4: the depository credits your shares

Shares no longer exist as paper certificates for practical purposes. They are entries in one of two depositories: National Securities Depository Ltd (NSDL) or Central Depository Services Ltd (CDSL). Your demat account is an account with one of these, opened through a depository participant, usually your broker or bank. Each security has a unique ISIN, so the depository knows exactly which share is moving.

On T+1, Priya's 20 shares are credited to her demat account. When she sells later, she authorises the debit either with a TPIN and OTP or through a DDPI (demat debit and pledge instruction) she signed with her broker. That extra authorisation exists to stop brokers from moving clients' shares without permission, a problem SEBI cracked down on after some broker defaults.

Where SEBI fits in, and what it does not do

SEBI registers and supervises exchanges, brokers, depositories, mutual funds, investment advisers and research analysts. It writes rules on disclosure, insider trading, market manipulation and investor protection, and it can bar people from the market or levy penalties. Exchanges also act as front-line regulators of their members.

What SEBI does not do is protect you from losing money on a bad investment. If a company's business deteriorates and the share falls 60%, that is market risk, not misconduct. Investor protection covers fraud, broker default (to a limit, through the exchange's investor protection fund) and unfair practices. For complaints, the route is your broker first, then SEBI's SCORES portal and the online dispute resolution (ODR) mechanism.

What Priya actually paid

Her ₹9,960 purchase does not cost exactly ₹9,960. On top come brokerage (zero for delivery at some brokers, a flat fee or percentage at others), securities transaction tax (STT), exchange transaction charges, SEBI turnover fees, stamp duty on the buy side, and GST on the brokerage and exchange charges. When she sells, DP charges apply per scrip. Rates change from time to time, so check your broker's charges page and the exchange website as of 2026.

For a long-term investor buying a few times a year, these costs are small. For someone trading the same ₹10,000 ten times a day, they compound into a serious drag. That single observation explains a lot about why frequent trading is harder than it looks.

Common confusions cleared up

Beginners often mix up the primary market and the secondary market. The primary market is where a company issues new shares, for example in an IPO, and the money goes to the company or selling shareholders. The secondary market is where existing shares change hands between investors; the company receives nothing when you buy its shares on NSE.

Another confusion is between the trading account and the demat account. The trading account is where your money sits and orders are placed; the demat account is where shares are stored. Most brokers open both together, which is why they feel like one thing. This post is for education only and is not investment advice.

  • Sensex and Nifty 50 are indices, not exchanges; they summarise how a basket of large companies is moving.
  • A broker going bust does not mean your shares vanish, because they sit with the depository in your name.
  • Market hours, settlement cycles and charges are set by rules that change; always confirm current details on the NSE, BSE or SEBI websites.

FAQs

Do I need both a demat and a trading account to buy shares?

Yes, for buying listed shares on an exchange. The trading account is used to place orders and hold funds, while the demat account stores the shares electronically with NSDL or CDSL. Most brokers open both through a single KYC process, so it usually feels like one account even though they serve different purposes.

What happens if my broker shuts down?

Your shares are held in your demat account with a depository, not in the broker's own name, so they remain yours. You can transfer them to another depository participant. Funds lying with the broker are a different matter; exchanges have investor protection funds that compensate eligible claims up to limits, which you should check on the exchange website.

Is T+1 settlement the same as being able to sell on the next day?

Not exactly. T+1 is when the shares are credited after a purchase. Many brokers let you sell shares bought yesterday even before they reach your demat account (a BTST trade), but this carries short-delivery risk if the original seller defaults. Settlement rules are separate from what your broker's platform permits.
