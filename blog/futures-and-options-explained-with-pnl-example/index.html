Futures and Options Explained Simply, With a Rupee-by-Rupee P&L Example

Futures and options explained in most places stop at definitions. This guide works through actual rupee numbers on an illustrative Nifty contract: what a futures trade gains or loses, how a call option buyer and seller fare at different expiry levels, and why the same contract can feel cheap to one side and dangerous to the other. Read it before you trade a single lot.

By the Success4Sure editorial team · Updated 25 November 2026 · Editorial process

Key takeaways

  • A futures lot carries exposure many times the margin, so small index moves cause large P&L swings.
  • Futures are marked to market daily; a margin shortfall can force your exit.
  • An option buyer's loss is capped at the premium, but breakeven requires a move larger than the premium.
  • Option sellers win more often but face losses far larger than the premium collected.
  • SEBI studies found most individual F&O traders lose money; trade only what you can lose entirely.

What a derivative is, in one paragraph

A derivative is a contract whose value comes from something else, the underlying, such as the Nifty 50 index or a stock. Exchange-traded derivatives in India are standardised by NSE and BSE: each contract has a fixed lot size, an expiry date and settlement rules, and the clearing corporation stands between buyer and seller. Futures and options are the two main types. Both use a small amount of money to take exposure to a much larger value, which is what makes them powerful and risky.

Futures: an obligation, with daily settlement

A futures contract is an agreement to buy or sell the underlying at a fixed price on a future date. Both sides are obligated. In practice, almost everyone closes the position before expiry or lets it settle in cash (index futures) or by delivery (stock futures, which are physically settled in India).

For our example, assume the Nifty future is trading at 25,000 and the lot size is 75 units. Lot sizes are set and revised by the exchange, so treat 75 as illustrative and check NSE for the current figure. One lot then represents 25,000 × 75 = ₹18,75,000 of exposure. You do not pay this amount. You deposit an initial margin, a fraction of it, set by the clearing corporation's risk models. Say it is about ₹2.5 lakh for this example.

If you buy one lot at 25,000 and the future rises to 25,200, your profit is 200 × 75 = ₹15,000. If it falls to 24,800, your loss is ₹15,000. Futures are marked to market daily: profits are credited and losses debited from your account every evening, and if your balance falls below the required margin, you must add funds or your broker will close the position.

Why a 1% move feels like 7%

Look at the last column. A 200-point move is 0.8% on the index, but 6% on the margin you deposited. That ratio is leverage. It works both ways at the same speed, and that is the first thing every beginner underestimates. A futures trader who keeps only the minimum margin in the account can be forced out by an ordinary one-day move, even if the view turns out right a week later.

Options: a right for the buyer, an obligation for the seller

An option gives the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy (call) or sell (put) the underlying at a fixed strike price on expiry. The buyer pays a price called the premium to the seller (also called the writer). Index options in India are European style, so they can only be exercised at expiry, though they can be bought and sold any time before then.

Suppose you buy one lot of the Nifty 25,000 call at a premium of ₹150. Your cost is 150 × 75 = ₹11,250, and this is the most you can lose. The seller receives ₹11,250 but takes on an obligation; because their potential loss is open-ended, the seller has to deposit a much larger margin, similar to a futures position.

The call option P&L, rupee by rupee

At expiry, a call is worth the amount by which Nifty is above the strike, or zero. Your breakeven is strike plus premium: 25,000 + 150 = 25,150. Here is how the buyer and seller fare at different expiry levels, before charges.

What the table teaches

First, the buyer loses money in most of the rows. Nifty must rise by more than 150 points before expiry just for the buyer to break even. If it stays flat, rises a little, or falls, the premium decays to zero. This decay, called theta, works against the buyer every day.

Second, the seller wins in more scenarios but with capped profit (₹11,250 at most) and losses that grow with every point above 25,150. A sharp, unexpected rally of 600 points costs the seller ₹33,750, three times the maximum gain. Option sellers often describe their strategy as collecting small, steady income, but the occasional large loss can wipe out months of premium. This is not a 'safe' side; it is a different shape of risk.

Third, before expiry the premium also depends on implied volatility and time remaining. A buyer can be right about direction and still lose money if volatility collapses after an event such as a policy announcement, because the option's price falls even though the index moved favourably.

Puts, briefly

A put works in mirror image. Buying the 25,000 put at ₹140 costs 140 × 75 = ₹10,500, with breakeven at 24,860. The buyer gains if Nifty falls below that by expiry and loses at most the premium. Investors sometimes buy puts to hedge a stock portfolio against a fall, much like insurance, accepting the premium as a cost. The put seller collects ₹10,500 but faces large losses in a sharp fall.

Charges and taxes change the table

The P&L tables above are before costs. Every F&O trade attracts brokerage, securities transaction tax, exchange transaction charges, SEBI fees, stamp duty and GST. For options, STT is charged on the premium when you sell, and a higher STT can apply if an in-the-money option is exercised at expiry, so check the current rules before holding to expiry (as of 2026, see the exchange and income tax department websites).

Costs shift the breakeven. If your round-trip charges on the call trade come to a few hundred rupees, the buyer needs Nifty a few points above 25,150 to actually break even, and the seller's maximum gain falls below ₹11,250. On a single trade that seems trivial; across hundreds of trades a year it becomes one of the main reasons the typical trader's results are negative. F&O profits and losses are also generally taxed as business income rather than capital gains, which affects which return form you file and how losses can be set off.

Before you trade your first lot

Derivatives are legitimate tools for hedging and price discovery, and the NISM Equity Derivatives (Series VIII) certification covers them thoroughly for anyone working in the industry. For individuals speculating with them, the evidence is sobering: according to SEBI studies (check sebi.gov.in for the latest), a large majority of individual F&O traders, roughly nine in ten in the samples studied, lost money, and the losses were larger after including transaction costs. SEBI has since tightened rules on contract sizes, weekly expiries and upfront margin collection (as of 2026 — check SEBI circulars for current rules). Nothing here is investment advice or a recommendation to trade.

  • Calculate the full contract value before trading, not just the margin or premium.
  • Know your breakeven, including brokerage, STT and other charges.
  • Never sell options without understanding the worst-case loss in a gap move.
  • Trade only with money you can afford to lose entirely.

FAQs

What is the maximum loss when buying an option?

The maximum loss for an option buyer is the premium paid plus transaction charges. If you buy one lot of a call at ₹150 with a lot size of 75, you can lose at most ₹11,250 plus charges. However, this full loss happens often, because options that expire out of the money become worthless.

Why do option sellers need so much more margin than buyers?

The seller's potential loss is not capped. A sharp move against a short call or short put can cost many times the premium received. The clearing corporation therefore requires the seller to keep margins comparable to a futures position, so that losses can be covered even in volatile markets.

Are stock futures settled in cash in India?

No. Stock futures and stock options in India are physically settled, which means positions held at expiry result in actual delivery of shares and payment of the full value. Index derivatives, such as Nifty contracts, are cash-settled. Many traders close stock positions before expiry to avoid delivery obligations and the higher margins near expiry.
