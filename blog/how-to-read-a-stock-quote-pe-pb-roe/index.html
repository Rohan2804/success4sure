How to Read a Stock Quote, and What P/E, P/B and ROE Really Tell You

Open any stock page and you will see twenty numbers before you see a single sentence. Learning how to read a stock quote is mostly about knowing which numbers describe today's trading and which describe the business. We decode the screen, then calculate P/E, P/B and ROE for an imaginary company so you can see where each one can mislead.

By the Success4Sure editorial team · Updated 20 November 2026 · Editorial process

Key takeaways

  • Separate the trading half of a quote (price, volume, range) from the business half (EPS, P/E, P/B, ROE).
  • P/E = price ÷ EPS; check whether EPS includes one-off gains before trusting it.
  • P/B matters most for banks and NBFCs and least for asset-light businesses.
  • P/B = P/E × ROE, so differences in P/B at the same P/E usually come from ROE.
  • High ROE built on heavy debt is riskier than it looks; check debt-to-equity too.

The two halves of every quote page

A typical quote screen mixes two very different kinds of information. The first half is about trading: last traded price, change, open, high, low, previous close, volume, the bid and ask, and the 52-week range. These change every second and tell you what buyers and sellers are doing right now. The second half is about the business: market capitalisation, earnings per share, P/E, P/B, dividend yield and sometimes ROE. These are calculated from financial statements and change only when new results or a big price move arrive.

Keep the halves separate in your head. A stock up 4% today tells you about today's demand, not about whether the company is good. A low P/E tells you about valuation relative to past profit, not about what the price will do tomorrow.

Decoding the trading numbers

Here is what each field on the trading side means, using a hypothetical company we will call Sahyadri Foods Ltd.

  • Last traded price (LTP) ₹420: the price of the most recent trade, not necessarily the price you will get.
  • Change +₹8 (+1.94%): measured against the previous close of ₹412.
  • Open, high, low: the first trade of the day and the day's range so far.
  • Bid ₹419.80 / Ask ₹420.10: the best price buyers are offering and sellers are asking. The gap is the bid-ask spread; wide spreads signal thin trading.
  • Volume 3.2 lakh shares: how many shares changed hands today. Compare it with the average; a spike usually has a reason such as results or news.
  • 52-week high/low ₹510 / ₹330: the range over the past year. Being near the low is not automatically cheap, and being near the high is not automatically expensive.
  • Upper and lower price band or circuit: the maximum the stock may move in a day under exchange rules.

Market cap and EPS: the bridge to the business

Market capitalisation is the share price multiplied by the number of shares. Sahyadri has 5 crore shares, so at ₹420 its market cap is ₹2,100 crore. That is what the market is valuing the whole company's equity at today.

Earnings per share (EPS) is net profit divided by shares. Suppose Sahyadri earned ₹150 crore of net profit in the last twelve months. EPS = ₹150 crore ÷ 5 crore = ₹30. Quote pages usually show trailing twelve-month (TTM) EPS, which means the last four quarters added up. It is backward-looking by definition.

P/E worked out, and what it hides

Price-to-earnings is the share price divided by EPS: ₹420 ÷ ₹30 = 14. Read it as 'investors are paying ₹14 for every ₹1 of last year's profit'. Equivalently, market cap ÷ net profit = ₹2,100 crore ÷ ₹150 crore = 14. The same number either way.

P/E is useful for comparing a company with its own history or with close peers in the same industry. It is close to useless across industries: a bank, a software company and a cement maker have different growth, capital needs and accounting, so their 'normal' P/Es differ widely.

The biggest trap is one-off profit. If ₹50 crore of Sahyadri's ₹150 crore came from selling a piece of land, the recurring profit is ₹100 crore, EPS is ₹20 and the true P/E is 21, not 14. A low P/E built on an exceptional gain is a mirage. Always check the 'exceptional items' line in results. The reverse also happens: a company with a one-time loss can show a scarily high P/E.

A practical tip: many quote pages calculate P/E from consolidated earnings, while some use standalone earnings of the parent company only. For groups with large subsidiaries, these can differ a lot. Check which basis the page uses, and use the same basis when you compare two companies, or your comparison will be meaningless.

P/B worked out, and where it matters

Price-to-book compares the market price with the book value per share, which is shareholders' equity (share capital plus reserves) divided by shares. If Sahyadri's net worth on the balance sheet is ₹1,000 crore, book value per share = ₹1,000 crore ÷ 5 crore = ₹200, and P/B = ₹420 ÷ ₹200 = 2.1.

P/B is most meaningful for businesses whose assets are mostly financial and marked close to market value, which is why analysts lean on it for banks and NBFCs. For a consumer brand or a software firm, the real assets (brands, people, code) are barely on the balance sheet, so P/B tends to look high and says little. A P/B below 1 can signal undervaluation, or it can signal that the market doubts the book value, for example because of hidden bad loans.

ROE: the ratio that connects P/E and P/B

Return on equity is net profit divided by shareholders' equity: ₹150 crore ÷ ₹1,000 crore = 15%. It tells you how much profit the business generates on the money shareholders have left in it. Using average equity over the year is more accurate than year-end equity, and many data providers do this.

Here is the relationship worth bookmarking: P/B = P/E × ROE. For Sahyadri, 14 × 0.15 = 2.1, which matches. So a company with a high ROE can justify a higher P/B for the same P/E. When you see two companies with the same P/E but very different P/Bs, the difference is almost always ROE.

ROE has its own trap: debt. A company can boost ROE by borrowing heavily, because equity stays small while profit grows. Two companies with 20% ROE can have very different risk if one has almost no debt and the other has debt four times its equity. Look at the debt-to-equity ratio and return on capital employed (ROCE) alongside ROE.

A five-minute routine for any quote page

When you open a stock page, run through the same questions in the same order. First, what is the market cap, so you know whether this is a large, mid or small company. Second, is the P/E based on normal profit or distorted by one-offs. Third, how does P/E compare with the company's own five-year range and with two or three direct peers. Fourth, is ROE healthy, and is it driven by margins or by debt. Fifth, does the trading data show unusual volume or a stock pinned at its price band, which signals news you should read before anything else.

None of these ratios tells you whether to buy. They tell you what the price already assumes. A P/E of 40 assumes strong growth for many years; a P/E of 8 assumes the business is stagnant or risky. Your job as a learner is to ask whether those assumptions look reasonable. This post is for education only and is not investment advice.

FAQs

Is a low P/E stock always cheap?

No. A low P/E can mean the market expects profits to fall, the business is cyclical and currently at peak earnings, or recent profit includes one-time gains. Compare the P/E with the company's own history and close peers, and read the results to see whether earnings are recurring before drawing any conclusion about value.

What is the difference between trailing and forward P/E?

Trailing P/E uses actual earnings of the last twelve months, so it is based on reported numbers. Forward P/E uses analysts' estimates of next year's earnings, which may turn out wrong. Quote pages in India mostly show trailing P/E. Forward P/E is useful for fast-growing firms but is only as good as the estimate behind it.

Why do banks have low P/E and P/B compared with FMCG companies?

Banks carry large balance sheets funded mostly by deposits, face credit risk and need regular capital, so the market pays less per rupee of profit and book value. FMCG companies need little capital, earn high returns on equity and have steady demand, so investors pay higher multiples. Comparing valuation ratios across these sectors is therefore misleading.
